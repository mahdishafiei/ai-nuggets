First item, and today's evening lead. On biorxiv from Theo Rispens at Sanquin in Amsterdam, with Nick van der Hoeven as first author, and with several coauthors from Genmab — "F-C-R-L-five is a fucose-sensitive I-g-G-F-c receptor with binding properties distinct from classical Fc-gamma receptors." This paper is a clean companion to the morning episode's lead on C-D-sixteen-A allosteric regulation, and it belongs in the same mental bucket: the Fc receptor side of the antibody-effector interaction is not the closed book that Fc-engineering practice sometimes treats it as, and there are engineerable levers on receptors the field has been ignoring.

Some background on why F-C-R-L-five matters. F-C-R-L-five is Fc-receptor-like-five, a low-affinity I-g-G-binding receptor expressed on B cells and, notably for oncology, on multiple myeloma cells. It shows up on immuno-oncology radar because bispecifics and antibody-drug conjugates targeting F-C-R-L-five as a tumor antigen are in the clinic. But it has also been implicated as a regulator of B-cell responses, and prior literature on the F-C-R-L-five-I-g-G interaction has been all over the map — reported affinities across studies vary widely, subclass preferences differ between reports, and the molecular requirements for binding have never been pinned down cleanly. That inconsistency has made it hard to know whether Fc-engineered therapeutics — silencing mutations like L-A-L-A or L-A-L-A-P-G, or A-D-C-C-enhancing changes like the S-two-thirty-nine-D, I-three-thirty-two-E pair, or afucosylation — actually engage or bypass F-C-R-L-five. That question matters for a myeloma-targeting bispecific whose payload cell is a B cell that itself expresses F-C-R-L-five.

The paper does the biochemistry properly. The measured affinity of F-C-R-L-five for I-g-G is very low, in the sixty-to-eighty micromolar range — squarely in low-affinity Fc-receptor territory but distinct from any of the classical Fc-gamma receptors. Surface plasmon resonance across the I-g-G subclasses shows a preference for I-g-G-one and I-g-G-four over the other subclasses. Structurally, F-C-R-L-five has multiple extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains, and the binding is driven primarily by the two N-terminal domains, with a contribution from the third domain needed for maximal binding. Critically, the essential residues on the I-g-G Fc-tail are different from those that classical Fc-gamma receptors care about. That last part is where the therapeutic hook enters.

The engineering result. When the authors screened a panel of I-g-G antibodies carrying defined Fc-mutation sets, most of the standard Fc-engineering strategies — the silencing mutations and the affinity-enhancing mutations — did not perturb F-C-R-L-five binding. That is not obvious a priori; those mutations disrupt classical Fc-gamma-receptor engagement, and if the F-C-R-L-five interface shared residues, you would expect crosstalk. The paper's screen identifies E-two-ninety-three as a key F-C-R-L-five binding determinant, and shows that the point mutation E-two-ninety-three-arginine — E to R at position two-ninety-three — selectively abrogates F-C-R-L-five binding while preserving binding to every classical Fc-gamma receptor tested. That is a specific, single-residue handle. If you are building a therapeutic that engages a Fc-gamma-receptor-dependent effector pathway — A-D-C-C, phagocytosis, complement activation via C-one-Q, all of which the Fc-gamma-receptor axis feeds — and you want to make sure it does not simultaneously engage F-C-R-L-five on the tumor B-cell side or on bystander B cells, E-two-ninety-three-arginine is the tool for that experiment.

The other finding worth flagging, and this is where the title language comes in. F-C-R-L-five has a considerable preference for binding afucosylated I-g-G. Afucosylation is a well-known enhancer of Fc-gamma-receptor-three-A engagement — it is the entire premise behind Kyowa Kirin's Potelligent platform and Roche's glyco-engineered antibodies for oncology. What this paper adds is that the fucose sensitivity is not exclusive to Fc-gamma-receptor-three-A. F-C-R-L-five reads the same glycan signature and responds similarly. For an engineer designing an afucosylated therapeutic for A-D-C-C, the takeaway is that F-C-R-L-five engagement rides along whether you wanted it to or not, and if that receptor is expressed on the target cell or on bystander B cells, that changes what your therapeutic actually does. Two engineering handles then, both cleanly stated: E-two-ninety-three-arginine as a selective F-C-R-L-five knockout, and awareness that afucosylation is not a Fc-gamma-receptor-three-A-only lever. This paper is a preprint and three of the authors are Genmab employees, so read the conclusions with the industrial interest declared, but the biochemistry is what biochemistry looks like and the E-two-ninety-three-arginine claim is a concrete, testable design mutation.

Second item. Also on biorxiv, posted July seventh from Fabian Meyer's group at Novartis Pharma in Basel with an Ivano Eberini coauthor — "ThermoFusion: a multimodal deep-learning framework for generalizable prediction of enzyme thermostability." First author Yu Wei. The task is single-point-mutation delta-delta-G prediction, the same class of problem the bonus episode's Genedata paper addressed. But the methodological argument is directly opposite, and the point of comparison is worth spelling out.

Recall from the bonus episode: the Genedata group argued a well-tuned sequence-only E-S-M-two model beats structure-informed methods on their delta-T-m benchmark, and reframed the sequence-only-versus-structure-informed debate as a matter of under-tuned training recipes rather than any fundamental information gap. ThermoFusion argues the other direction. The setup is a hybrid model that takes two input streams — three-D protein structure embeddings from ThermoMPNN, which is a stability-focused variant of ProteinMPNN, and sequence-based embeddings from the pretrained protein language model E-S-M-two — and fuses them to predict single-point delta-delta-G. Benchmarking is on a curated enzyme dataset of one-hundred-and-five enzymes and three-thousand-one-hundred-forty-four mutations.

The headline result is not that ThermoFusion beats the state of the art on the in-distribution average — that would be a modest incremental claim. The headline is out-of-distribution generalization. Where prior methods, including the ThermoMPNN half of ThermoFusion's own inputs and other state-of-the-art tools, fall off a cliff on sequences with low identity to the training set, ThermoFusion holds up. The authors' framing is that many of the leaderboard-topping delta-delta-G models are more memorization than generalization — they know the mutation landscape of well-represented protein families and lose predictive power when you move to a family they have not seen. The multimodal fusion, in the ThermoFusion argument, is what gives you a model that stabilizes on out-of-distribution enzymes: neither stream alone generalizes, but the combination does.

The take-home. If both of these papers survive peer review, the field ends up with a nuanced picture rather than a resolved one. Genedata's argument stands where it stands: for the specific task of delta-T-m prediction on antibody-adjacent sequences that resemble the training distribution, a well-tuned sequence-only pipeline is competitive with structure-informed methods, and the design of the training recipe is the load-bearing choice. ThermoFusion's argument stands where the distribution shift is real: when you are designing far from training data — a novel scaffold, an unusual family, an engineered protein — the structural information stream matters, because sequence-only models degrade faster on the tail. For antibody engineering the practical read is that developability triage on humanized variants of natural antibody frameworks may not need structure, but delta-delta-G prediction for a distant designed sequence probably does. I read this from the abstract, since the full text has not rendered on biorxiv yet — but the framing is clean and the enzyme benchmark specifics are stated tightly enough to act on.

Third item, and the one that ties several of the field's threads together. Also on biorxiv, posted July seventh from Derek Woolfson's group at the University of Bristol, with the Itzhaki lab at Cambridge, Wilson at Leeds and Bristol, Huang at Beatson-Glasgow, and Edwards at Leeds — "De novo designed bifunctional proteins for targeted protein degradation." First author Bram Mylemans. This is a full end-to-end demonstration of a de-novo-designed protein-based P-R-O-T-A-C, and it is worth explaining because the connection to antibody engineering is not that they built an antibody, but that the design methodology — hot-spot grafting onto a de-novo scaffold, ProteinMPNN sequence optimization, AlphaFold-two confidence-metric selection, negative-selection filtering — is exactly the toolkit modern antibody-and-nanobody designers are learning to use.

The setup. Targeted protein degradation is the therapeutic modality where you route a disease-causing protein to the proteasome by simultaneously binding it and an E-three ubiquitin ligase, forming a ternary complex that gets ubiquitinated and cleared. Small-molecule P-R-O-T-A-Cs have made real clinical progress, but they inherit two structural limitations: designing them is hard because you are chasing a ternary complex whose geometry, linker length, and lysine positioning all matter simultaneously; and the field has mostly relied on just two E-three ligases — Cereblon and von-Hippel-Lindau — because the small-molecule ligands for other E-threes are limited. Peptide-based P-R-O-T-A-Cs can access more E-threes through short linear motifs, called SLiMs — S-L-i-M-s, spelled out — but the SLiMs bind weakly, and controlling geometry with a floppy peptide is difficult. The proposal here is to sidestep both problems by using a structured de-novo protein as the bifunctional agent: rigid enough to enforce ternary-complex geometry, and modular enough to graft in different target binders and different E-three-recruiting SLiMs.

The pipeline. First, the scaffold. The authors design a small, sixty-seven-residue helix-loop-helix miniprotein called sc-a-p-C-C-two, derived from a previously published antiparallel heterodimeric coiled coil, and made single-chain by finding a suitable loop with the MASTER structure-search tool and refining it with Rosetta. A one-point-five-angstrom X-ray crystal structure confirms the design matches the model with a zero-point-three-six-angstrom root-mean-square deviation, and the scaffold is highly thermostable. That is the display module: two solvent-exposed helical surfaces you can decorate with binding hot-spots, plus a loop you can replace with a SLiM.

Second, the target binder. The chosen protein of interest is B-C-L-x-L, an anti-apoptotic member of the B-C-L-two family and a well-studied cancer target — a small-molecule P-R-O-T-A-C against it, D-T-two-two-one-six, is in clinical trials. B-C-L-x-L is inhibited by natural helical effector peptides binding into a well-characterized cleft, so the authors take six hot-spot positions from natural binders, graft eighteen sequence motifs onto three locations on the scaffold surface, run ProteinMPNN with a RosettaRelax cycle to embellish the interface, and evaluate each design with AlphaFold-two against the target. The best B-C-L-x-L binder — sc-a-p-C-C-two-B-C-L-x-L-three — reaches a three-nanomolar dissociation constant by isothermal titration calorimetry, with a co-crystal structure at three-angstrom resolution that confirms the predicted side-chain conformations. That is a genuine tight binder from a de-novo scaffold, produced by the standard modern design pipeline.

Third, and this is where the results turn interesting for anyone tracking design metrics: naive scaffold-based binders picked up dual specificity for B-C-L-x-L and its close family member M-C-L-one, which is a common failure mode when target and off-target share the same peptide-binding cleft geometry. The authors add a computational negative-selection step, running AlphaFold-two on complexes of every candidate with both proteins and ranking by shape complementarity for the intended target minus the off-target. This yields sc-a-p-C-C-two-B-C-L-x-L-seventeen, which retains high B-C-L-x-L affinity and binds M-C-L-one more than a thousand-fold more weakly. Two things are worth noting. First, that thousand-fold selectivity jump comes not from a new binder design but from a re-ranking of already-computed candidates using AlphaFold structural metrics — this is a cheap, general trick. Second, when the authors tested other confidence metrics — p-L-D-D-T, p-A-E, and combinations — only shape complementarity gave the selectivity gain. p-L-D-D-T and p-A-E did not. That is a specific, negative result about which AlphaFold metrics actually reflect binding differences, and it echoes what recent large-scale de-novo binder studies have been finding: standard confidence scores do not correlate well with measured binding affinity, and shape complementarity is doing more work than the field's default ranking scheme reflects.

Fourth, the E-three recruiter. The authors chose K-L-H-L-twenty as the E-three ligase, and a five-residue SLiM that adopts a turn-like conformation in the K-L-H-L-twenty binding groove. They graft the SLiM into the loop of the scaffold, using AlphaFold-two to check the geometry and ProteinMPNN to optimize the surrounding residues. The resulting K-L-H-L-twenty binders have low-micromolar affinity — three-to-four micromolar I-C-fifty in the fluorescence-anisotropy competition assay — which is in line with prior reports for this SLiM. Not tight by protein-binder standards, but tight enough to function as a proximity handle inside a bifunctional design where the target binder does the heavy lifting on affinity.

Fifth, and the payload of the paper: combining the two modules into a bifunctional protein. The bifunctional design carries the B-C-L-x-L binder on one helical surface and the K-L-H-L-twenty-recruiting SLiM in the loop. In a lung-cancer cell line — A-five-four-nine — the bifunctional construct degrades endogenous B-C-L-x-L to a similar extent as the clinical small-molecule P-R-O-T-A-C, D-T-two-two-one-six. Adding the proteasome inhibitor carfilzomib blocks the degradation, confirming a proteasome-dependent mechanism. Downstream apoptosis, measured by Annexin-V and D-A-P-I flow cytometry, tracks the degradation. Neither of the monofunctional halves — the B-C-L-x-L binder alone, or the K-L-H-L-twenty SLiM alone — is a degrader. Both are needed.

Why this matters for the antibody-engineering audience. Three reasons. First, the design pipeline itself — hot-spot graft, ProteinMPNN embellishment, AlphaFold-two confidence-based selection, negative-selection filtering — is the same pipeline being used and refined for de-novo binders, including for antibody-alternative binders like miniproteins directed at viral antigens or receptors. Every methodological refinement here transfers to a de-novo nanobody-alternative program. Second, the paper's honest reporting that confidence metrics do not correlate well with measured affinity is a running theme this year, and it means the ranking step is a design bottleneck that any de-novo-binder effort should assume the field has not yet solved. Third, the bifunctional scaffold approach shows that once you have reliable single-target binders, combining them into higher-order functionalities — bispecifics, biparatopics, degraders — is a matter of scaffold geometry rather than a fundamentally new problem. That is a template that the antibody-and-nanobody design community has been converging on from the other direction.
