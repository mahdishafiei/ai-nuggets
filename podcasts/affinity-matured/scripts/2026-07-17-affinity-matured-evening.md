First item. PeptideCLM-2, from Aaron Feller, Maxim Secor, Sebastian Swanson, Claus Wilke, and Kristine Deibler at Novo Nordisk Molecular AI in Lexington, Massachusetts and the Wilke lab at U-T Austin. The paper is titled "Scaling SMILES-Based Chemical Language Models for Therapeutic Peptide Engineering." The bioRxiv preprint has been up since January, but the peer-reviewed version indexed on PubMed this week is what got it back onto the radar. Regular listeners will recognize the context — this morning's episode covered PeptiVerse from the Chatterjee lab at Duke, which uses the earlier PeptideCLM-1 at twenty-three million parameters as its SMILES-branch backbone. This is a scaling study on that same family from the group that trained PeptideCLM in the first place.

The motivation. Therapeutic peptides sit in a chemical gap that neither protein language models nor small-molecule chemical language models cover well. Protein language models — E-S-M and family — accept canonical amino acid sequences and choke on noncanonical residues, macrocycles, backbone modifications, or the kind of D-amino acids and unnatural side chains that appear in almost every clinically developed peptide drug. Small-molecule chemical language models like MoLFormer accept S-M-I-L-E-S but were trained on drug-like molecules that are one to two orders of magnitude smaller than a typical peptide by atom count, and they inherit tokenizers and context lengths that don't handle a hundred-atom polymer gracefully.

PeptideCLM-2 is a targeted scale-up along all three axes — training corpus, model size, and objective. The corpus is one hundred eighteen million molecules, drawn from three sources balanced during training to prevent the small-molecule tail from swamping the peptide signal. About one hundred nine million small drug-like molecules from PubChem after quality filtering. About nine point six million peptide sequences from E-S-M Atlas filtered for confident structure prediction, meaning p-T-M above zero point seven and p-L-D-D-T above zero point seven and length at most one hundred amino acids. About forty-nine thousand lipid structures from LIPID MAPS. Each pretraining epoch upsamples the lipid set to two hundred fifty thousand, uses the full peptide corpus at around ten million, and downsamples the small-molecule set to ten million. So the effective epoch is a roughly balanced mixture that keeps the model attending to peptide chemistry.

The tokenizer is a custom k-mer scheme with maximum k-mer length of six, yielding four hundred five tokens total — one hundred sixty single-atom tokens plus two hundred forty-five multi-character k-mers. For a natural peptide, this reduces sequence length by sixty-four percent relative to atom-level S-M-I-L-E-S encoding. That length reduction is significant in an attention-quadratic transformer: a peptide that costs a hundred K tokens at atom-level costs closer to thirty-five K here, so the transformer stays computationally sane on the polymer regime.

The architecture. BERT-style encoders with modern upgrades — rotary positional embeddings, SwiGLU activations, pre-layer normalization, sixty-four dimensions per attention head. Three scale variants. Small at thirty-two million parameters — six layers, hidden dimension three hundred eighty-four, six attention heads. Base at one hundred fourteen million parameters — twelve layers, hidden dim seven hundred sixty-eight, twelve heads. Large at three hundred thirty-seven million parameters — twenty-four layers, hidden dim one thousand twenty-four, sixteen heads. Three pretraining objectives — masked language modeling with span masking at twenty-five percent rate, multi-task regression against ninety-nine R-D-Kit physicochemical descriptors, and a hybrid combining both with masking weighted at zero point six and regression at zero point four. That gives three sizes times three objectives — nine trained models, all released.

Benchmarks against prior baselines. Cyclic peptide membrane permeability on the CycPeptMPDB set — best A-U-R-O-C zero point eight three six from the hybrid objective, versus PeptideCLM-1 at zero point seven eight one, MoLFormer-XL numbers below that, and Morgan fingerprints at zero point seven nine two. Tumor-homing peptide classification on THPep — best Matthews correlation coefficient zero point seven five six from the masked-language-modeling-only objective, versus THPep's own published number of zero point seven one zero. Cell penetration on CellPPD — Matthews correlation zero point eight seven five. Antimicrobial peptide classification on AmpHGT — Matthews correlation zero point eight eight four versus AmpHGT's own zero point seven nine seven, and here they note out-of-distribution generalization matters: their held-out set contains noncanonical residues that AmpHGT's training data lacked. Blood plasma stability on PepMSND — best Matthews correlation zero point seven four nine from the multi-task-regression-only objective, versus the multimodal PepMSND published number of zero point seven two six.

Two scaling observations worth flagging. First — for aggregation prediction on a thioflavin-T fibrillation assay, A-U-R-O-C scales cleanly with model size, from zero point six nine four at thirty-two million to zero point seven five one at one hundred fourteen million to zero point eight two three at three hundred thirty-seven million, versus Morgan fingerprints at zero point five seven nine. Aggregation is famously hard for descriptor-based models because the driving physics are subtle backbone conformational effects and side-chain interactions that aren't captured by additive descriptor sums. That the transformer picks these up and does so monotonically with scale is a real result. Second — as scale increases, the choice of pretraining objective matters less. At thirty-two million parameters, hybrid objective beats masked-language-modeling-only by a meaningful margin; at one hundred fourteen and three hundred thirty-seven million, that gap closes. Self-supervised masking is enough at scale, matching what the general-purpose protein-language-model scaling literature has been saying.

They also flag two honest caveats. First, downstream adaptation matters — frozen embeddings out of the model give an R-squared below zero point three on CycPeptMPDB regardless of size, so linear probes are not the right way to use these models. Second, benchmark overlap. They compute train-benchmark data overlap and report low leakage on most benchmarks, below three point two percent, but higher overlap on CycPeptMPDB at eight percent and PepMSND at twenty-eight percent — noting the numbers on those benchmarks should be read with an appropriate skepticism. Code is at github dot com slash Aaron Feller slash PeptideCLM-2, weights on Hugging Face under aaronfeller, pretraining data on Zenodo. License is Creative Commons Attribution four, permits commercial reuse.

For the audience — if you have a peptide-adjacent antibody format on your bench, whether it's a peptide-drug conjugate, a macrocyclic-peptide antibody alternative, or a bispecific with a peptide linker, PeptideCLM-2 gives you a stronger developability feature extractor than PeptideCLM-1 on almost every property, at three well-marked scales, with data leakage accounted for. Plug it in behind PeptiVerse's SMILES branch and you have a materially better front end.

Second item. From Dominik Schwarz, Sven Giese, Franziska Siegel, and Atanas Kamburov at Bayer's Machine Learning Research group in Berlin with collaborators at the Broad Institute — Akansha Gupta, Andrew Cherniack, Xiaoping Yang, David Root, Gizem Karsli-Uzunbas, and Heidi Greulich. Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling, indexed on PubMed this week. "Protein Language Model-Based Fitness Estimates Facilitate Resistance Mutation Identification."

The problem is a familiar one from cancer drug development. On-target resistance mutations — amino acid substitutions in the drug-target protein that reduce inhibitor binding while preserving enough of the protein's native function to keep the tumor alive — are the failure mode that terminates most kinase inhibitor programs. If you could identify likely resistance mutations before the drug enters humans, you could either prioritize a compound whose resistance profile is cleaner, or you could start work on a follow-up inhibitor covering the anticipated escape mutations in parallel with the primary program.

The workhorse computational method for on-target resistance prediction has been free-energy perturbation — a physics-based estimate of how much a single amino acid substitution shifts the binding free energy for a bound inhibitor. F-E-P gives you a prioritized list of mutations that should decrease inhibitor affinity and therefore should confer resistance. In practice this list is dominated by false positives. The reason is that many of the mutations F-E-P flags as affinity-decreasing also destroy the protein's own function — the mutant protein doesn't fold, doesn't localize, or doesn't catalyze — so even if it would escape the drug, it can't be a resistance driver because a cell carrying it is not viable. Filtering that failure mode out with a physics stack is expensive — you'd need to model each mutant's stability and its impact on native activity, both of which are error-prone and computationally heavy.

The paper's proposal is to use a protein language model fitness estimate as the filter. The intuition is standard by now in the protein-language-model literature — a masked-language-model over natural protein sequences assigns higher log-likelihoods to substitutions that appear in the natural sequence space and lower log-likelihoods to substitutions that don't. For a well-conserved residue, that likelihood ratio is a decent proxy for whether a mutation is compatible with functional protein, because natural selection has already run the experiment of which substitutions survive.

The validation setup uses two deep mutational scanning datasets covering resistance to a known inhibitor with a known binding mode. One is a public D-M-S from the Broad on E-R-K-2 tested against the inhibitor S-C-H seven seven two nine eight four. The other is an internal Bayer D-M-S on an E-G-F-R exon-twenty mutant tested against a Bayer small-molecule inhibitor. For each dataset they know which single amino acid mutations really do confer resistance in the assay — those are the true positives — and which mutations reduce inhibitor binding in F-E-P but don't confer resistance in the experiment, because the mutant protein is either non-functional or otherwise not a resistance driver — those are the false positives F-E-P is generating.

The main finding is that adding a protein language model fitness filter on top of F-E-P reclassifies many of the F-E-P false positives correctly. Mutations that F-E-P flagged as affinity-decreasing but that also carry low language-model fitness — the P-L-M assigns them a low likelihood, meaning natural evolution has essentially never picked them — get filtered out, and those are exactly the mutations that D-M-S shows are not resistance drivers. Mutations that F-E-P flags as affinity-decreasing and that also carry high language-model fitness — the P-L-M sees them as tolerated — are retained, and those match the D-M-S resistance calls. In effect, the P-L-M is doing the "is this a viable protein" check without you having to run a stability calculation or a functional simulation.

There are two caveats to take seriously. First, this is on-target resistance only — off-target compensation or altered pharmacokinetics through a completely different protein is out of scope. Second, the D-M-S ground truth is per-site single-mutation only, so combinatorial escape isn't tested here. But as a way to make the F-E-P shortlist tractable for a drug development team, it's a clean methodological upgrade.

The reason to flag this on an antibody-focused show. Antibody escape is structurally the same problem in reverse. When you develop a broadly neutralizing antibody against a variable target — H-I-V envelope, influenza hemagglutinin head, SARS-CoV-2 spike, an oncogenic driver mutation — you want to know which antigen mutations escape your antibody without destroying antigen function. The right computational stack for that question is a per-mutation binding calculation like F-E-P on the antibody-antigen interface, filtered by a fitness estimate on the antigen. Which is exactly the same pipeline this paper describes, run against the antigen rather than the drug target. A group doing broadly neutralizing antibody design who wants a computationally efficient way to anticipate escape before a passaging experiment tells them can borrow the recipe directly. Whether the protein language model choice — the paper leaves the specific model somewhat under-specified in the abstract — should be a general P-L-M like E-S-M or an antigen-specific fitness model conditioned on the actual variant landscape of that pathogen is an open question the paper is set up to answer for antibody teams that adopt it.

Third item. A bioRxiv preprint posted today from Océane Marescal and Iain Cheeseman at the Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research and the M-I-T Department of Biology — the Cheeseman lab, best known for kinetochore and mitotic cell biology, using a machine learning model to answer a question that sits between cell biology and protein engineering. The title is "A machine learning model predicts protein stability of annotated and alternate protein isoforms." The body of the preprint is out — I have the full P-D-F — so this is not an abstract-only summary.

The starting point is a published large-scale experimental analysis by Timms and colleagues that quantified how the N-terminal amino acid sequence of a protein affects its stability inside human cells. The Global Protein Stability system fuses a candidate N-terminal peptide sequence to the front of G-F-P and reads out steady-state G-F-P fluorescence, which turns protein stability into a flow-cytometry number. The Timms dataset covers about fifty thousand peptide sequences — the first twenty-three amino acids of the primary isoform of every human protein — each assigned a Protein Stability Index score from one, most unstable, to six, most stable.

The Cheeseman model. A multi-layer perceptron regressor trained on the fifty-thousand-sequence dataset, one-hot encoding of the twenty-three-residue peptide, random eighty-twenty train-test split. It hits R-squared of zero point nine seven on training data and zero point seven two on test data. Given that the input is just sequence and the label is a continuous experimental score, this is a strong fit — the amino acid identity across the first twenty-three positions is doing most of the work explaining stability variance.

They probe the model by computational permutation. Take the five hundred most stable peptides in the dataset, generate every single-amino-acid substitution, re-run the model, count how often each position accounts for significantly destabilized permutations. Position one dominates — ninety-five percent of significantly destabilized permutations come from a substitution at the very first residue. Ranked destabilizing substitutions at position one: tyrosine, lysine, arginine, tryptophan, aspartate, phenylalanine, histidine, asparagine, leucine, glutamine, glutamate, isoleucine. Which recovers, from a data-driven readout, the exact catalog of destabilizing amino acids the N-end rule literature has established over decades — primary type-one degrons like arginine, lysine, histidine; primary type-two degrons like leucine, phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan, isoleucine; secondary degrons aspartate and glutamate; tertiary degrons asparagine and glutamine. The stabilizing substitutions at position one are the small-side-chain residues serine, alanine, methionine, threonine, proline, glycine, valine — again matching the biological literature.

Now the interesting part. They run the opposite permutation — take the five hundred most unstable sequences and see what single substitutions stabilize them. And they find that changes at positions three through twenty-three account for thirty-seven percent of significantly stabilized permutations. So the first two amino acids are not the whole story. They test this directly by training a second model on just the first two residues of each peptide. That truncated model hits R-squared of zero point three seven on training and zero point three six on test — dramatically worse than the twenty-three-residue model. First two residues alone are informative but not sufficient. There is real signal in positions three through twenty-three.

The specific new finding they surface with a heat map of position-by-amino-acid stabilization frequency is that substituting arginine or lysine at positions roughly four through thirteen — deeper in the sequence than the classical N-end rule — stabilizes proteins substantially. They chase this down mechanistically with a series of reporter constructs, starting from the two most unstable N-termini in the Timms dataset, ADAM10 with experimental P-S-I of one point four nine and Z-N-F two zero eight at one point four one. They generate variants with progressively more arginines inserted at positions six through eleven and measure real stability via the same G-F-P readout. Model prediction and experimental result agree — increasing arginine content stabilizes the reporter monotonically.

Then they ask whether this is loss-of-hydrophobic-residue or gain-of-arginine. For ADAM10, they mutate the hydrophobic V-L-I stretch at positions five through seven to aspartate, alanine, threonine, or arginine. All four substitutions stabilize, so removing hydrophobic residues does help. But the arginine version stabilizes more than the aspartate, alanine, or threonine versions — so there is an arginine-specific effect on top of the hydrophobic-removal effect. That is a genuinely new sequence rule falling out of a small interpretable model applied to a well-designed experimental dataset.

The final application uses the model to predict the stability of alternative N-terminal isoforms. Recent translation-initiation-site profiling has catalogued thousands of proteins where the same m-R-N-A is translated from multiple in-frame start codons — some upstream of the annotated start, generating N-terminal extensions; some downstream, generating N-terminal truncations. Both classes produce protein isoforms with distinct N-termini from the annotated protein, and until this paper nobody had a good way to predict which of those isoforms would be stable relative to their annotated counterpart. The model, applied across the catalog, identifies isoform pairs where the extension or truncation of just a few residues predicts a dramatically different stability. This is a candidate list for follow-up experiments and, for the audience, a reminder that N-terminal composition can be tuned intentionally in a therapeutic protein design — an antibody or an antibody fusion with a stability-sensitive N-terminus is a use case this model can score.

Stepping back. The theme tonight is not one paper's specific result but a shared pattern — three sequence-based machine learning contributions, each targeting a different developer pain point. PeptideCLM-2 replaces a weaker peptide-chemistry feature extractor with a systematically better one at three scales, with objective ablations, code, and weights. The Bayer-Broad paper repurposes protein language model fitness as a cheap filter that fixes a specific failure mode of free-energy perturbation for resistance prediction, and the same recipe transfers to antibody escape. The Cheeseman lab paper shows that a modest fifty-thousand-peptide dataset plus an interpretable model can rediscover the N-end rule from scratch, extend it with a new arginine effect, and give you a catalog of alternative-start-site isoforms whose stability differs materially from their annotated versions. Different problems, same underlying pattern — take the domain's best experimental readout, train a sensibly-sized sequence model, use the model to nominate candidates the experiment couldn't have found by itself.
