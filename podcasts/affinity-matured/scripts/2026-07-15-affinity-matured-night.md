## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured. This is the evening bonus edition for July fifteenth, twenty twenty-six. Three items tonight, and they fit together in a way today's earlier episodes did not. First, the first-in-human Phase one readout of an AI-designed antibody from Generate Biomedicines, targeting the conserved stem helix of the SARS-CoV-2 spike. Second, a paper in Nature Computational Science that pushes back on the assumption that bigger protein language models are always better for fitness prediction. And third, a careful benchmarking, from the Koohy and Deane groups at Oxford, of what AlphaFold 3 can and cannot do when it is asked to predict T cell antigen specificity. Let us begin.

The first item is the Generate Biomedicines paper in the Journal of Infectious Diseases, describing the Phase one trial of a molecule called G-B dash zero-six-six-nine. This is important for one specific reason. Generate is one of the highest-profile AI antibody-design companies of the last few years, and their platform, which they have described as a molecular programming system, has been building toward clinical readouts for a while. This paper is, in their own language, the first-in-human trial of a first-in-class AI-designed monoclonal antibody. So the question the community has been asking, which is whether a fully computationally optimized antibody can actually clear the first hurdle of human safety and pharmacokinetics without behaving like a laboratory artifact, now has at least a partial answer.

Here is what the molecule is. G-B dash zero-six-six-nine is a half-life-extended monoclonal antibody targeting the stem helix of the S2 subunit of the SARS-CoV-2 spike. That target choice matters. The receptor-binding domain, which was the epitope for essentially every clinical monoclonal antibody through the first three years of the pandemic, has been under enormous selection pressure and burned through one antibody after another as variants swept in. The stem helix in S2 is much more conserved, in part because it experiences less selection from natural infection or vaccination. It is a harder epitope to reach, and it has been the subject of a lot of academic antibody engineering work, but as a commercial product, this is the first monoclonal antibody built there using a computational design pipeline. Generate does not, in this abstract, disclose which parts of the molecule are AI-designed and which come from a natural template, but the framing is that the antibody is AI-optimized, meaning at minimum the sequence has been through their generative and property-prediction stack rather than emerging from a straightforward donor B cell.

The trial itself is a standard first-in-human dose escalation. Fifty-one healthy adults between eighteen and fifty-five received single intravenous doses across five ascending cohorts, from one hundred milligrams up to two thousand four hundred milligrams, or placebo. Follow-up ran for forty-three weeks. The findings are what a Phase one is supposed to deliver. No dose-limiting toxicities. All adverse events were mild, grade one or two. Pharmacokinetics were dose-proportional up to the top dose. And the half-life came in at fifty-four days, which is the number you get from proper F-c engineering for extended in vivo persistence, roughly on par with what you see in half-life-extended anti-viral monoclonals like nirsevimab. Serum live-virus neutralization rose in a dose-dependent way, separating from placebo at the six hundred and twelve hundred milligram cohorts. The authors define a neutralizing index that adjusts serum concentrations for the in vitro potency of the antibody, and by that index, therapeutic coverage extends for about two weeks after a single dose at the higher levels. They also show in vitro that G-B zero-six-six-nine plays well with the small-molecule antivirals — remdesivir, nirmatrelvir, molnupiravir — in combination. The proposed next step is a Phase two of the twelve hundred milligram dose in immunocompromised patients, where a durable, variant-agnostic neutralizer would be genuinely useful.

Now, what should you take away from this. The trial is small, single-dose, healthy volunteers. It does not establish clinical efficacy. It also does not, on its own, prove that AI-designed antibodies behave better in humans than conventional ones — the baseline for a well-executed first-in-human monoclonal antibody trial is, in fact, uneventful safety and clean pharmacokinetics. What it does do is remove one specific risk from the AI-antibody-design story. There has been a persistent worry that computationally optimized sequences might carry hidden liabilities — off-target polyreactivity, immunogenicity, or the kind of pharmacokinetic weirdness that only shows up in vivo. G-B zero-six-six-nine, so far, does not. It behaves like an ordinary well-engineered antibody, with a clean profile and a half-life consistent with proper F-c engineering. For a working antibody engineer, the interesting question is not whether AI can design something that binds — that has been settled — but whether it can design something that survives development. This is one data point that says: at least sometimes, yes.

The second item shifts from the clinic to methodology. It is a paper by Hou and colleagues in the Shen group at Columbia, published this week in Nature Computational Science, with an accompanying News and Views by Tong Wang. The paper is titled "Understanding language model scaling for protein fitness prediction," and the preprint has been maturing on bioRxiv since April of twenty twenty-five. The reason it lands with force in the peer-reviewed version is that its central claim contradicts the working intuition of much of the field. In natural language, scaling laws for large models are, roughly speaking, monotonic. Bigger model, more data, better performance across essentially any downstream task. For protein language models, if you look specifically at zero-shot fitness prediction — that is, using the log-likelihood of a sequence under a pre-trained model to score how good a mutation is — the pattern is not monotonic. Beyond a certain model size, performance degrades. This is not a small, easily-explained effect. Hou and colleagues make the case that it is a structural feature of how these models represent proteins.

Here is the intuition. Self-supervised protein language models — the E-S-M 2 family, M-S-A dash Transformer, E-V-E, inverse-folding models like E-S-M dash I-F 1, and hybrid models like E-S-M 3 — all learn a distribution over natural protein sequences. The likelihood a model assigns to a sequence, which the paper calls p of sequence, tracks the evolutionary support for that sequence. To score a point mutation, you compare p of mutant to p of wildtype, and the assumption is that the ratio reflects the fitness cost. That assumption breaks in a specific regime. When p of wildtype is at a moderate level, the model has actually learned something informative about the local sequence landscape and can distinguish deleterious mutations from tolerated ones. When p of wildtype is very high — the model is essentially memorizing this sequence and its close relatives — the model tends to predict uniformly high likelihoods across essentially all substitutions, because none of them look particularly implausible relative to the memorized reference. And when p of wildtype is very low — a sequence the model finds anomalous — it predicts uniformly low likelihoods, because everything looks wrong. In either extreme, the fitness predictions collapse into noise.

Analyzing across one hundred fifty-four experiments in ProteinGym, which is now the canonical benchmark for fitness prediction, the authors show that this moderate-p regime is where the models earn their Spearman correlations. And the twist that ties it all back to scaling is this: larger models tend to push p of wildtype higher, because larger models are simply more confident about what a real protein looks like. So a model that is too big will, for many proteins, sit above the moderate-p sweet spot and lose fitness-prediction performance, even though it is objectively a better generative model of natural proteins. This is a real tension. The property that makes a model a better prior over sequence space — high, confident likelihoods on the training distribution — is not the property that makes it a good fitness predictor, which requires calibrated relative likelihoods in a narrow band around the wildtype.

The practical guidance the paper offers is concrete. Do not just pick the largest available model. For a given protein, look at where p of wildtype falls, and prefer a model whose predicted wildtype likelihood sits in the moderate range for that sequence. For antibody engineers this is a useful reframing. Antibody-specific language models like Ab-Lang, Antiberta, and Iglm are trained on repertoire data, which is much more concentrated around a small number of germline scaffolds than general protein space. This means that a very large antibody language model is more likely to assign extreme likelihoods to a given C-D-R than a general model would to a general protein. It suggests that the current mid-sized antibody language models — models in the hundred-million to few-hundred-million parameter range — may in fact be in the right regime, and that naive scaling to billions of parameters could be counterproductive for the fitness-scoring tasks people actually want to run. It is also a reminder that when you use an antibody language model to rank C-D-R variants, you should be checking whether the wildtype likelihood you are anchoring on sits in the informative range for that model, and not treating log-likelihood as a universally meaningful score.

The third item, and this one is genuinely of the moment because a version three was posted today, is the Koohy lab's characterization of AlphaFold 3's ability to predict T cell antigen specificity. The correspondence goes to Hashem Koohy at the University of Oxford, with Charlotte Deane and Jamie Rossjohn also on the paper. The Deane group's involvement is notable because they have been building the structural benchmarks for antibody and T cell receptor modeling for years, and Rossjohn is one of the leading structural immunologists working on T cell antigen recognition.

The problem the paper takes on is one that has been simmering for two years. T cell receptors, or T-C-Rs, recognize peptide antigens presented by M-H-C molecules on the surface of other cells. Mapping which T-C-R sees which peptide-M-H-C, or p-M-H-C, complex is central to designing T cell vaccines, engineering T cell therapies, and predicting immune reactivity. It is also brutally hard. The interface is small, the peptide is a fragment, and sequence similarity between T-C-Rs is a poor predictor of shared specificity. Sequence-based machine learning methods have struggled to generalize to peptides they were not trained on. The recent I-M-M-R-E-P twenty-five benchmark showed that AlphaFold-based methods appeared to promise better generalization to novel antigens. Whether that promise holds up under rigorous, benchmark-controlled scrutiny is the question the paper takes on.

Their contribution is threefold. First, they built a pipeline that runs AlphaFold 3 for T-C-R colon p-M-H-C structure prediction more than one hundred times faster than the default implementation. That matters because if you want to screen realistic-sized repertoires, you cannot afford minutes per interaction. Second, they benchmarked AlphaFold 3 and related structure-prediction models on the I-M-M-R-E-P twenty-five task and asked what signal the models are actually using to distinguish binders from non-binders. And here the finding is careful, and it is skeptical of a naive interpretation. When you look at what determines an AlphaFold-derived binding score, the dominant correlate is the positioning of the T-C-R over the p-M-H-C — the geometric orientation of the docked structure — not the chemical detail of the interaction interface. In other words, AlphaFold is telling you whether the T-C-R is in a plausible pose, not whether the two proteins are engaging in the specific residue-level contacts that would define real binding. That is a much weaker claim than "structure prediction has solved T-C-R specificity."

Third, they refine the AlphaFold-derived scores by training a small machine learning model — they call it the P-A-E Aggregator — that combines the predicted aligned error output of AlphaFold 3 into a more calibrated binding score. And with that refinement, they can do two things that suggest AlphaFold 3 has captured something real, if partial. It clusters sequence-similar T-C-Rs by their binding mode, meaning T-C-Rs that share sequence structure end up docked onto their p-M-H-C in similar orientations, which is the kind of geometric consistency you would expect from a good structural model. And it recapitulates mutational scanning experiments, where the effects of point mutations on binding are correctly ranked in the model's output. Both of these are non-trivial. Clustering by binding mode is exactly the kind of structural regularity a sequence-only model cannot see, and mutational scanning recovery is a stringent test because point mutations often live in the details.

The take from this paper is neither the celebratory version nor the dismissive one. AlphaFold 3, applied to T-C-R and p-M-H-C interactions, is not a specificity oracle. Its predictive signal comes disproportionately from whether the docking pose is plausible, not from the fine chemistry of the interface. But at the same time, it is not empty. With a small learned refinement layer on top, it detects mutational effects, and it clusters related T-C-Rs by binding mode in a way that suggests it has learned something genuine about the geometry of recognition. For antibody engineers reading this, there is a direct parallel. AlphaFold 3 does antibody colon antigen complex prediction with the same architectural machinery it uses for T-C-R colon p-M-H-C, and the same critique likely applies. The confident binding scores probably reflect docking-pose plausibility more than interface chemistry, and the practical utility comes from combining the model's structural output with a task-appropriate scoring head — not from taking the confidence metric at face value.

Three items, then, for tonight. A first-in-human trial that says an AI-designed monoclonal antibody can, in fact, be an unremarkable antibody where it counts — safety, pharmacokinetics, half-life. A scaling paper that says the largest protein language model is not always the right protein language model for fitness scoring. And a structure-prediction benchmark that says AlphaFold 3 can help you rank T-C-R and antigen interactions, but only if you understand which parts of its output to trust. Taken together, they are a picture of a field that is starting to answer, with hard data, the questions that have been standing in for hope for the last three years. Whether AI-designed antibodies work in humans. Whether scaling laws behave normally for proteins. What structure prediction can and cannot tell you about specificity. The answers are not the ones you would give in a slide deck. They are better. They are specific.
