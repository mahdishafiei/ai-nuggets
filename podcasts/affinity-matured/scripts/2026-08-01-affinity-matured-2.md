## Script

Good afternoon. Bonus edition of Affinity Matured for Saturday, August first. This one covers three items that came through the peer-reviewed journal side of the pipeline while the morning episode ran off the fresh bioRxiv corpus. First, from the Jin lab at the University of Science and Technology of China, published in the Journal of Virology on July thirtieth, a nanobody-A-C-E-two fusion that targets a cryptic conserved epitope at the inner base of the spike receptor-binding domain and gets a thirty-three-fold neutralization boost from combining single-domain antibody recognition with viral-attachment blockade in one molecule. Second, from the Boyd and Frank labs at Stanford and Tulane, published in Science Advances on July thirty-first, the discovery that twenty-six species of bats — an entire mammalian family — carry two immunoglobulin heavy chain gene loci on separate chromosomes, the first time any mammal has been documented with dual heavy chain loci, with distinct diversification and class-switch behavior between them. And third, from the Svilenov group at Ghent and the Technical University of Munich, in the Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences on July thirty-first, an integrated experimental-and-computational developability panel run on five human Fab fragments with long third-C-D-Rs on the heavy chain that argues developability liabilities of extended-C-D-R-H-three antibodies are sequence-dependent, not length-dependent.

First item. Journal of Virology, "A-C-E-two-fused nanobody targeting a cryptic R-B-D epitope broadly neutralizes SARS-like viruses," from Tengchuan Jin's group at the University of Science and Technology of China with collaborators at the Academy of Military Medical Sciences. The problem statement is familiar. SARS-CoV-two has been circulating for over five years, most clinically approved monoclonal antibodies have been rendered ineffective by successive variants, and the twenty-plus receptor-binding-domain mutations carried by variants like B-Q-one-point-one have knocked out most of the approved antibody arsenal. The strategic question is how to find neutralizing antibodies that stay effective across an entire class of SARS-like coronaviruses, including future spillovers.

Their approach starts at the immunization stage. Instead of immunizing an alpaca with one antigen, they alternated three rounds of SARS-CoV — the original SARS-one — receptor-binding domain with three rounds of SARS-CoV-two receptor-binding domain. Because these two antigens diverge substantially in variable epitopes but preserve a set of conserved surface residues in the receptor-binding fold, immunizing against both alternately biases the B-cell response toward the conserved, cross-reactive regions and away from the variable ones. The alpaca serum after alternating immunization titered above fifteen thousand against SARS-one and above three thousand against both SARS-two and the Omicron receptor-binding domain, confirming the cross-reactive polyclonal response. From that response they built a phage-display nanobody library, panned one round on Omicron receptor-binding domain, screened individual clones for SARS-one and SARS-two binding by phage E-L-I-S-A, and sequenced positive hits. Four unique nanobody sequences came out — aS-R-twenty-nine, aS-R-one-ninety-six, aS-R-three-forty-seven, and aS-R-three-forty-eight — all four with picomolar-to-nanomolar affinities against both SARS-one and SARS-two receptor-binding domains as measured by surface plasmon resonance. And critically, the same four nanobodies also bound the receptor-binding domains of Omicron BA-one, BA-two, BA-five, X-B-B, B-Q-one-point-one, and the X-B-B-one-point-five spike — with picomolar-to-nanomolar affinities across the whole variant panel, comparable to their wild-type SARS-two affinity. So the strategy worked: alternating immunization biased the repertoire onto conserved surface.

The most interesting nanobody is aS-R-twenty-nine, and the reason is where it binds. They crystallized a ternary complex of aS-R-twenty-nine, aS-R-three-forty-seven, and SARS-one receptor-binding domain and solved the structure at two-point-sixty-eight angstrom resolution by molecular replacement. Interface analysis shows aS-R-twenty-nine contacts twelve residues on SARS-one receptor-binding domain that map onto what the authors describe as a cryptic, highly conserved epitope at the inner base of the receptor-binding domain — a face of the domain that is sterically shielded from conventional antibodies in the closed spike conformation and is essentially never accessible to conventional immunoglobulin-G paratopes because it takes single-domain camelid V-H-H geometry to reach into the crevice. Because the nanobody targets this shielded face rather than the receptor-binding motif, viral escape at the motif does not compromise binding, which is the mechanistic root of the broad cross-variant activity. aS-R-twenty-nine potently neutralized pseudotyped SARS-one, SARS-two, and multiple Omicron subvariants including X-B-B-one-point-five and B-Q-one-point-one — the same X-B-B-one-point-five and B-Q-one-point-one that knock out most clinical antibodies.

The engineering step is where the paper turns from a discovery paper into an engineering paper. They fused aS-R-twenty-nine to the extracellular domain of A-C-E-two — the same A-C-E-two the virus uses as its cellular receptor — and then to an Fc for half-life extension. The construct, aS-R-twenty-nine-A-C-E-two-Fc, is a bispecific: one end recognizes the shielded, conserved epitope at the inner base of the receptor-binding domain, and the other end presents A-C-E-two, which competes for the same site the virus is trying to engage on the host cell. Both binders are simultaneously engaged with the receptor-binding domain but through independent, non-overlapping mechanisms — nanobody at the cryptic base, A-C-E-two at the exposed apex. The neutralization potency against wild-type SARS-two goes up thirty-three-fold compared to the parental aS-R-twenty-nine-Fc alone. And because the A-C-E-two arm is a decoy that mutates when the virus mutates its own receptor-binding motif — the virus can't escape without also losing binding to its actual receptor — the fusion is resilient against future escape variants in a way a pure antibody isn't. That's the strategic argument: bispecific nanobody-A-C-E-two fusions as a template for pandemic-preparedness anti-coronavirus therapeutics.

For antibody-engineering listeners the transferable lessons are three. Alternating immunization against paired divergent antigens is a scalable, in-vivo way to bias the germinal-center response toward conserved surface, without needing to computationally design the immunogen. Nanobody geometry accesses cryptic epitopes that conventional immunoglobulin-G paratopes cannot reach — the specific single-domain footprint here contacted twelve residues in a shielded pocket. And fusing an antibody or nanobody to its target's own natural ligand is a way of guaranteeing mutation-resistant activity: the virus cannot escape a decoy of its own receptor without also losing the receptor.

Second item. Science Advances, "Immunoglobulin heavy chain locus duplication in bats," from Taylor Pursell in Scott Boyd's group at Stanford Pathology with Hannah Frank's group at Tulane. This one is a repertoire biology paper rather than an engineering paper, but the finding is dramatic enough that anyone modeling antibody repertoires should know about it. The context is that in every mammal characterized to date — humans, mice, rats, dogs, cows, rabbits, non-human primates, all of them — the antibody heavy chain is encoded by a single locus on a single chromosome. Two functional heavy-chain loci is a fish thing, not a mammal thing. Salmonid fish have two immunoglobulin heavy chain loci on separate chromosomes, but that came from a whole-genome duplication event in the salmonid lineage. Mammals do not have that history.

Pursell and colleagues assembled a long-read genome for the big brown bat, Eptesicus fuscus, and found two heavy-chain loci on separate chromosomes. The smaller locus, which they call A-I-G-H, is on chromosome five, spans two hundred seventy-two kilobases, and encodes thirty-three V-genes with only fifteen percent pseudogenes. The larger locus, B-I-G-H, is on chromosome twenty-four, spans nine hundred eighteen kilobases, and encodes ninety-nine V-genes with forty-six-and-a-half percent pseudogenes. Both loci carry the canonical mammalian architecture — V-D-J segments followed by the five major constant-region genes I-G-H-M, I-G-H-D, I-G-H-G, I-G-H-E, and I-G-H-A — with the flanking genes intact on both chromosomes and A-I-D switch motifs enriched at the expected positions upstream of each constant-region gene. So both loci are, on genomic structure, fully functional.

They then extended the analysis to thirty-four bat species and found the duplication in every vespertilionid bat they examined except one, meaning it is not a fluke of one species. Phylogenetic analysis of the constant-region I-G-H-M sequences shows two clean well-supported clades by locus of origin across the family, with species relationships within each clade mirroring known species phylogeny. That places the duplication event at the vespertilionid family ancestor, roughly twenty-six to thirty-seven million years ago. Since bats have no history of whole-genome duplication, this appears to be a targeted immune-gene duplication driven by selection for enhanced humoral immunity — a convergent evolutionary trajectory to the salmonid situation but from an independent mechanism.

Then they asked the functional question: does the bat actually use both loci? Single-cell R-N-A sequencing on splenocytes from four bats, over sixty thousand cells profiled, gave them the tools to look at heavy-chain expression at single-cell resolution. Four findings matter. One: allelic exclusion is intact. Of the roughly ten thousand B-cells for which they could assemble complete heavy-chain rearrangements, only two percent had two in-frame C-D-R-H-three transcripts — consistent with allelic exclusion holding despite the dual-locus architecture, with the two-percent likely reflecting sequencing doublets rather than genuinely double-expressing cells. Two: rearrangement is ordered, with A-I-G-H first. The ratio of A-I-G-H-expressing to B-I-G-H-expressing naive B-cells is about three-point-four to one on average, which is above the two-point-two ratio you'd expect from purely sequential rearrangement with a one-in-three success rate per attempt. That extra bias suggests either even more strongly ordered rearrangement or negative selection against B-I-G-H expressers in the naive compartment. Three: class switching and somatic hypermutation differ between the two loci. I-G-A class-switched cells disproportionately use the A-I-G-H locus — thirty-eight I-G-A cells from A-I-G-H versus seven from B-I-G-H per animal on average — and class-switched cells that do use B-I-G-H carry substantially lower somatic-hypermutation frequencies than their A-I-G-H counterparts. Since somatic hypermutation and class switching are both driven by the same enzyme, activation-induced cytidine deaminase, the finding suggests that A-I-D access or activity is systematically lower at the B-I-G-H locus in class-switched cells. Four: V-gene family usage differs sharply between the loci. Both loci lean on the V-H-three family, but A-I-G-H is enriched for V-H-one and B-I-G-H is enriched for V-H-seven and V-H-six, and the V-gene usage patterns shift between I-G-M-expressing and I-G-G-expressing cells in locus-specific ways consistent with antigen-driven selection acting on both loci but with different preferred germlines.

The interpretation is that the two heavy-chain loci in vespertilionid bats are not redundant — they are functionally partitioned, with A-I-G-H carrying the dominant class-switched, high-affinity, mucosal I-G-A-heavy compartment and B-I-G-H carrying a more diverse but less-affinity-matured, more-I-G-M-heavy compartment. This creates a system where the same B-cell developmental repertoire supports two distinct response modes — a fast, diverse, largely primary-response arm on one chromosome, and a slower, affinity-matured, class-switched arm on the other. The paper explicitly speculates that this may contribute to bats' ability to host so many zoonotic viruses without visible disease. For anyone building antibody repertoire language models or repertoire-analysis pipelines, the implication is concrete: bats are a wholly new immunoglobulin gene template that current tools built on human-and-mouse germlines will call wrong. And more broadly, the dual-locus finding is a reminder that the single-locus assumption baked into germline databases, repertoire assemblers, and antibody-annotation tools is a mammalian generalization that doesn't hold for a whole family of animals.

Third item. Journal of Pharmaceutical Sciences, "Physicochemical developability insights into human Fab fragments with extended hypervariable loops," from Hristo Svilenov at the Technical University of Munich with collaborators at Ghent University — this one is going off the abstract only, the full text is behind an Elsevier subscription wall the pipeline can't get through. Longer-C-D-R-H-three antibodies are the go-to solution when your target is a small buried epitope or when you need to reach into a groove or a cryptic pocket — the extended loop lets the antibody probe geometrically inaccessible sites that a short-loop paratope simply can't reach. That's the therapeutic upside. The downside is that everyone in the field has an intuition that long C-D-R-H-three tends to correlate with developability problems — poor expression, aggregation, non-specific binding, conformational instability — and this intuition has been informal enough that early-stage screening pipelines often penalize C-D-R-H-three length outright.

The Svilenov group ran the actual experiment. They took five human Fabs with unusually long third-C-D-Rs on the heavy chain, expressed them, and ran the standard biopharma developability panel — secretion yields, protein-A elution and homogeneity, thermal-and-chemical denaturation for conformational stability, self-interaction and diffusion coefficients for colloidal stability, non-specific-binding assays including standard cross-interaction chromatography and the more recent baculovirus-particle E-L-I-S-A. They complemented the experimental panel with molecular-dynamics simulations of each Fab so they could interpret the observed liabilities in terms of specific loop conformations and residue-level flexibility.

The headline finding is that C-D-R-H-three length by itself does not predict which Fab has which liability. The five Fabs, all with long third-C-D-Rs, split across the developability panel — some had secretion problems, some had colloidal stability problems, some had non-specific binding hits, some had multiple liabilities, and the distribution of which Fab had which liability did not correlate with the C-D-R-H-three length. Instead, the specific liabilities tracked sequence composition, molecular-dynamics-observed conformational dynamics of the loop, and framework context — mechanistically informed features rather than a single length parameter. The conclusion, in the authors' own framing, is that developability strategy for extended-C-D-R-H-three antibodies has to be sequence-and-mechanism-informed, not length-informed. Screening antibodies out solely because they carry a long C-D-R-H-three throws away binders that would develop cleanly, and passing antibodies through solely because they have a short C-D-R-H-three lets liabilities slip past.

The practical takeaway for anyone doing computational developability triage on early antibody libraries is straightforward. Length filters on C-D-R-H-three are cheap and easy but they are not calibrated to the actual biophysics — the same length can carry very different loop-flexibility profiles depending on the underlying sequence, and it's those flexibility profiles that drive the observed liabilities. Models that take C-D-R-H-three sequence as input and predict developability score — the direction the field has been moving for the last two years — are where this paper's data would slot in as a validation set. And for wet-lab campaigns targeting difficult epitopes that require an extended loop, the message is not to preemptively deselect long-loop candidates but to run the full developability panel and pick the sequence-clean ones from the pool. Going off the abstract here — the specific residues and dynamics findings from the molecular-dynamics analysis would be worth reading in full when the paper is accessible.
