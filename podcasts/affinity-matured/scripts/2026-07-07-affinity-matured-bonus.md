First item, and today's lead for this bonus round. On biorxiv from Brian Pierce's group at U-Maryland I-B-B-R, with Rui Yin as first author — "Benchmarking AlphaFold and related deep learning approaches for modeling antibody and T-C-R antigen recognition." This is the question anyone doing structural work on immune complexes has an opinion about, and now there is a systematic answer to point to. The setup: the authors evaluated AlphaFold-two, AlphaFold-three, increased sampling protocols on top of both, and related deep-learning methods, across three interface classes — antibody-protein complexes, antibody-peptide complexes, and T-C-R-peptide-M-H-C complexes. This last class is important because peptide-M-H-C is where a huge fraction of immunotherapy design happens.

The headline findings. AlphaFold-three outperforms AlphaFold-two on average, and increased sampling — running many more seeds and picking the top-ranked model — helps both. So the operational advice is the direction the field has already been drifting: use AF-three, and run a lot of samples. But the improvements are not uniform. The class that gives every model the most trouble is antibody-peptide complexes — small antigens, and yet the hardest, which is counterintuitive if you think of small size as easier for docking. The paper frames this as a real limitation. Per-case success across methods showed complementarity, meaning different methods succeed on different targets, and a model-pooling strategy — running multiple methods and picking the best confidence across them — raised antibody-peptide near-native success from forty-one percent to fifty-nine percent. That is an eighteen-percentage-point jump, and it is essentially free: no new model, no retraining, just running the tools you already have and taking the union of their wins.

Two nuances worth naming. First, the paper analyzes AlphaFold confidence scores across these three classes, which speaks to the workflow question of "can I trust the p-A-E to tell me which of my predictions to believe" — the answer is class-dependent, and antibody-peptide is again where confidence is most treacherous. Second, they include a case study of modeling a noncanonical complex, meaning the kind of geometry that isn't well-represented in the training set — this is essentially probing generalization beyond the frame the model was fit on. The take-home for the antibody-engineering audience is practical. If your pipeline runs AlphaFold-three once per candidate and takes the top model, you are leaving a meaningful chunk of correct predictions on the table, particularly for peptide-sized antigens; and if you are engineering against a peptide antigen or a T-C-R-p-M-H-C system, the field's structural-modeling floor is lower than the field's structural-modeling headline claims. The paper is a preprint and I am reading it from the abstract — the full text hadn't rendered on biorxiv when I fetched it — but the numbers and framing are clean enough to act on as guidance.

Second item. Also on biorxiv, posted July sixth from a Genedata team based in Basel — Daniel Siegismund, Mario Wieser, Eriberto Natali, and Stephan Steigele — "Accurate delta-T-m prediction without protein structure inputs for biomolecular stability." Delta-T-m, spelled out for the ear, is the mutation-induced change in a protein's melting temperature. It is one of the two canonical numbers you care about in developability triage — the other being expression titer — and predicting it well from sequence has become a race between sequence-only protein language models and structure-informed methods that consume an AlphaFold or crystal structure alongside the sequence.

The claim in this paper is that structure is not necessary. A well-tuned sequence-only E-S-M-two-six-hundred-fifty-million-parameter model beats structure-informed methods on their benchmark, achieving the lowest mean absolute error and root-mean-squared error and a competitive Pearson correlation without pH input and without structural input. The methodological interest here is not the architecture — it is E-S-M-two, off the shelf — but the training design. The authors ran what they call an autoresearch-inspired setup search combined with controlled ablations, and they report that the design choices that materially move the number are: the choice of loss function, the pooling strategy over token representations, the auxiliary supervision signal, and the fine-tuning regime. In their framing, prior sequence-only baselines underperformed structure-informed methods not because sequence-only is fundamentally worse, but because the sequence-only pipelines had been under-optimized on these training-recipe dimensions.

Why this pairs with what shipped this morning. The E-S-M plus M-M-slash-G-B-S-A paper from Schrödinger — which I covered in the first episode — concluded that E-S-M alone gets zero point five nine seven R-O-C-A-U-C on their large single-mutation stability benchmark and needs the physics method's complementary signal to reach zero point seven four three. This Genedata paper argues that when you invest carefully in the training recipe, sequence-only can outperform the structure-informed baseline for delta-T-m regression. These two are not in direct contradiction — they use different benchmarks, different labels, different targets: R-O-C-A-U-C for stability-enhancing versus destabilizing versus M-A-E and R-M-S-E for a continuous delta-T-m value; single mutations versus a mixed set — but they are pointing at different practical conclusions. The reconciling story is probably that the untuned E-S-M log-odds signal is weak, and that fine-tuning on a large delta-T-m dataset extracts a lot more of the model's stability knowledge than log-odds does. If you are building a stability triage tool for antibody variable domains, the take-home is that "structure-informed" is not automatically better than "sequence-only" — and the ablations in this preprint are more informative about *how* to train a good sequence-only predictor than the headline claim.

Third item and the last for this bonus round. On biorxiv from Rune Damgaard's group at the Novo Nordisk Foundation Center for Protein Research in Copenhagen, with Randi Franck as first author — "Development and characterization of a versatile single-domain antibody specific for methionine-one-linked ubiquitin chains." Ubiquitin signaling is mediated by chemically distinct polyubiquitin linkage types — methionine-one, lysine-forty-eight, lysine-sixty-three, and so on — and each linkage encodes a different downstream signal. The community's ability to study atypical linkages has been limited by the shortage of chain-type-specific affinity reagents, which today are almost entirely conventional monoclonals or bespoke tandem-U-B-D constructs.

What is new here is the scaffold and the workflow. The authors used phage display against synthetic human single-domain-antibody libraries — human sdAbs, meaning the human-derived V-H domain scaffold with C-D-R diversity encoded synthetically, not immunized camelid nanobodies — and pulled out a binder named two-A-six that binds M-one-linked ubiquitin chains with nanomolar affinity. They validate specificity at two levels: two-A-six discriminates M-one linkages from other linkages both at the diubiquitin level and at the level of long polyubiquitin chains. To the authors' knowledge, two-A-six is the first reported sdAb with defined specificity for a homotypic ubiquitin chain linkage. It sits in a small physical form factor — a single domain — and it can be produced in E. coli from a single expression plasmid.

The AI-relevant piece is the epitope mapping. The authors used AlphaFold-three to model the two-A-six binding mode against M-one-linked diubiquitin, and the model predicted that two-A-six recognizes both the proximal and distal ubiquitin moieties together with the region right around the M-one linkage — that is, the linkage itself is part of the epitope, which is exactly how a linkage-specific binder should work. They then tested that model by saturation mutagenesis of the binder, and the mutagenesis data support the AF-three-predicted binding mode. The functional payload is standard reagent-development validation — E-L-I-S-A, immunoblotting, immunoprecipitation under semi-denaturing conditions, substrate-ubiquitination readout, and immunofluorescence microscopy — and two-A-six works in all of them. The two-part message for our audience is worth naming. First, this is a compact demonstration of the modern sdAb development workflow — synthetic human sdAb library, phage display, AlphaFold-three-guided epitope hypothesis, saturation-mutagenesis validation — as a template for producing linkage-specific or conformation-specific binders more generally, not just for ubiquitin. Second, the fact that AlphaFold-three's predicted binding mode was corroborated by mutagenesis for a nanomolar-affinity sdAb against a linkage-specific epitope is one more data point in the ongoing empirical question of when to trust the AF-three complex prediction — the answer here is that when the confidence is reasonable and the geometry is chemically sensible, mutagenesis backs the model.

That is the bonus round. A systematic AlphaFold benchmark that puts a number on the antibody-peptide problem and shows that model pooling gains an easy eighteen points on near-native success; a sequence-only E-S-M-two paper that pushes back on the "structure is required" position for melting-temperature prediction with a careful training recipe; and a linkage-specific single-domain antibody developed from a synthetic human sdAb library with AlphaFold-three-guided epitope mapping and mutagenesis validation.
