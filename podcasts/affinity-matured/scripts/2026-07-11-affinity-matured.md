**First: PTIm-mAb — an end-to-end multi-objective platform for bispecific antibody design, benchmarked on eleven FDA-approved products.**

Late-stage attrition in therapeutic antibody discovery is dominated by developability liabilities: aggregation, polyspecificity, charge-driven non-specific binding, and chain-mispairing artifacts. Bispecific antibodies compound those risks because each additional binding arm adds a biophysical envelope that has to be jointly satisfied. The in-silico ecosystem for antibody engineering has plenty of individual tools — humanization scorers, structure predictors, single-metric developability profilers like Aggrescan, CamSol, and the Therapeutic Antibody Profiler — but relatively few platforms integrate them end-to-end with joint optimization across the axes.

PTIm-mAb, from SANSHI Bio Solutions, is a multi-objective machine-learning-driven antibody design platform that jointly optimizes sequence liabilities, surface aggregation propensity, charge balance, humanness, and predicted binding affinity, and recommends a bispecific architecture in one workflow. This preprint is a benchmarking exercise: the authors apply their platform to the published sequences of eleven FDA-approved bispecific antibodies using the platform's default-parameter Pareto-acceptance optimization loop, run to convergence or to an internal iteration ceiling, with no human curation between the platform run and the external profiler.

The setup is important. Both wild-type and platform-optimized sequences are profiled independently with three publicly available developability tools — the same Aggrescan, CamSol, and Therapeutic Antibody Profiler that the community actually uses. The scoring is not done by the platform itself. Paired-sample statistical tests — Wilcoxon signed-rank, exact binomial sign test, McNemar exact test — evaluate whether the direction and magnitude of the changes are significant.

The results. Across seventeen evaluable paired arms profiled by the Therapeutic Antibody Profiler, PTIm-mAb cleared four wild-type CDR-vicinity Positive Charge Patch flags — the CDR-vicinity positive charge patch is one of the profiler's canonical developability red flags, and its presence in a therapeutic candidate correlates with high non-specific binding and poor pharmacokinetics. Specifically: Blinatumomab arm one went from one-point-nine-nine to zero-point-six-nine. Mosunetuzumab arm one went from one-point-three-four to zero-point-zero-six. Linvoseltamab arm two went from zero-point-eight-one to zero. And the headline case, Elranatamab arm one, went from one-point-eight-zero to zero-point-five-eight. Those four fixes were achieved without trading off any other in-range metric, and were corroborated by both Aggrescan and CamSol on the same arm.

Total CDR length was significantly shortened across the cohort — Wilcoxon two-sided p-value zero-point-zero-zero-seven-five, effect size zero-point-six-five — which is significant improvement on the metric most directly under the optimizer's control. On Aggrescan integrated aggregation propensity, the directional shift was also significant by sign test: twenty-four of thirty-six chains improved, two were unchanged, ten worsened, with a p-value of zero-point-zero-two-one. On the already-clean Zenocutuzumab profile the optimizer identified residual headroom and closed it further, demonstrating that the platform's value extends to candidates that already pass all flags.

Three cases did not clear all flags — Teclistamab arm one, Emicizumab, and Talquetamab arm two — and are presented as candidates for iterative re-invocation of the platform on the already-optimized output. The remaining Therapeutic Antibody Profiler metrics — the profiler's positive surface hydrophobicity score, positive charge patch magnitude, positive negative charge, and the Fv chain space charge product — trended in the improvement direction without reaching significance in this cohort. That is a pattern one expects when a calibrated multi-objective optimizer is applied to molecules that are already inside the clinical-stage envelope: there is not much headroom left to move the metrics that were already fine.

Two things stand out about the framing of this paper. One, the benchmarking is done blindly by external tools, not by the platform's own scorers, which is a much harder test than the usual self-scoring. Two, the study reports the developability improvements without claiming that the resulting sequences would be better clinical candidates than what actually reached patients. That is the correct level of restraint: developability metrics are a filter, not a substitute for wet-lab characterization, and clearing a flag on a computational profiler does not guarantee superior clinical behavior. What the paper shows is that a multi-objective optimizer running unattended can produce sequences that outperform the wild-type on quantitative developability axes in a meaningful fraction of cases. As triage and lead-optimization for bispecific antibody discovery, that is a useful step.

The platform reports a mean of twelve-point-eight months and roughly seven-hundred-and-twenty-four-thousand U-S dollars of computational front-loading per project across the nine-project cohort. Those numbers are the platform's own accounting, not third-party validated, so treat them as vendor-supplied context rather than as measured savings. But the direction is clear: multi-objective in-silico optimization runs on modern compute for pennies compared to running the equivalent Design-Build-Test-Learn cycle at the bench.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.07.736933

**Second: R-F-diffusion and Protein-M-P-N-N applied to plant immune receptor engineering — designing new pathogen recognition into a rice-blast N-L-R sensor domain.**

This is a David Baker collaboration with Nicholas Talbot's group at the Sainsbury Laboratory and Mark Banfield's group at the John Innes Centre, and it is one of the more interesting proof-of-concept applications of de novo binder design I've seen this month. It also crosses over from the antibody-and-therapeutics world into plant pathology in a way that showcases what generative protein design tools can actually do when you push them beyond the settings they were originally developed for.

The problem: plant intracellular immune receptors — nucleotide-binding leucine-rich repeat receptors, or N-L-R receptors — are the sensors that detect pathogen effector proteins inside plant cells and trigger cell death and immune responses. A subset of these receptors, of which the rice-blast Pik-1 and Pik-2 pair are the archetype, have a modular architecture: the sensor N-L-R carries an integrated domain that is genetically fused into the receptor's scaffold and is the actual binding partner for the pathogen effector. The integrated domain evolved from a copy of a host protein that the pathogen targets, and when the pathogen's effector binds the integrated domain inside the plant cell, the receptor pair activates and the plant mounts an immune response.

Natural integrated domains are constrained: they evolved to recognize whatever effector the ancestral pathogen produced, and while breeders and molecular biologists have been able to swap known integrated domains between receptor chassis with some success, the space of effectors that plant N-L-R receptors can recognize is bounded by what's already in nature. The pace of pathogen evolution outstrips the pace of discovering and deploying new resistance genes.

The idea in this paper is to short-circuit that limitation by computationally designing new integrated domains from scratch against arbitrary pathogen effectors, and fusing those into the Pik-1 receptor chassis. The design pipeline is textbook current-generation de novo binder design: R-F-diffusion generates de novo backbones with a specified binding interface against the target effector protein, then Protein-M-P-N-N assigns sequences to those backbones, then the top designs are ranked by AlphaFold-based interface confidence metrics and taken forward to expression.

The showcase target here is a virulence factor — a pathogen effector protein — from Fusarium oxysporum f. sp. cubense Tropical Race 4, the pathogen responsible for Panama disease of banana. This is a devastating pathogen with no natural N-L-R-based resistance in cultivated banana. The authors design de novo integrated domains against this Fusarium effector, insert them into the rice-blast Pik-1 receptor chassis, and then test in Nicotiana benthamiana whether the designed receptor mounts an immune response when the Fusarium effector is co-expressed.

The functional readouts work. The designed integrated domains facilitate specific effector perception and initiate immune signaling — the receptor triggers cell death in the presence of its target effector but not in negative controls, showing that the specificity of the designed binder has been transferred through the receptor architecture to become a physiologically meaningful immune output. Structural and biophysical analyses confirm that the de novo integrated domains fold with high structural fidelity to the initial designs and associate with their targets via the interfaces the design pipeline predicted — the geometry between design and reality is close enough that the receptors work as intended.

A subtler finding: the authors note that some designed integrated domain insertions caused autoactivity — the receptor triggering cell death even without the target effector present — while others led to effector-mediated repression of cell death, essentially the opposite of what a functional resistance receptor should do. This is orthogonal evidence for the role of the integrated domain in regulating N-L-R signaling: the geometry and identity of the integrated domain doesn't just determine what the receptor recognizes, it also constrains whether the receptor's on-off switch operates in the intended direction. This is a fundamental biology finding riding on top of the engineering success.

For the show's audience, the key takeaway is not the plant immunology per se. It is that R-F-diffusion plus Protein-M-P-N-N, plus AlphaFold-based ranking — the same design stack that groups are using to design de novo binders against therapeutic targets — is now producing wet-lab-validated custom sensor domains that can be transplanted into complex biological signaling architectures with the specificity preserved. The design tools are increasingly good enough that the interesting question is which complex biological problem to point them at next.

The authors position this as a framework for programmable plant immune receptors — engineering crop resistance against emerging pathogens by design rather than by discovery. Beyond agriculture, the same conceptual approach — computationally designing binders that get inserted into modular signaling scaffolds to reprogram what the scaffold responds to — has obvious analogues in synthetic biology and mammalian receptor engineering, including chimeric antigen receptor design.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.10.737686

**Third: UMA-Inverse — a dense pair-representation encoder for ligand-conditioned inverse folding, benchmarked head-to-head against Ligand-M-P-N-N.**

Ligand-M-P-N-N is the current standard for ligand-conditioned inverse folding — designing protein sequences that will fold into a target backbone and bind a specified ligand. It uses a sparse graph encoder in which residue nodes and ligand-atom nodes exchange messages along edges built from spatial neighbors. That representation is compact and computationally efficient, but its message-passing radius is limited by the graph's edge topology: information about the ligand's identity and geometry has to hop through the graph to reach distal residues, and the signal decays as it propagates.

UMA-Inverse, posted this week to arXiv, asks whether a different representational choice — a dense pair encoder that operates on all residue-residue and residue-ligand-atom pairs — can propagate ligand information more effectively for the same design task. The architecture is a six-block Pair-Mixer that uses triangle multiplication updates — the same class of operation used in the pair track of AlphaFold — but drops the more expensive triangle self-attention. There is no separate sequence track: the pair tensor is the model's whole representation. An auxiliary distogram objective supervises the pair encoder to predict inter-residue distances, providing structural training signal. The autoregressive decoder that emits residue identities attends over ligand atoms through a learned, position-specific readout of the pair tensor.

The model is small: about three-point-three million parameters, comparable to Ligand-M-P-N-N's roughly two-point-five million. Training data is exactly the LigandMPNN splits — same PDB source, same thirty-percent-sequence-identity cutoffs, same test evaluation — so the comparison is head to head on the metrics the field uses.

Interface recovery — the fraction of designed residues at the ligand interface that match the native crystal-structure identity — comes out slightly below Ligand-M-P-N-N across all three splits. Small molecules: fifty-six-point-one percent for UMA-Inverse, fifty-nine-point-eight for Ligand-M-P-N-N. Metals: fifty-five-point-one versus sixty-four-point-four. Nucleotides: thirty-five-point-three versus fifty-three-point-three. On the metric that the field uses to rank inverse folding models, the new architecture underperforms the incumbent.

But the paper also runs the designed sequences through Boltz-2 co-folding, which asks: if you take the designed sequence and let a structure prediction model fold it in the presence of the ligand, does it recover a ligand pose close to the native? Here, UMA-Inverse sequences show median ligand-pose R-M-S-D of one-point-five-three Angstroms against a native floor of zero-point-nine, and a ligand interface p-T-M of about zero-point-nine-six, indicating that Boltz-2 co-folding on the redesigned sequence produces very high-confidence native-like ligand-binding geometry. Ligand-M-P-N-N is modestly better on that metric — one-point-two-seven Angstroms median — but the gap is smaller than the interface recovery gap would suggest.

The authors argue that the dense encoder propagates ligand identity to residues far beyond the interface, where Ligand-M-P-N-N's sparse graph signal decays. That is plausible from the architecture, and it would predict that the two models make systematically different design choices at second-shell positions — positions that don't directly contact the ligand but shape the pocket. The paper does not directly confirm this with a residue-level analysis in the main text, and it does not run controlled ablations to isolate whether the distogram objective, the position-specific ligand attention, or the pair-mixer architecture individually contribute to the observed behavior. The authors acknowledge this as a limitation. There are no antibody-specific benchmarks — the evaluation is entirely on general protein-ligand systems.

The take-away here is not that UMA-Inverse should replace Ligand-M-P-N-N. The take-away is architectural: for ligand-conditioned inverse folding, a dense pair representation with distogram supervision is a viable design and matches the incumbent on structural-consistency metrics while trailing on interface recovery. The choice of pair representation over sparse graph has downstream consequences on the sequences the model proposes, and those consequences aren't fully characterized yet. For teams building ligand-conditioned design tools — including antibody-hapten design, where the ligand is a small molecule that the antibody has to grip — the paper is worth reading as an alternative point in the architecture space, not as a plug-in replacement.

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.07866

**Fourth: a review — Progress in structure prediction and design of adaptive immune receptors.**

This one is short. A review from Cohen, Hochner, and Schneidman-Duhovny at Hebrew University of Jerusalem posted this week in Current Opinion in Structural Biology surveys recent progress on adaptive immune receptors — antibodies and T-cell receptors — with a focus on structure prediction and design. The reason it's worth flagging is that it addresses in one place a cluster of questions this show revisits daily: how do CDR loop modeling accuracy, receptor-antigen interface prediction, and generative epitope-conditioned design actually stand up when evaluated carefully, what are the sources of bias in current benchmarks, and where do current methods reliably fail.

The authors cover three technical threads. First, adaptive immune receptor-specific language models — the antibody and T-cell receptor language models trained on repertoire data rather than on general protein sequence data. Second, structure prediction for antibody and T-cell receptor complexes with their antigens — the harder problem than free-standing receptor structure, because the CDR loops rearrange on binding and the receptor-antigen interface is where the physically interesting geometry lives. Third, generative design strategies, including inverse folding, diffusion-based backbone generation, sequence-space diffusion, and full sequence-structure co-design.

They also spend meaningful space on the datasets, evaluation metrics, and sources of bias that complicate cross-study comparisons — a specific problem the field has, where benchmarks often include structural data from the same complexes that models were trained on, or evaluate on distributions that don't match the design-time problem. The review calls out that reliable ranking of receptor-antigen complexes remains an unsolved problem, that accurate modeling of flexible CDR loops is still not a settled matter, and that scalable epitope-specific receptor design at the level of the pockets and hot-spot residues is still where the biggest methodological gains are to be made.

For anyone working on antibody or T-cell receptor design who wants a single reference to point at the current inflection point and its outstanding open problems, this is a useful survey. It is also a reasonable pointer to hand to collaborators from adjacent fields who want to catch up on what the deep learning revolution has and hasn't delivered for adaptive immune receptor engineering.

Paper link: https://doi.org/10.1016/j.sbi.2026.103326
