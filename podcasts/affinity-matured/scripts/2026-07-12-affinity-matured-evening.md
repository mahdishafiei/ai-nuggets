## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured for the evening of Sunday, July 12th. Two picks tonight from a genuinely thin weekend. The lead is a first-in-human phase 1 trial in Nature Medicine of a bispecific broadly neutralizing antibody engineered against H-I-V, from David Ho's group at Columbia and collaborators — a rare clinical readout on a format-engineered broadly neutralizing antibody, and worth reading for anyone thinking about how engineered antibody formats actually behave in people. The second item is a bioRxiv preprint on protein-language-model-guided data augmentation for downstream sequence prediction tasks, from Qiong Luo's group at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. Different problems, but they connect on a common thread: the gap between what an engineered antibody does on paper and what it does in a real biological system.

The lead is titled "Bispecific 10E8.4-iMab broadly neutralizing antibody in people with or without H-I-V-1: a partially randomized phase 1 trial." Fifty-four participants, corresponding authors Deborah Theodore and Magdalena Sobieszczyk at Columbia and David Ho at the Aaron Diamond A-I-D-S Research Center. Trial identifier N-C-T-0-3-8-7-5-2-0-9.

The molecule and why it exists. 10E8.4-iMab is a bispecific antibody with two functionally distinct arms. The 10E8.4 arm is a matured variant of 10E8, one of the well-known broadly neutralizing antibodies discovered in the mid-two-thousand-tens against a highly conserved region of the H-I-V envelope glycoprotein called the membrane-proximal external region, or M-P-E-R. M-P-E-R is broadly conserved across H-I-V strains because it is functionally constrained — the virus needs it for membrane fusion — which makes it an attractive target for broad neutralization but also a difficult one because the epitope sits close to the viral membrane and is transiently exposed. The other arm is ibalizumab, called iMab in the paper, a humanized monoclonal against human C-D-4, the cellular receptor H-I-V uses to enter T cells. Ibalizumab is already an F-D-A-approved therapeutic — the first monoclonal antibody approved for H-I-V, back in 2018 — for people with multi-drug-resistant infection.

The design logic behind pairing them is engineering-relevant. Combining a viral-epitope arm with a host-receptor arm is a recognized strategy for potent H-I-V neutralization: the C-D-4 arm concentrates the molecule at the cellular target that H-I-V has to engage, and the M-P-E-R arm then covers a difficult conserved viral epitope from the position that concentration puts it in. It is a spatial-targeting trick built into the format itself. Whether that trick translates into safety, developable pharmacokinetics, and antiviral activity in humans is exactly what the trial was designed to answer.

The design of the trial. Fifty-four participants across four arms and a partially randomized layout, split between people living with H-I-V and people without H-I-V. Arms one through three tested a dose-escalation from three-tenths of a milligram per kilogram up to 30 milligrams per kilogram, comparing intravenous and subcutaneous administration. Arm four was randomized between the bispecific and placebo at two-and-a-half and ten milligrams per kilogram subcutaneous. The primary outcome was safety and tolerability in the two weeks after administration. Secondary outcomes were pharmacokinetics, antiviral activity, induction of anti-drug antibodies, longitudinal C-D-4 and C-D-8 T cell counts, and long-term safety.

The result. The primary safety endpoint was met. No treatment-related serious adverse events, and no adverse events at grade three or above. The most common solicited adverse events were mild — injection-site tenderness in about eighteen percent of participants, fatigue in about a third, headache in about a fifth. What is worth flagging is that three of nine people living with H-I-V developed a generalized rash eight to twelve days after infusion, which resolved on its own within nine to sixteen days. Three out of nine is a meaningful signal in a phase 1 cohort — the paper does not read as alarmed by it, but it is the kind of finding that follow-up trials will want to characterize mechanistically. Delayed rash after antibody infusion is consistent with a serum-sickness-like pattern, which raises a question about anti-drug-antibody kinetics that the secondary-outcome data — collected but not reported in the abstract — should address in the full text.

Why does this matter to an audience that spends its time on antibody-engineering computation and design? Three reasons. First, this is what the endpoint of your work looks like. A bispecific broadly neutralizing antibody engineered against a conserved viral epitope goes into fifty-four people and produces manageable safety with a specific tolerability signal to characterize. If you have been designing bnAbs computationally against H-I-V-envelope epitopes, this trial is a concrete data point on how those molecules translate. Second, the specific format — one viral-epitope arm plus one host-receptor arm as a spatial-targeting bispecific — is a design pattern that generalizes. Analogous constructions are being explored for other viruses with hard-to-reach conserved epitopes, including influenza hemagglutinin stem antibodies. Third, immunogenicity of engineered antibody formats is an under-modeled problem in the computational-antibody-engineering literature. This trial produces exactly the kind of data — anti-drug-antibody kinetics, delayed hypersensitivity signals, long-term safety follow-up — that developability-prediction models will need to be benchmarked against if they are going to make claims about clinical translatability. It is a small dataset, but it is real, and it is publicly reported. The Ackerman group at Dartmouth, which does high-quality antibody-effector-function work, is listed on the author line, which is a positive signal for the immunology-side data quality.

One caveat worth naming. This trial is not itself an artificial-intelligence-driven engineering story — 10E8 and ibalizumab predate the current generation of antibody design tools, and the bispecific was engineered by conventional means. But the value for the audience is diagnostic rather than methodological: this is the kind of end-to-end data readout that A-I-designed antibodies will eventually need to produce, and reading trial-level results now sharpens what "success" means for the computational pipelines currently being built.

The second item is a bioRxiv preprint titled "Prot-Aug: An Empirical Investigation of protein-language-model-Guided Data Augmentation for Protein Sequence Prediction Tasks." First author Zhuoyang Chen, corresponding author Qiong Luo, at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology. Posted July 11th on bioRxiv.

The motivation is a familiar practitioner problem. Protein language models — E-S-M-2, ProtGPT-2, and their descendants — provide strong representations of protein sequences, but downstream fine-tuning on specific tasks is often bottlenecked by scarcity of labeled data. Data augmentation is the standard remedy in machine learning generally, but for protein sequences we do not have a clean equivalent of image cropping or word substitution. What does a plausible data augmentation for a protein sequence even look like? Prot-Aug is a systematic empirical study of protein-language-model-guided sequence augmentation across seven downstream protein prediction tasks.

The augmentation strategy is substitution-based. Take a labeled protein sequence, mask some positions, and let a protein language model propose amino-acid substitutions at those positions. The paper compares two flavors: substitutions guided by E-S-M-2, an encoder-based masked-language-model protein LM, and substitutions guided by ProtGPT-2, an autoregressive protein LM. They vary the fraction of positions substituted, from two percent all the way up to high variation levels, and compare against simpler augmentation baselines including random substitution and homology-based retrieval from B-L-A-S-T-adjacent sequences. The four research questions they frame at the outset are: does protein-LM-synthesized augmentation preserve more of the original biological signal than simpler methods; how much does augmentation improve downstream prediction accuracy; how does the variation level affect that improvement across tasks and model choices; and is biological plausibility a necessary condition for the augmentation to help.

The findings. First, the E-S-M-2-guided version — call it Prot-Aug-E-S-M — preserves motifs and structural similarity better than simple random substitution, often at a level comparable to using genuine homologous sequences retrieved from a database. That is a nontrivial claim in itself: a masked-language-model protein LM, doing local substitution, produces sequences that look more like real homologs than random noise around the seed sequence. Second, augmentation delivers consistent but task-dependent improvements — Prot-Aug-E-S-M is the best or second-best augmentation strategy in five of the seven tasks at ten-percent variation. Third, and this is the practically useful finding, low-to-moderate variation levels — roughly two to thirty percent of positions substituted — work best across most tasks. But structure-related tasks can actually benefit from high-variation augmentation, apparently because at high variation the augmentation stops functioning as label-preserving data and starts functioning as regularization. Fourth, biological plausibility of the augmentation is task- and variation-dependent — semantic preservation correlates with performance at low variation, but at high variation levels the improved generalization suggests the mechanism has shifted from label preservation to noise regularization. So the mechanism of benefit changes with the variation level, and the paper is careful to name that.

The implication for antibody language modeling is direct. Antibody-specific protein LMs — antibody-BERT, Ab-Lang, IgLM, p-IgGen, B-A-L-M, and their descendants — sit downstream of exactly the kind of fine-tuning task that is data-limited: paratope prediction, developability classification, affinity regression, immunogenicity prediction. If Prot-Aug's finding transfers, then using an antibody protein LM to generate substitution-based augmentations at ten to twenty percent variation is a straightforward thing to try, and the paper's guidance on variation levels is a useful starting point for a hyperparameter sweep. Antibodies have complementarity-determining regions with distributions very unlike a general protein backbone, so it is entirely plausible that the useful variation level in a complementarity-determining region is different from the useful variation level in a framework region — and testing that split explicitly is exactly the follow-up experiment this paper makes cheap to design. The general study is on the seven-task, non-antibody-specific benchmark, so any transfer claim should be tested; the paper is not itself claiming antibody transferability. But the empirical results and the four-question framing are unusually clean, and the paper is likely to become a reference point for augmentation work in the antibody-language-model space.
