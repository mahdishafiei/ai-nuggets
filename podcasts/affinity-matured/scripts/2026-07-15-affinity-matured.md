## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is July fifteenth. Five items to walk through, and they line up nicely around a single theme — how much of the antibody engineering task you can push into learned representations rather than task-specific training. First, H-three-BERT-a, a language model trained on nothing but heavy-chain C-D-R-three loops that still recovers most of what full-variable-region models get on repertoire analysis and bnAb classification. Second, a compact study showing that a frozen protein foundation model's embeddings, with a lightweight linear head, beats a from-scratch antibody-antigen delta-delta-G ranker. Third, a machine-learning-driven, closed-loop pipeline for antibody formulation optimization — thermal stability, viscosity, and diffusivity in one design cycle. Fourth, a computational framework called C-O-M-P-A-S-S paired with a modular T-cell-engager platform that predicts patient-specific dose compositions for multi-specific therapies. And fifth, a de novo binder-design paper from the Sahtoe and Knowles groups that targets an intrinsically disordered aggregation intermediate, hitting nanomolar affinity with a single round of partial diffusion.

Let's start with the antibody language model, because it is directly on-brief and it is a genuinely interesting question about representation scope.

The paper is titled "H-three-BERT-a: a C-D-R-H-three-specific language model for antibody repertoire analysis," published in Patterns on July tenth. The authors are at ETH Zurich and colleagues. The premise is one that anyone who has trained a full-length antibody language model has probably had to reason about explicitly. The heavy-chain variable region is roughly a hundred and twenty residues, but the bulk of that is conserved framework and secondary C-D-R loops with heavy germline constraints. The one region that actually carries most of the antigen-specificity signal is C-D-R-H-three — the third heavy-chain complementarity-determining region, formed by V-D-J recombination with junctional diversity, typically ten to twenty residues long, and by far the most variable segment of the whole molecule. When you train a masked language model on full variable regions, most of the tokens you are learning to reconstruct are framework residues where the answer is nearly predictable from germline. The gradient on the interesting positions gets diluted. So the question the authors ask is: what if you train only on C-D-R-H-three? Do you keep the immunologically meaningful signal, or do you lose the framework context that anchors it?

The approach is straightforward. H-three-BERT-a is a RoBERT-a-style transformer with about eighty-six million parameters, pretrained on approximately eighteen million C-D-R-H-three sequences from the Observed Antibody Space database — I-g-G and I-g-A isotypes from healthy, unvaccinated donors. Training runs for one hundred and thirteen epochs with a final masked-language-modeling loss of one-point-five-eight. The comparison model is AntiBERT-a-two, which is pretrained on full-length heavy-chain variable regions and is roughly the reference antibody language model to beat on this kind of task.

The results are informative on multiple axes. First, when you project H-three-BERT-a embeddings on U-MAP, the dominant structure is J-gene usage — meaning the model has learned that the C-terminal residues of C-D-R-H-three carry the I-G-H-J segment identity, and this organizes the latent space. AntiBERT-a-two, in contrast, is dominated by V-gene clustering, because it has access to the framework where V-gene identity is encoded. Second, when you apply linear discriminant analysis to distinguish naive from memory B cells, both models yield strongly bimodal separations — the C-D-R-H-three alone carries enough of the maturation signal that a linear classifier can tell them apart. Third, the H-three-BERT-a embeddings support antigen-binding discrimination on immunized alpaca sequences targeting SARS-CoV-two spike, human interleukin-six, and human tumor necrosis factor alpha, with comparable separability to the full-length model. The interpretation is that even though framework residues carry species and V-gene signal, the C-D-R-H-three itself is where the antigen-recognition regularities are concentrated, and a model that focuses only on that region can pick them up.

The most useful downstream result is the broadly neutralizing antibody classifier. Using a hand-labeled dataset of three hundred and ninety C-D-R-H-three sequences — half HIV-one bnAbs from the C-A-T-N-A-P database, half non-neutralizers with a mix of HIV-infected donors and hard negatives from healthy repertoires that share high sequence similarity to known bnAbs — they train a linear-kernel support vector machine on frozen H-three-BERT-a embeddings. On the independent test set, that S-V-M hits ninety-three percent accuracy with a macro-F-one of zero-point-nine-two. A semi-supervised extension using a G-A-N-BERT framework — which lets you exploit unlabeled sequences from HIV-positive repertoires — gets similar classification numbers but produces a more structured latent space, which the authors argue is useful for downstream sequence-level exploration. Applied blind to a full B cell repertoire from an HIV-one donor known to produce bnAbs, both classifiers correctly identify the experimentally validated bnAb and two other neutralizers, and place them inside the high-confidence bnAb region of latent space.

There is also a repertoire-level analysis worth calling out. When you compute pseudo-perplexity — a per-sequence plausibility score under the model — on the four-point-four million healthy-donor sequences and the four-point-two million bnAb-donor sequences from a published cohort, the aggregate distributions look nearly identical in the center. But the tails differ. In the upper tail, healthy repertoires have higher pseudo-perplexity than bnAb repertoires by a statistically robust margin; the lower tail differs in the opposite direction. Low pseudo-perplexity sequences are enriched in longer loops with tyrosine, consistent with affinity-matured contact chemistry; high pseudo-perplexity sequences are shorter and leucine-rich. So the model is not just discriminating individual sequences — it is picking up distribution-level shifts that correlate with chronic-antigen selection pressure on the repertoire. Whether that generalizes to other chronic-infection contexts, or to the vaccine-elicited setting, is an open question the paper flags but does not resolve.

The take-home for antibody-engineering practice is that scope of input matters more than parameter count for at least some repertoire tasks. H-three-BERT-a at eighty-six million parameters trained on eighteen million loops matches or nearly matches AntiBERT-a-two on downstream tasks. This is directly relevant if you are building a repertoire-mining pipeline for bnAb discovery: instead of running a full-variable-region model over millions of sequences, you can operate at C-D-R-H-three scope and get comparable functional signal at a fraction of the compute. And because C-D-R-H-three is the least species-specific region, the same encoder ports across human, mouse, and alpaca repertoires without species-specific adaptation.

Item two, staying in the antibody-representation lane. The paper is "Frozen protein foundation-model embeddings improve antibody-antigen delta-delta-G ranking," posted to bioRxiv on July fourteenth by Wang, Jin, and Pan, all at Ainnocence, Inc. — the authors note this as a competing interest, since the model they evaluate is a commercial product. The scientific point is worth reporting anyway because it lands in the middle of an active debate about when transfer from general-purpose protein language models to antibody-specific tasks pays off. The task is affinity maturation as a ranking problem: given a wild-type antibody-antigen pair and a set of point mutations in the antibody, rank the mutations by their predicted change in binding free energy — the delta-delta-G. This is a canonical antibody-engineering evaluation because affinity maturation, in practice, is exactly the problem of picking which mutations to test experimentally.

The design is a head-to-head under identical five-fold cross-validation. The from-scratch baseline is a task-specific sequence model trained end-to-end on the delta-delta-G dataset. The comparison is a lightweight downstream head — including a regularized linear probe — trained on top of the frozen embeddings from AINN-P-one, an autoregressively trained protein foundation model. Two headline numbers. First, the regularized linear probe alone, with no fine-tuning of the foundation model, already beats the fully trained from-scratch model. Second, an optimized lightweight head raises the mean Spearman rank correlation from zero-point-four-two to zero-point-five-three — a roughly twenty-eight-percent relative improvement — while training in seconds. The interpretation the authors put on this is worth taking seriously: because the linear probe already exceeds the end-to-end baseline, the improvement is not from downstream-model capacity, it is from representation quality. In other words, a general protein foundation model, without any antibody-specific fine-tuning and without any exposure to delta-delta-G labels during pretraining, has picked up a representation in which affinity-maturation rankings are approximately linear.

Two caveats. The Spearman of zero-point-five-three is a moderate rank correlation, not a strong one — this is a starting point for downstream methods, not a solved problem. And the paper does not fine-tune the foundation model on the delta-delta-G task; the authors explicitly frame frozen-embedding performance as a lower bound that task-adaptive fine-tuning is expected to exceed. Still, the fact that a linear probe on frozen embeddings beats an end-to-end trained model matters for practice: if you have a modest delta-delta-G dataset and you were about to train a task-specific network from scratch, a linear head on a foundation-model embedding is probably a better default.

Item three moves from sequence-level engineering to developability, and specifically to formulation. The paper is titled "Automation and active learning for the multi-objective optimization of antibody formulations," in Advanced Science, published on July thirteenth. Antibody drug products are almost always concentrated liquid formulations, and the excipient stack — the buffers, sugars, amino acids, surfactants added to keep the antibody stable — is where a lot of C-M-C engineering effort goes. Excipients interact with each other and with the antibody in non-linear ways: a stabilizer for one target might reduce solubility for another, and viscosity at high concentration is notoriously hard to predict. So formulation development is traditionally trial-and-error over a large multidimensional design space, and it consumes both time and material.

The paper reports a closed-loop design-build-test-learn pipeline. The model is a machine-learning predictor of formulation behavior — trained on multiple objectives simultaneously: melting temperature as a stability proxy, translational diffusivity, and high-concentration viscosity. The build side is an automation-assisted formulation pipeline that can produce dozens of uniquely formulated antibody solutions on demand, each with a specified excipient composition. The test side is downstream biophysical characterization on each formulation. And the learn side is that the results feed back into the model, and the model designs the next round of formulations. The excipients are restricted to the G-R-A-S set — the generally-recognized-as-safe list — so the pipeline is producing compositions that could in principle move into clinical-formulation development without additional regulatory work on the additives.

The result the authors emphasize is that the loop converges on both improved formulations and accurate predictive models across multiple objectives. They demonstrate that the pipeline maps the underlying property-function landscape rather than just optimizing point solutions — meaning the trained models generalize to unseen compositions, not just recall the ones they were trained on. For antibody engineers who work upstream on sequence design, this is worth noting because it operationalizes developability as an active-learning problem, not just a static prediction task. And it draws a line between the two: the model does not need to be gigantic — it needs to be integrated with automated experimentation and multi-objective decision-making. Extending this kind of loop to sequence-level developability optimization, where the excipient stack is fixed and the antibody sequence is variable, is a natural next step.

Item four moves from single-antibody engineering to multi-antibody therapy design. The paper is "Computer-guided dosing of multi-specific T-cell engagers," in Communications Biology, published on July thirteenth, from the Gambles group at the University of Utah. The clinical problem is that T-cell engager therapies — bispecific and multispecific antibody-like constructs that link tumor antigens to T-cell surface markers to redirect T-cell killing — are hitting a hard ceiling from patient-specific tumor heterogeneity. A dose that clears one patient's tumor may leave another patient with residual disease if the antigen expression profile is different, or may exhaust T-cells if the tumor burden and T-cell counts are wrong for the dose. Multi-specific formulations against multiple tumor antigens simultaneously are a plausible answer, but the dose-composition space explodes combinatorially, and picking the right composition for a specific patient is not something you can do by trial and error at the bedside.

The paper presents two coupled things. First, M-A-T-C-H, a modular split-antibody T-cell engager platform that lets you assemble multi-specific engagers targeting arbitrary combinations of tumor antigens — the authors demonstrate it with C-D-nineteen, C-D-twenty, and C-D-thirty-eight, which cover B-cell malignancies. Second, C-O-M-P-A-S-S, a computational framework that takes patient-specific inputs — T-cell counts, tumor burden, and antigen expression across the target set — and predicts a dose composition of multi-specific therapies that is expected to maximize tumor killing while preserving T-cell viability. The optimization is against a joint objective that treats T-cell exhaustion as a hard cost rather than an afterthought.

The validation is two-tier. On patient P-B-M-C samples from chronic lymphocytic leukemia, C-O-M-P-A-S-S predictions match effective multi-specific formulations. In a human lymphoma xenograft mouse model, C-O-M-P-A-S-S-predicted compositions produced significantly improved survival relative to alternatives. The authors frame the pipeline as a lab-in-the-loop workflow: an experimental measurement of patient antigen expression and T-cell state goes in, a computational prediction of a personalized multi-specific composition comes out, and the loop is closed by validating the composition against that patient's cells before administration. For antibody engineers who work upstream on the bispecific-and-multispecific design side, the interesting handoff is that a computational framework at the dose-composition layer changes the relative value of individual constructs. The engineer's job is no longer to find the single best construct but to produce a menu of composable specificities that C-O-M-P-A-S-S can mix at the point of care.

Item five is a de novo binder-design paper against an intrinsically disordered target — a harder case than the standard folded-antigen design problem, and directly relevant to anyone thinking about how the R-F-diffusion family of tools generalizes beyond well-behaved targets. The paper is "Potent substoichiometric inhibition of alpha-synuclein aggregation by de novo oligomer-binding proteins," bioRxiv July fourteenth, from the Sahtoe and Knowles groups. Alpha-synuclein is the protein whose misfolding drives Parkinson's disease; the aggregation-driving segment is the N-A-C domain, which is intrinsically disordered as a monomer, and therefore lacks a stable epitope for conventional structure-based ligand design. What the authors exploit is that the N-A-C domain preferentially adopts an extended beta-strand conformation when it engages other structural elements — so if you design a binder that presents a matching beta-strand-recognition scaffold, you can catch the disordered segment in a specific conformer and stabilize it.

They generate compact single-chain binders using deep-learning-based protein design, express twenty-one designs, and find that three engage the target both in vitro and in live cells. Then they do one round of partial diffusion — that is, they take the initial designs, partially noise them in the diffusion model's latent space, and re-denoise conditioning on the target — as an affinity-maturation step. The best design after partial diffusion binds with a dissociation constant of one-point-nine-four nanomolar, with no detectable cross-reactivity to tau, amylin, or amyloid-beta. Solution N-M-R confirms that the alpha-synuclein peptide associates with the binder and that binding induces conformational changes consistent with the intended architecture.

The functional result is the interesting one. The three binders each engage a distinct point on the aggregation pathway — monomer, on-pathway oligomer, or fibril. Kinetic modeling combining seeded aggregation experiments with the measured monomer, oligomer, and fibril affinities resolves exactly which microscopic step each binder inhibits. The most potent binder acts by selectively capturing on-pathway oligomers, which is the species most closely tied to neurotoxicity, and it suppresses fibril formation at substoichiometric ratios without engaging the bulk monomer pool. That is the design win: not just affinity, but the ability to place the binder against a specific transient assembly state and get functional selectivity out of that placement. For antibody engineers, the interesting transferable lesson is that partial diffusion works as an affinity-maturation operator — you do not need a full redesign cycle to get from a functional-but-modest binder to a nanomolar one, and this parallels what R-F-antibody-family tools are doing for antibody complementarity-determining regions.
