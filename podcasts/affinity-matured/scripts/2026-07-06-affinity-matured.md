## Script

Welcome to Affinity Matured, your daily brief on machine learning for antibody and protein design. Four items today: a longitudinal look at how the healthy human antibody repertoire drifts over four years, with clear implications for repertoire-trained language models; a frustration-guided way to subsample multiple sequence alignments that pushes structure predictors into alternative conformations; a deep-learning framework that predicts which human leukocyte antigen restricts a T-cell receptor from sequence alone; and a fusion architecture that pairs a protein language model with a residue graph for cell-penetrating peptide prediction, mostly interesting for what it says about how these hybrids are converging.

First item, and the lead today. Scientific Reports on July second published a paper out of the Briney and Burton labs at Scripps — "Longitudinal analysis of drift in the circulating human antibody repertoire over four years," by Collin Joyce and Benjamin Nemoz as co-first authors, with Raiza Bastidas, Bryan Briney, and Dennis Burton. This is a follow-up to the group's landmark 2019 study which used deep repertoire sequencing on ten healthy donors to estimate that the accessible human antibody repertoire has on the order of ten to the fifteen to ten to the eighteen distinct members. That number is often quoted; less discussed is whether that repertoire is stable in an individual over time, and by how much. The new paper resamples two of those original donors approximately four years later and asks exactly that.

The high-level answer is: the gross features are stable, the fine features are not. Overall repertoire size, overall diversity, V-gene and J-gene usage frequencies, and the length distribution of the heavy-chain third complementarity-determining region are all conserved across the four-year gap. Those are the summary statistics you would use to describe the repertoire on a poster. What changes substantially is the fine-grained composition: the specific combinations of V, J, and D gene segments, and the P and N junctional nucleotide sequences that get added during V-D-J recombination. In other words, if you zoom in past marginal distributions to joint distributions and junctional details, the four-year-later repertoire is a different draw.

Why this matters for our audience. Antibody language models trained on repertoire data — think Ab-Lang, AntiBERTy, I-g-L-M, and the p-I-g-Gen family — implicitly assume that a training set assembled from cross-sectional donor cohorts is exchangeable with the population you will later query. This paper says that assumption holds for marginals but breaks for joint statistics involving junctional composition and gene-segment combinations. If your downstream task is naïve repertoire generation, primary response prediction, or repertoire drift itself, that mismatch matters. It also means that when you evaluate a repertoire model on a held-out donor collected at a different time point, part of what you are measuring is population drift, not just model generalization. The paper does not offer a numeric divergence score for the drifting fine features — it argues the qualitative point from two donors — and the authors themselves note that primary responses to newly encountered pathogens and to vaccination may be influenced by exactly this drift. Concrete follow-up work for the field is clear: retrain a repertoire model on time-matched samples and see whether the assumption of stationarity in the naïve compartment is quietly hurting performance.

Second item. On the arXiv preprint server on June thirtieth, a group from the Chinese University of Hong Kong and the University of Illinois, in collaboration with Pranam Chatterjee at the University of Pennsylvania, released S-F-Cluster — frustration-guided multiple sequence alignment subsampling for alternative protein conformation recovery. Accepted for oral at the ICML AI-for-Science workshop. The motivation is a familiar problem: deep-learning structure predictors like AlphaFold are sensitive to their multiple sequence alignment input, and the earlier AF-Cluster method showed that clustering the alignment in sequence space and running the predictor on subsets can expose alternative conformations — fold-switching, allosteric, and so on. S-F-Cluster changes what you cluster on. Instead of sequence similarity it uses patterns of predicted local energetic frustration — a per-residue score of how much competing forces the local sequence context imposes on the fold — as the representation that drives which sequences group together.

The benchmark is forty-eight cases spanning fold-switching, allosteric, oligomerization-coupled, and intrinsically disordered systems, scored with an AF-Cluster-style dual-reference r-m-s-d criterion for recovering the alternative state. S-F-Cluster improves target-state recovery over AF-Cluster across the two-state classes; the biggest jump is on allosteric systems, plus fifteen and a half percentage points. Two mechanistic details make this more than a benchmarking exercise. One, the frustration-selected alignments transfer to an architecturally different structure predictor — the conformational signal lives in the alignment composition, not in a predictor-specific interaction. Two, matched-depth controls suggest most of the recovery advantage comes from the effective depth of the selected subset — frustration-pattern selection reliably reaches depths that recover the alternative state, while random subsampling at matched depth mostly does not. Separately, they show that highly frustrated residues are enriched at sites already flagged by deep mutational scanning and N-M-R two-state exchange, and that frustration covariation is enriched at state-switching contacts but remains distinct from coevolutionary coupling.

Two things to note for our purposes. First, the paper does not include an antibody-specific case in the benchmark, but the mechanism — antibody complementarity-determining regions, especially C-D-R-H-three, are conformationally heterogeneous, and AlphaFold and its cousins famously collapse that ensemble into a single low-frustration guess — makes this a natural tool to try on paratope loops when you want the predicted ensemble rather than the most-likely state. Second, it is a clean example of a broader trend: representation choice, not architecture choice, is what unlocks conformational diversity from these models.

Third item, back to biorxiv on July fourth. Nicholas Rose and colleagues, from Vanessa Jonsson's group at U-C Santa Cruz — the same lab that gave us PRISM yesterday — posted TRIOPS, a deep-learning framework for predicting T-cell receptor to M-H-C binding specificity. T-cell receptor recognition is M-H-C-restricted, meaning a receptor sees peptide only in the context of a particular human leukocyte antigen allele, but assigning that restricting allele from receptor sequence alone has been an open problem — the labels are noisy at allele boundaries and public data over-represents a small subset of H-L-A alleles. TRIOPS is a dual-branch convolutional network with a soft cross-attention that fuses the T-cell receptor sequence branch with an allele branch. The key trick is cross-reactivity-aware negative sampling: they generate negative training pairs based on H-L-A pseudosequence similarity, which reduces label noise at the allele boundary and lets the model generalize to alleles that were not in the training set at all.

Held-out area under the curve is zero point nine seven for paired-chain T-cell receptor inputs and zero point nine two for single-chain, and after locus-specific calibration, TRIOPS can assign a given receptor clonotype to its likeliest restricting allele across an individual's H-L-A genotype. The number that stood out for me is what happens when they apply this to T-C-G-A tumor samples. Using only bulk T-cell receptor repertoire and bulk R-N-A sequencing, they find that in tumors with allele-specific H-L-A loss the T-cell receptor repertoire preferentially engages the allele that got expression-lost at H-L-A-A and H-L-A-B, and the retained allele at H-L-A-C. That recapitulates a known immune-escape pattern — H-L-A-A and B down-regulate under immune pressure while H-L-A-C is preserved because it is an inhibitory ligand for natural killer cells — from bulk sequencing alone, without single-cell resolution. For the audience: the cross-reactivity-aware negative sampling strategy is a general recipe. If you are training an antibody-antigen binder classifier and your labels have boundary noise across closely related epitopes or M-H-C haplotypes, weighting negatives by structural or pseudosequence proximity is exactly the trick to borrow.

Fourth and final item. In I-E-E-E Journal of Biomedical and Health Informatics, published July second, a group led by Jian Guan and colleagues describes DeepCPP, a dual-branch framework for predicting cell-penetrating peptides. This one is only tangentially about antibodies, but the architecture pattern is worth flagging because it is now the default recipe in this area. One branch is an E-S-M-two protein language model providing context-aware embeddings. The other branch is a multi-view residue graph — nodes are residues, edges encode local sequence context and residue-wise physicochemical similarity — aggregated with a top-k subgraph focusing operation and a gated broadcast. The two views are fused with cross-attention regularized by an H-S-I-C decorrelation term to push the two branches to encode complementary rather than redundant information. On top sits a Kolmogorov–Arnold Network head instead of the usual multi-layer perceptron.

Their reported result is state of the art on a re-curated CPPsite three benchmark under cluster-controlled splits, which are the ones you should trust. Interpretability analyses recover charge clustering and terminal residue patterns and amphipathic arrangements — the biophysical picture the peptide-design community has known for years — which reads as a sanity check on the learned representations rather than a new biological finding. The reason I flag this for the antibody-language-model audience: this exact fusion recipe — protein language model plus residue graph plus cross-attention with an information-theoretic decorrelation term — is showing up over and over now in paratope prediction, antibody-antigen binding, and developability prediction papers. The Kolmogorov-Arnold Network head is the newer wrinkle, and it is worth tracking whether it actually adds anything on antibody tasks or is just a stylistic choice.

That is the day. A repertoire-drift result that should make anyone training antibody language models look at their donor timepoints; a frustration-guided alignment trick that gives structure predictors access to alternative conformations, with obvious potential for complementarity-determining-region ensembles; a T-cell receptor to human leukocyte antigen prediction framework whose negative-sampling strategy generalizes cleanly to any receptor-antigen classification with noisy allele boundaries; and a fusion architecture that is quietly becoming the field's default for sequence-plus-graph tasks.
