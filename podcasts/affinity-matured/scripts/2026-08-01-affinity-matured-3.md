## Script

Good evening. Third and last edition of Affinity Matured for Saturday, August first. This one keeps the design-and-generation thread of the morning bioRxiv batch and the peer-reviewed afternoon episode, but points at three different levels of the AI-for-protein stack. First, from the Correia lab at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne, a proteome-scale atlas of designed protein binder candidates — three hundred and six thousand computationally designed binders against about forty-one percent of the human proteome, released as a searchable database with structural models and machine-learning-ready splits. Second, from a Purdue-Vanderbilt group, a new few-step generative model for protein backbones that pushes designable-sample generation from the standard hundred-plus network evaluations down toward twenty and even fewer, working natively in the geometry of rigid-body frames rather than through a Euclidean approximation. And third, from the Georgakopoulos-Soares lab at the University of Texas at Austin, a straightforward and immediately useful engineering paper — speculative decoding applied to the two most-used protein language model generators, ProGen-two and Prot-G-P-T-two, delivering one-point-two to two-times inference speedup with no change in the target model's sampling distribution.

First item. bioRxiv, posted July thirtieth, "The Human Bindome: A Proteome-scale Atlas of Designed Binder Candidates," from Bruno Correia's lab at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology in Lausanne with collaborators at ETH Zurich, the Technical University of Munich, the Centre for Genomic Regulation in Barcelona, the University of Toronto, the AITHYRA Research Institute in Vienna, and the Protein Data Bank in Europe at E-M-B-L-E-B-I. This is a landmark resource paper for anyone working on AI-designed affinity reagents. The premise is straightforward. Deep-learning-based binder-design methods — BindCraft, Bolts-Gen, R-F-diffusion, Proteina-Complex — can now generate high-affinity binders from a target structure with experimentally validated success rates above ten percent, sometimes higher, but running these pipelines still requires specialist expertise and substantial compute. So most biologists cannot get a custom binder against the target they care about. The paper's contribution is to precompute the entire atlas: three hundred and six thousand one hundred and forty-six binder candidates against structured domains of eight thousand two hundred and ninety-six human proteins, which is forty-point-nine percent of the full human proteome or seventy-seven-point-eight percent of the domain-level targetable proteome as they define it.

The engineering to get there is nontrivial. They start from the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database — the version six release with full-length human structural models and Predicted Aligned Error matrices — and use graph-based clustering on the P-A-E matrices to segment each full-length protein into structural domains. A domain is retained as a design target only if it satisfies a set of criteria: mean predicted local structure quality score above zero-point-seven, length between thirty and five hundred fifty residues, a normalized radius of gyration below five, and a per-residue contact density above two. Membrane-embedded regions and single-helix bundles are dropped. Then, for each retained domain, they run BindCraft — the recently benchmarked back-propagation-through-AlphaFold binder-design pipeline — in a parallelized S-L-U-R-M array with two acceleration tricks: fixing binder length to ninety amino acids to avoid recompiling the ColabFold class instances of AlphaFold and ProteinMPNN across runs, and cropping the design target to the domain rather than the full protein to keep the search space small. Every domain-level design is then superimposed onto the full-length target structure and rejected if the designed binder clashes with structured regions outside the target domain — a critical filter because domain-cropped design is blind to the rest of the protein. Each surviving candidate carries the BindCraft filters: monomer confidence above zero-point-eight and normalized interface Predicted Aligned Error below zero-point-three-five. The final atlas has a median monomer confidence of zero-point-nine-two and a median interface P-A-E of zero-point-two-three, so the distributions are not skewed toward the acceptance thresholds — the candidates that made it are well inside the confidence envelope.

Now the useful analysis for anyone considering downstream deployment. Coverage is measured in three orthogonal ways. By target count, the Bindome is the second-largest human-proteome affinity resource after Antibodypedia — Antibodypedia has more targets, ninety-two percent, but its listings are commercial monoclonals with poorly characterized sequences and no structural data. Every candidate in the Bindome comes with a defined sequence, a predicted binder-target complex structure, and in-silico confidence metrics. By structural family, seventy-eight percent of C-A-T-H superfamilies represented in the human target-domain set have at least one binder candidate. But by proteome surface area, only thirteen-point-nine percent of the modeled surface is contacted — sixty-four-point-five percent is targetable under the current design scope, so per-target epitope coverage is where the atlas is thin. The authors are frank about this: target coverage is high, epitope coverage is low, and closing the epitope-coverage gap is a real methodological gap. Native protein-protein interfaces in the P-I-N-D-E-R dataset have a diverse repertoire of secondary-structure pairings; Bindome interfaces are strongly biased toward helix-on-helix and helix-on-loop contacts, which is a well-known bias of the current generation of BindCraft and R-F-diffusion outputs.

Two findings from the epitope analysis are worth noting for the antibody-engineering audience. First, epitopes preferentially engage conserved surface residues. They defined the epitope of a target at three consensus thresholds — residues contacted by any binder, residues contacted by at least thirty percent of a target's binders, and residues contacted by at least ninety percent of a target's binders — and at every threshold, epitope residues were significantly more conserved than a residue-count-matched random surface control. Conservation increased with consensus: the more binders converged on a set of residues, the more conserved they tended to be. Second, epitopes frequently overlap with annotated functional sites. Twenty-six percent of proteins with annotated allosteric sites in the Allosteric Database have at least one binder candidate whose epitope overlaps the allosteric site. Roughly half of homomeric and heteromeric protein-protein interfaces annotated in P-I-N-D-E-R overlap with at least one binder epitope. Post-translational modification sites — phosphorylation and acetylation being the largest categories — and metal-ion and nucleotide binding sites are also commonly hit by binder epitopes. So the atlas isn't just a coverage exercise: the binders are landing on functionally important patches, which is exactly what you'd want for a genetically encodable perturbagen.

The access story is the third contribution and probably the most immediately useful for anyone at the bench. The Bindome is at bindome-dot-e-p-f-l-dot-c-h with three access modes: an interactive web interface for browsing by UniProt accession, a standardized 3D-Beacons application programming interface for pipeline integration, and a Model Context Protocol server that lets a large language model query the database in natural language and iterate over the results. The full dataset is also on Hugging Face at wjulius slash HumanBindome as leakage-controlled train, validation, and test splits — two hundred and five thousand training complexes, twenty-one thousand validation complexes, and twenty-two thousand test complexes, clustered by interface to prevent train-test leakage. That's a substantial and well-curated distillation dataset for anyone training their next generation of binder-design models. The competing databases that give sequences — A-B-C-D at seven percent coverage, S-Ab-Dab at two-point-four percent — are dwarfed by this.

What the paper deliberately does not do is experimental validation of the atlas. The underlying BindCraft pipeline has been experimentally validated with success rates ranging from ten percent to one hundred percent depending on target, so the atlas inherits those priors — but this specific set of three hundred thousand candidates has not been tested at the bench. The authors are explicit about two selectivity concerns worth calling out: cross-reactivity with unrelated proteins in the crowded cellular environment, and cross-reactivity among paralogs — the latter is a real risk because the atlas preferentially engages conserved epitopes, and conserved epitopes are exactly where paralog overlap concentrates. For the antibody-engineering audience specifically, the atlas is not a substitute for a de novo antibody design campaign — the ninety-residue helix-biased binder format is not an immunoglobulin — but it is a proteome-scale prior over where designable binding modes cluster on each human protein, and the accompanying distillation dataset is a genuinely large training corpus for interface prediction and binder generation models that could feed the next round of antibody-specific design work.

Second item. arXiv, posted July twenty-ninth, "S-E-three-MeanFlow: Few-Step Protein Backbone Generation on Lie Groups," from Yikun Bai and Ruqi Zhang's groups at Purdue with Soheil Kolouri's group at Vanderbilt. This is a generative-modeling-methods paper aimed squarely at the practical bottleneck in generative protein-design pipelines: inference speed. Current diffusion and flow-matching methods for protein backbones — FrameDiff, R-F-diffusion, FoldFlow, FrameFlow, Q-Flow, Re-Q-Flow — produce high-quality backbones, but every sample requires numerically integrating an ordinary differential equation on the product of rigid-body-frame groups, which is a hundred to five hundred network evaluations per sample. On the special Euclidean group in three dimensions, each of those evaluations involves not just a forward pass through the network but also a matrix exponential map from the Lie algebra back to the group, so the wall-clock cost per sample is materially higher than in Euclidean generative models. In a high-throughput design campaign where you want millions of candidates screened, that hundred-to-five-hundred-step inference is the wall.

The MeanFlow framework, introduced earlier this year in Euclidean space by Geng and colleagues, is one solution. Standard flow matching learns the instantaneous velocity of the flow at time-t; MeanFlow instead learns the average velocity of the flow between times s and t, which turns the integration inside the loss and yields a training objective that supervises only on instantaneous velocity but produces a network you can evaluate at inference in one forward pass to get from noise to sample. That's the trick. The problem is that on a curved manifold like the special Euclidean group, velocities at different points along a trajectory live in different tangent spaces, and averaging them is not well-defined without parallel transport. A previous paper, Riemannian MeanFlow with parallel transport, addressed this at full generality with a Riemannian derivation, but did not exploit the specific algebraic structure of S-E-three as a Lie group.

The Purdue-Vanderbilt paper's central contribution is to exploit that structure. They observe that the special Euclidean group in three dimensions decomposes as the product of the special orthogonal group in three dimensions with the three-dimensional translation group — rotations and translations, treated separately. This decomposition lets them derive the MeanFlow identity in closed form in the Lie algebra of the rotation part and in the flat Euclidean translation part independently, without parallel transport. That is, average velocity has a clean, tractable expression in each component, and the training targets can be computed in a simulation-free manner analogous to standard MeanFlow in Euclidean space. They also introduce an alpha-Flow warm-up objective that removes the Jacobian-vector product from the rotation branch during early training, which stabilizes the notoriously fragile MeanFlow objective on rotations before switching to the full small-t stabilized MeanFlow loss for the remainder of pretraining. There's a rectification-based post-training stage on top of that, following the Re-Q-Flow recipe.

Now the numerical results, which are the reason to care. Benchmark is the S-C-O-P-e dataset of thirty-six-seventy-three preprocessed protein backbones with residue lengths from sixty to one hundred and twenty-eight, evaluated by standard designability — the fraction of generated backbones whose self-consistency root-mean-square deviation to a re-designed sequence and re-predicted structure falls below two angstroms — plus diversity and novelty metrics from prior work. At one hundred sampling steps, the rectified S-E-three-MeanFlow model reaches designability of ninety-three-point-six percent, compared to eighty-eight-point-five percent for Q-Flow at the same one hundred steps and eighty-four-point-nine percent for FrameFlow at five hundred steps. So even at high budget, this model beats the current best. The interesting result is what happens as you cut steps. At fifty steps, rectified S-E-three-MeanFlow is at ninety-point-six percent designability while Q-Flow drops to eighty-seven-point-zero percent and Riemannian MeanFlow is at eighty-two-point-five percent. At twenty steps, S-E-three-MeanFlow holds at eighty-six-point-seven percent while Q-Flow drops to seventy-seven-point-eight percent. At ten steps — this is where things really diverge — Q-Flow drops to fifty-five-point-nine percent designability, Riemannian MeanFlow is at seventy-seven-point-eight percent, and S-E-three-MeanFlow is at seventy-two-point-eight percent. So the reduction from a hundred to twenty inference steps costs about seven percentage points of designability, and even at ten steps the sample quality remains usable, which for a large-scale generative pipeline is the practical win.

The trade-off is diversity. On the diversity-T-M-score metric — lower is better since it means samples are more structurally distinct from each other — S-E-three-MeanFlow at one hundred steps is at zero-point-four-one-five, meaningfully higher than Q-Flow's zero-point-three-nine-three and FrameFlow's zero-point-three-six-nine. So the model is producing higher-designability samples but from a somewhat tighter mode-covering distribution. The authors are upfront about this — they call it "a modest cost in diversity" — and for antibody-adjacent applications where you want a strong candidate at every generation and are downstream-filtering aggressively anyway, that trade-off is probably the right one.

For the antibody-engineering audience the specific impact is downstream. This is not an antibody generator — it's an unconditional protein backbone generator trained on S-C-O-P-e — but the methodology carries directly to antibody-frame generation. If you're building or fine-tuning an antibody backbone generator on top of a flow-matching baseline, the S-E-three-MeanFlow objective is a drop-in acceleration that would let you go from a hundred inference steps per antibody backbone to twenty and keep most of the designability. In a screening pipeline where you're generating tens of thousands of scaffolds per campaign and downstream-filtering by AlphaFold-three or Boltz-three, cutting inference cost by five-times without moving the designability window is a real production win. The code is on the paper's project page and the underlying implementation lives in the Q-Flow/Re-Q-Flow codebase, so adoption is straightforward.

Third item. Bioinformatics, published online July thirty-first, "Accelerating inference in genomic and proteomic foundation models via speculative decoding," from Ilias Georgakopoulos-Soares' group at the University of Texas at Austin with collaborators at B-S-R-C Alexander Fleming in Greece and the Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence in the United Arab Emirates. Peer-reviewed, journal-published, and immediately usable — the code is at github-dot-com slash Georgakopoulos-Soares-lab slash BioSpecDec. This is a small paper compared to the previous two but it is directly deployable for anyone running protein language models in production.

The problem is inference latency in autoregressive protein language model generation. ProGen-two and Prot-G-P-T-two are decoder-only transformer language models over amino-acid alphabets that can generate novel protein sequences token-by-token — every token requires one forward pass through a large transformer, and for long-sequence generation the compute quickly dominates. Speculative decoding is a well-known technique from the natural-language-model literature — introduced by Leviathan and colleagues at Google and refined by many groups since — for accelerating autoregressive inference without changing the output distribution. The idea is: run a lightweight draft model to propose a short span of tokens, then use the large target model to verify the whole span in a single forward pass, accepting the tokens where draft and target agree and correcting the first disagreement. The verification step recovers the target model's sampling distribution exactly — this is a lossless speedup at the token distribution level.

The Georgakopoulos-Soares group implements a probabilistic variant of speculative decoding for three foundation models: D-N-A-G-P-T for genomic sequences and ProGen-two plus Prot-G-P-T-two for protein sequences. They systematically vary four factors — speculation window length, sampling temperature, draft-model architecture, and prompt length — and benchmark tokens-per-second on multiple runs per configuration. Results are consistent across all three models: speculative decoding delivers reliable speedups over standard key-value-cached autoregressive decoding, with maximum observed speedup around a two-times acceleration and average speedups across configurations in the one-point-two to one-point-four-times range — roughly twenty to forty percent throughput gains. Critically, the target model's output distribution is unchanged. If you were running ProGen-two at temperature zero-point-eight for antibody-C-D-R-H-three enumeration, the sequences you now get after adding speculative decoding are statistically indistinguishable from the ones you'd have gotten before, just cheaper to produce.

The paper's utility for antibody-engineering listeners is direct. If your pipeline generates candidate antibody sequences via a Progen or Prot-G-P-T-style autoregressive model — either as a front-end sequence proposer or as part of a mask-infilling loop — this is a drop-in inference acceleration with essentially no cost to output quality. The BioSpecDec repository has the three foundation models pre-integrated. For a single-run experiment the twenty-to-forty-percent gain is not transformative, but for a repeat-sampling design loop that runs a fixed model over millions of prompts, it adds up quickly. The paper does not extend to antibody-specific models like AbLang or p-I-g-Gen — those would need their own draft models trained — but the technique applies mechanically to any autoregressive protein language model, and the implementation blueprint is now public.
