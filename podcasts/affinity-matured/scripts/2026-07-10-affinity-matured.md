**First: IgGM2 — an all-atom foundation model for adaptive immune receptor design.**

Most published methods for antibody CDR design separate the problem into two stages: first predict or assume a fixed protein backbone, then design the CDR sequence onto that backbone using an inverse folding model. The fixed-backbone assumption is computationally convenient but physically questionable. Antibody CDR loops — especially the third heavy-chain loop, which is the main driver of antigen specificity in most human antibodies — are structurally plastic. They rearrange when their sequences change. A design method that fixes the backbone before choosing the sequence risks proposing residues that would adopt a different geometry than the one you scored, and the wet-lab validation will tell you what the calculation missed.

IgGM2, posted yesterday to bioRxiv from Jian Ma and colleagues at Shanghai Jiao Tong University and related groups, takes a structurally integrated approach. It is an all-atom generative framework that jointly models CDR sequence identities and full-atom receptor structures, adapting both in the same generative step rather than treating them as independent problems. The authors describe a structure-to-design strategy: the model first learns how immune receptors as a class are positioned around antigen target structures, establishing a target-conditioned structural prior for the full receptor geometry including the CDR and framework regions. It then uses that learned prior to guide CDR design, so that the backbone is not fixed — it is generated together with the sequence in a coupled process that lets framework geometry respond to whatever CDRs the model proposes.

The all-atom representation uses atom-14 placeholders, the standard compact format for full sidechain geometry that handles residue-specific atom counts in a fixed-width encoding. Keeping sequence prediction explicit — as discrete residue identity outputs rather than as a geometric coordinate to be decoded — is a deliberate design choice that makes the sequence prediction interpretable and checkable, as opposed to a geometric latent that needs further decoding.

The model is trained and evaluated across three receptor classes simultaneously: conventional antibodies, nanobodies — the single-domain antibody fragments also known as V-H-H domains — and T-cell receptors. These three receptor types share a common structural logic for how their hypervariable loops make contact with target antigens, but they differ enough in loop lengths, canonical structures, and disulfide patterns that a model trained on one class does not trivially generalize to the others. A unified framework trained across all three provides a richer and more diverse structural prior and may eventually support design strategies that draw on structural lessons from, say, nanobody CDR patterns to inform antibody CDR loops that occupy similar topological niches.

On structure prediction, IgGM2 outperforms AlphaFold3 on FoldBench at capturing the spatial positioning of receptors relative to their antigen targets — the receptor-target geometry, not just the receptor structure in isolation. It also demonstrates strong performance on T-cell receptor peptide-MHC modeling, a benchmark where the interface geometry is dominated by the CDR3 loops and where the peptide's structural context within the MHC groove complicates the prediction considerably.

On sequence design benchmarks, IgGM2 improves amino-acid recovery — the fraction of designed CDR positions that match the experimentally characterized native sequence — and improves Rosetta-based interface preference metrics, which estimate whether the designed binding interface has favorable interaction energies. Higher interface scores do not guarantee improved binding affinity, but unfavorable scores are a reliable filter for designs that are likely not to work.

**Second: FlexRibbon — joint sequence-structure pretraining for protein modeling, with strong antibody CDR results.**

The two established paradigms in protein language modeling both have failure modes that are specifically problematic for antibody design. Sequence-only models — E-S-M-2 being the main reference point — are broadly trained and computationally efficient, but they encode only sequence context and lack explicit structural grounding. They can learn correlations that proxy for physical constraints, but they cannot directly represent three-dimensional geometry, and their predictions in highly mutated, alignment-sparse regimes depend on extrapolation from sequence patterns rather than structural reasoning.

Alignment-based structure predictors — AlphaFold2 and AlphaFold3 — overcome the geometry problem by leveraging evolutionary couplings from multiple sequence alignments to achieve very high folding accuracy. But that accuracy is contingent on deep alignment depth. For antibody CDR loops, particularly CDR-H3, the sequences are inherently diverse. That is the biological point of CDRs: they are the product of somatic hypermutation and selection optimized for antigen recognition, not evolutionary conservation. Asking an alignment-based model to predict or generate CDR loops is asking it to work in exactly the regime — shallow, sparse alignments and high sequence diversity — where it has the least signal.

FlexRibbon, posted yesterday to bioRxiv from groups in China as a major revision of an October 2025 preprint, takes a different route. It is a three-billion-parameter protein foundation model that combines masked language modeling on sequences — analogous to BERT-style pretraining in natural language processing — with diffusion-based denoising on three-dimensional structures in a single pretraining objective. Both tasks are applied simultaneously on the same model, so that the learned representation must encode both sequence identity information and structural geometry in the same embedding space rather than in two separate, independently trained components.

The architecture is organized into three stages. A sequence module embeds protein residues and, for protein-ligand applications, small-molecule atoms into a unified representation using a standard transformer with rotary position embeddings and pair-feature updates for residue-residue and residue-atom interactions. A coarse-grained structure module takes those sequence embeddings and applies a diffusion transformer to denoise backbone coordinates at residue resolution — modeling the overall chain fold. An all-atom structure module then refines the coarse-grained coordinates to full sidechain-level geometry, using the coarse structure as a conditioning input and adding atom-type and bond-type attention biases to maintain chemical validity.

This hierarchical coarse-to-fine design directly addresses a practical problem in all-atom protein modeling: representing every sidechain atom explicitly makes the representation expensive, and naively scaling it with model size hits memory limits that have kept prior full-atom models small. By doing coarse-grained denoising first and reserving the all-atom module for refinement, FlexRibbon can allocate model capacity across both levels efficiently. For low-confidence structure regions — common when training on AlphaFold-predicted structures rather than experimental data — an adaptive loss selectively downweights those positions to prevent the model from fitting noise.

The revised preprint evaluates FlexRibbon across twelve tasks in three broad categories: flexible interface prediction and design (including antibody and nanobody CDR loop modeling and peptide binding), intermolecular interaction prediction (protein-ligand docking, ligand-induced conformational change, binding affinity), and protein function prediction (gene ontology and enzyme activity). It reports state-of-the-art performance across all twelve tasks, with the largest reported improvements concentrated in the flexible-interface and mutation-rich regimes — exactly the category that encompasses antibody and nanobody CDR design. The model's training strategy of bypassing multiple sequence alignment dependence means it does not require deep evolutionary context to generate useful representations of highly mutated loop sequences, which is the setting that matters most in an affinity maturation or CDR redesign workflow.

Code and model weights are described as available under an open license, though the specific repository is in the preprint body.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.10.08.681293

**Third: the Dayhoff Atlas — a large-scale open resource for protein language model training.**

The third item today is less a computational method and more an infrastructure paper, but it bears directly on how next-generation protein language models get trained. Kevin Yang and collaborators at Microsoft Research and Absci posted a revised version of the Dayhoff Atlas yesterday — a centralized collection of protein sequence data and pretrained generative language models.

The name is a deliberate callback to Margaret Dayhoff's 1965 Atlas of Protein Sequence and Structure, the foundational database that made comparative protein biology possible before the genomics era. The thesis here is that the databases descended from Dayhoff's work — UniProt and its relatives — now need to be supplemented with a more intentional organization of metagenomic sequence diversity to drive the next generation of protein language models.

On the data side, the authors combine UniRef100 with metagenomic sequences and re-cluster them to produce GigaRef, a dataset of three-point-three-four billion protein sequences across one-point-seven billion sequence clusters. That is the largest open protein sequence dataset currently available. Alongside the natural sequence data, they generate BackboneRef: 46 million synthetic protein sequences predicted from 240,811 de novo designed backbones. The idea behind BackboneRef is to encode structural diversity explicitly rather than relying on sequence diversity to implicitly cover structural space — if you want the model to understand structural context across diverse topologies, you generate sequences conditioned on a diverse set of structures and add them to the training corpus directly.

Using these data, they train the Dayhoff family of protein language models. The notable architectural contribution is the first published model that jointly trains on individual protein sequences and on sets of evolutionarily related sequences — multiple sequence alignments — in the same model at scale. Prior work has trained either on single sequences or on alignments, but not both simultaneously at this scale. Operating entirely in amino acid sequence space, the Dayhoff models support mutation effect prediction, structural motif scaffolding, and guided generation within specific protein families.

The practical evaluation shows that training on metagenomic and structure-conditioned synthetic data increases the cellular expression rates of generated proteins relative to models trained on the standard UniRef distribution. Expression rate is a concrete proxy for whether a generated sequence actually folds and functions — it's not a benchmark metric but a wet-lab measurement of model quality.

All datasets, code, and models are released under a permissive license. For groups training or fine-tuning protein language models — including antibody-specific models — GigaRef and BackboneRef represent a substantially larger and more structurally diverse starting point than the databases that most current antibody language models were trained on.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2025.07.21.665991
