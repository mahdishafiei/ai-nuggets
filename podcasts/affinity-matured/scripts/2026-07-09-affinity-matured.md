**First: E-S-M-2 and AlphaFold-fused sequence-structure model for antibody self-association prediction.**

Developability screening is the unglamorous twin of affinity maturation. An antibody can bind its target with picomolar affinity and still fail in the clinic because it aggregates at the high concentrations needed for subcutaneous delivery, drives formulation viscosity above what a syringe can handle, or clears too quickly to maintain therapeutic levels. Self-association — the tendency of an antibody molecule to stick to copies of itself — sits upstream of all three failure modes. It is also one of the harder biophysical properties to predict computationally, because it arises from patches of hydrophobicity and charge distributed across the non-binding surface of the antibody rather than at the antigen-contacting CDR tips.

The assay the AstraZeneca group used as their training label is clone self-interaction biolayer interferometry, or C-S-I-B-L-I, a plate-based format that measures weak, reversible self-interaction under low-material conditions. In a panel of 246 monoclonal antibodies, C-S-I-B-L-I correlates moderately with high-concentration viscosity. In a separate set of 41 antibodies tested in human F-c-R-n transgenic mice, it correlates strongly with in-vivo clearance. That combination — a proxy that reaches both a formulation problem and a pharmacokinetics problem — makes C-S-I-B-L-I a practically useful training signal for a developability model.

Their prediction model, published in mAbs, fuses sequence and structure through three separate attention streams. The sequence stream takes E-S-M-2, the Meta protein language model, which provides residue-level embeddings that encode sequence context and evolutionary conservation. The positional stream encodes chain identity — heavy versus light — which matters for self-association because heavy and light chain surfaces contribute differently to the exposed patches that drive aggregation. The structural stream takes the residue-level graph derived from AlphaFold-predicted structures, with edges connecting residues that are spatially close but may be distant in sequence — exactly the kind of non-local contact that a sequence model alone would miss. These three streams are fused through what the authors call disentangled multi-stream attention: each stream attends independently before fusion, preventing dominant streams from overriding weaker ones.

They evaluate this on 1,499 I-g-G molecules and 841 V-H-Hs, with edit-distance-controlled train-test splits, meaning test antibodies are sequence-dissimilar from training antibodies to prevent inflated performance from sequence memorization. For V-H-Hs, the structure-aware model achieves an F1 score of 0.76 on classifying high versus low self-interactors. For I-g-Gs, the corresponding score is 0.57. The lower I-g-G performance likely reflects the greater structural complexity of the full-length antibody, where contributions from the constant region and hinge are harder to capture with current single-domain structure prediction. They also evaluate a sequence-only version of the disentangled model, without AlphaFold inputs, and it still outperforms a standard E-S-M-2 baseline — suggesting the disentangled attention architecture adds value before structure is even introduced.

For interpretability, they fit a separate set of biophysical feature models on physicochemical descriptors derived from AlphaFold — charge, dipole, hydrophobicity, aggregation propensity — and apply Shapley value analysis to identify which features drive the predictions. The dominant signals are charge and dipole moments across the CDR and framework regions, followed by hydrophobicity. This aligns with the wet-lab consensus on what drives viscosity-prone antibody sequences, and it provides a mechanistic readout indicating which CDR residues to target in re-engineering for improved developability.

The practical message is simple: if you are already running AlphaFold on your antibody panels for paratope analysis, you are generating the structural inputs this model needs at no additional cost. Adding a self-association classifier to that workflow could help triage candidates before committing to the battery of expensive downstream biophysical assays. The authors also note the framework is designed to generalize to other developability endpoints beyond self-association, which makes it a potentially flexible tool across the broader developability space.

Paper link: https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2694124

**Second: USP-ddG — a unified delta-delta-G framework and the CR6261 H1N1 influenza benchmark.**

Predicting the change in binding free energy caused by a mutation — delta-delta-G — is one of the most practically important quantities in antibody affinity maturation. If you have a lead antibody and a set of candidate mutations in the CDR loops, a reliable delta-delta-G predictor lets you rank them computationally, prioritize the most promising for synthesis, and avoid wasting wet-lab resources on mutations that are likely to be destabilizing or neutral. USP-ddG, published this week in the I-S-M-B proceedings issue of Bioinformatics from Central South University, is a new method for this problem that sets the state of the art on the S-K-E-M-P-I version 2.0 benchmark and explicitly demonstrates its utility on CR6261, a broadly neutralizing antibody against H1N1 influenza A.

The core design principle of USP-ddG is that binding free energy change upon mutation has contributions from physically distinct sources, and that modeling these separately before combining them gives better predictions than any single-source approach. The model decomposes delta-delta-G into three additive components. The first is an inverse folding term: ProteinMPNN, the inverse folding model, assigns log-likelihood scores to protein sequences given their backbone structures. Using the Boltzmann distribution, the difference in sequence log-likelihood between mutant and wild-type gives a thermodynamic signal for how favorable the mutation is given the structural context. The second term comes from FoldX, the empirical force field, which captures side-chain packing, electrostatics, and solvation energetics rapidly and with physical interpretability. The third term is a trainable geometric neural network — an improved version of the authors' prior model, CATH-ddG — that encodes the full 3D complex and applies Gaussian noise to atomic coordinates during training as a regularization strategy, improving robustness to small structural errors that come from structure prediction.

To handle the structural diversity of protein-protein interfaces in the S-K-E-M-P-I training set — which spans thousands of different protein families with very different folding topologies — they adopt a Mixture of Experts layer in the neural network. Each input token is routed to a small subset of specialized experts. One shared expert captures domain-invariant features common across all protein structures; the others specialize in the structural and functional idiosyncrasies of particular protein domains. This is the same sparse routing strategy used in large language model architectures for efficient specialization at scale. For training data organization, they propose C-A-T-H-guided Folding Ordering, a curriculum learning strategy that presents training examples in order of increasing structural divergence, progressing from easy to hard to reduce catastrophic forgetting during training.

On the hold-out C-A-T-H test set — 813 mutations with no structural family overlap with training data, which eliminates the data leakage that inflates results on standard S-K-E-M-P-I splits — USP-ddG achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.655 and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.794 across all mutations. The prior best model on this partition, CATH-ddG, reaches 0.615 Pearson correlation. The gains are largest on multi-point mutations, which is the regime that matters most for antibody affinity maturation because real optimization campaigns accumulate multiple CDR mutations whose effects interact epistatically, producing a non-additive landscape.

The H1N1 case study is the most directly relevant benchmark for this audience. CR6261 is a broadly neutralizing anti-hemagglutinin antibody that targets the conserved stem region across H1 and H5 subtypes. The AbBiBench dataset for this antibody covers 1,887 mutants spanning combinatorially complete libraries across 14 heavy-chain positions, all experimentally characterized against A/New Caledonia/20/1999 H1N1. The original Phillips lab study generated this dataset specifically to map the full epistatic landscape, and it is challenging precisely because epistasis — where the effect of one mutation depends on which other mutations are present — makes additive assumptions fail. USP-ddG achieves a Pearson correlation of 0.658 and a Spearman correlation of 0.627 on this benchmark, compared to 0.583 and 0.566 for the best prior method, an improvement of roughly 13 percent in Pearson correlation. For trastuzumab against HER2, evaluated on 419 computationally designed CDR loop variants that were validated by surface plasmon resonance, USP-ddG is also best or tied for best performance.

The authors are explicit about one limitation worth noting: ProteinMPNN, the inverse folding component, assumes a fixed protein backbone, which means CDR loop flexibility is not modeled. Antibody CDR loops, especially the third heavy-chain loop, are among the most structurally dynamic regions in the proteome, and mutations within them can induce backbone rearrangements that a static-structure term will miss. Antibody-specific inverse folding models — AbMPNN in particular — are a natural plug-in replacement, and the authors call this out as the obvious path forward. The code is publicly available on GitHub.

Paper link: https://doi.org/10.1093/bioinformatics/btag249

**Third: Atlas — A-Alpha Bio opens 450 million affinity measurements.**

The third item today is a platform launch rather than a research paper, but it bears directly on the data bottleneck that constrains every model covered in this show. A-Alpha Bio announced this week the public opening of Atlas, a licensed data ecosystem built on top of their AlphaSeq quantitative binding measurement platform.

The motivating observation is stark. All publicly available antibody-antigen structure databases combined contain fewer than 10,000 structures across roughly 2,000 unique targets. Of those, fewer than 800 have any paired quantitative affinity measurement. Most sequence-to-affinity models in the antibody AI literature — the models used for virtual screening, for CDR optimization, for developability flagging — are trained on datasets of this size. That means they are fitting high-dimensional parameters to a narrow and structurally biased slice of antibody-antigen interaction space.

A-Alpha Bio's AlphaSeq platform measures quantitative binding affinities by displaying antibody variants on the surface of yeast and measuring competitive binding in a standardized format with consistent internal controls, making measurements quantitatively combinable across experiments. The Atlas database now contains 450 million such measurements, accumulated over years of running this platform at scale. It also includes over 7,000 lab-validated antibody-antigen pseudo-structures — computational structural models anchored to experimental binding data, which are the inputs that structure-based design models need.

The data access layer offers pre-packaged Data Blocks for immediate licensing, and a custom data generation service where customers can specify antibody or V-H-H sequences and receive either a private dataset or contribute to the shared database. There is an Atlas Consortium currently enrolling members for V-H-H-specific data aggregation — which is notable given that the mAbs paper from earlier in today's episode found nanobodies to be the better-predicted format in the self-association model, and given the broad industry interest in V-H-H as a developable single-domain scaffold. Single-chain variable fragment data access is planned for 2027.

The AI model trained on the Atlas database is AlphaBind, which takes antibody and antigen sequences alone and predicts binding strength. Training a sequence-to-affinity predictor on 450 million measurements is a qualitatively different regime from the public training sets that academic models use. Whether AlphaBind generalizes broadly across antigen classes or is concentrated on a narrower set of scaffolds and antigens that limits its coverage — that is something external benchmarking will need to establish. But the data access matters independently of the in-house model. Having a standardized, large-scale measurement database as an external resource changes the training distribution available to any group building antibody AI, not just A-Alpha Bio.
