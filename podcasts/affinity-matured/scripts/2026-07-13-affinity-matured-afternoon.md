Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. This is the afternoon edition for July thirteenth. The morning episode was thin on antibody-specific work, but the day has since delivered — three items today, all antibody-relevant. First, a version-two revision of the Fleishman lab's CADAbRe pipeline, a structure- and energetics-based approach to designing entire synthetic antibody repertoires with drug-like properties, now with wet-lab validation of designed binders against four unrelated antigens. The first item is a bioRxiv preprint, version-two posted July thirteenth, titled "Computational design of structurally diverse antibody repertoires with drug-like properties." First author Ariel Tennenhouse, corresponding author Sarel Fleishman at the Weizmann Institute of Science in Rehovot. This is worth spending real time on because it is one of the more ambitious attempts yet to replace animal immunisation with a fully computationally designed antibody library, and unlike most methods papers in this space, it comes with an end-to-end wet-lab demonstration.

The framing problem is that animal immunisation still dominates therapeutic antibody discovery — the E-U alone sacrifices over a million animals per year in this context — and it is slow, ethically fraught, and only weakly controllable at the sequence level. Synthetic repertoires exist as an alternative, but they historically deliver binders that lack drug-like properties: they aggregate, they self-associate, they clear fast in vivo. Recent synthetic repertoire designs have improved but still tend to fix a single heavy-chain framework, or randomise CDRs largely without regard to whether the mutated sequences will fold. The Fleishman group's CADAbRe pipeline attempts a principled fix. Every antibody in the library is designed by structure and by energy to be stable and foldable before it ever gets synthesised, and diversity is generated by combining germline gene segments and designed C-D-R-H-three loops rather than by randomising positions in place.

The design pipeline runs in four computational steps. Step one: combine all human V and J germline gene segments — heavy and light — to generate thousands of sequences that cover every region of the antibody variable fragment except the C-D-R-H-three loop. Step two: for each germline combination, graft in each of one hundred and forty-one experimentally determined C-D-R-H-three loops from diverse Fv structures, matching the length distribution seen in therapeutic antibodies. That gives roughly one hundred and fifty thousand germline-plus-H-three combinations, each of which is then atomistically modelled with the antibody stability design algorithm CUMAb on the parent structure the H-three came from. The Rosetta energy of that model is the primary stability filter, and every combination is also independently folded with A-Body-Builder-two to catch cases where the modelled backbone diverges from a plausible fold. Combinations where CUMAb and A-Body-Builder-two disagree are flagged as prone to misfolding and dropped. Step three uses the EpiNNet machine-learning method to select sets of germline genes and C-D-R-H-threes that can be freely mixed and matched without breaking stability — a combinatorial constraint-satisfaction problem the group had previously used for enzyme design. Ten such sets emerged from this pipeline, containing thirty-five thousand unique variable-region sequences with an eighty-five per cent predicted stable-and-foldable rate, and over seven hundred unique light-chain and heavy-chain V-gene pairs across the ten sets — two orders of magnitude more V-gene diversity than in recently described synthetic repertoires. Step four uses Rosetta atomistic design to diversify each C-D-R-H-three while pinning down the residues that make critical stabilising contacts — the intra-H-three hydrogen bonds and the H-three-to-Fv hydrophobic contacts — and also disallowing methionine, cysteine, and N-linked glycosylation motifs to reduce developability risk up front.

The design step alone produces billions of unique antibody sequences. Because C-D-R-H-three diversity is compact — hundreds of thousands of short peptides — the actual repertoire can be assembled combinatorially by ligating a small number of V and J gene fragments with a large H-three oligo pool. As a proof of concept the group picked the largest of the ten sets — including the I-G-K-V-one, I-G-K-V-three, I-G-K-V-six, I-G-H-V-one, and I-G-H-V-three subgroups — expanded to over four hundred and fifty light-heavy V-gene pairs by including homologous alleles, and used a one-pot Golden Gate reaction to assemble the library. The final synthesised library contains over five hundred million unique antibodies, at a D-N-A cost of under three ten-thousandths of a dollar per variant, ten-day turnaround, and the whole thing gets displayed on phage as Fabs.

Now for the validation, which is where a paper like this either lives or dies. The group panned the library against four unrelated antigens — hen egg white lysozyme, the SARS-Co-V-two spike receptor-binding domain, mouse F-L-T-three, and a soluble version of the Lujo virus spike protein. From fewer than one hundred and fifty phage colonies sequenced across the four campaigns, they recovered thirteen designed binders, twelve of which exactly matched a designed sequence — that is, the vast majority of binders came from within the designed space rather than from stochastic mutations introduced during assembly. The binders spanned six different light V genes, five different heavy V genes, and six different parental H-three scaffolds, producing very different electrostatic and structural properties in the antigen-binding surface across hits. Multiple different binders emerged against the same target — five against R-B-D, two against H-E-W-L, five against F-L-T-three — which is what you want to see if the library actually samples binding solutions rather than converging on one lucky sequence.

Expressed as full-length human I-g-G-one, nine of the designs expressed cleanly, with yields well above the historical average of the expression system. E-C-fifties measured by ELISA came in between zero-point-three and eleven nanomolar, comparable to the known clinical benchmarks for the two targets — R-B-D and H-E-W-L — where a benchmark was available. Specificity across the four antigens was clean; none of the designs cross-bound the noncognate targets. Developability was the acid test. The group ran the standard suite — nano-D-S-F for thermal stability and aggregation temperature, B-V-P and cross-interaction chromatography for nonspecific binding, size-exclusion, A-C-SINS, and D-L-S for self-association, and hydrophobic interaction chromatography for hydrophobicity — and compared against twelve clinical-stage antibodies including Adalimumab as a low-liability benchmark and Pembrolizumab as an approved but liability-laden benchmark. All nine designs had aggregation temperatures above seventy-four degrees Celsius and melting temperatures above sixty-nine degrees Celsius. Seven had over ninety per cent purity by size-exclusion. Most exhibited low nonspecific binding and low self-association. Three flagged high for hydrophobicity — and interestingly, two of those three were the R-B-D binders, which is consistent with the known observation that anti-R-B-D antibodies tend to run hydrophobic, so this is likely a target-specific rather than pipeline-specific liability.

The take-home is that most designs, isolated from a standard phage panning campaign with no downstream engineering, sat inside the developability envelope defined by approved therapeutic antibodies. The paper's specific methodological arguments are worth internalising for anyone building antibody libraries. First: pairing an energetic filter with an independent foldability check catches a class of designs that pass energy but fold to something the fold-predictor doesn't like, and that class turns out to correlate with real developability problems downstream. Second: eschewing in-place randomisation in favour of pre-designing every H-three sequence to preserve stabilising contacts is what preserves the drug-like properties. Third: EpiNNet's role in selecting freely combinable V-and-H-three sets is what makes the repertoire scale — because oligo synthesis is much cheaper than gene synthesis, and combining a small number of V-gene fragments with a large H-three oligo pool is the trick that gets you to five hundred million variants for thirty thousand dollars.

For the audience of this show, the specific transferable pieces are the combined-model foldability check — running both CUMAb and A-Body-Builder-two and cross-comparing — and the strategy of designing library diversity through combinatorial fragment assembly rather than positional randomisation. Both are exportable to any nanobody, T-cell-receptor, or general binder repertoire, not just I-g-G. The repertoire itself will be available for academic use. If the developability numbers hold up at scale, this is a meaningful step toward replacing animal immunisation with a design-test-learn loop in silico.

The second item is another bioRxiv preprint from July thirteenth, titled "Structure-guided design of a C-D-eighty-one-binding mini-protein that blocks hepatitis C virus entry." First author Sojin An, corresponding authors Hyun-Soo Cho at Yonsei University in Seoul, and Seungtaek Kim at the Institut Pasteur Korea. This is not an antibody paper, but it is a well-executed application of the standard de novo binder design stack — R-F-diffusion plus Protein-M-P-N-N plus AlphaFold — to a difficult host-factor target, and the details of what did and did not work at the design stage are useful reference points for anyone running that pipeline against a small extracellular loop.

The problem: hepatitis-C-virus infection is largely treatable with direct-acting antivirals, but the current drugs act intracellularly and do not prevent reinfection. The tetraspanin C-D-eighty-one is a host-factor entry receptor for H-C-V — its large extracellular loop, sometimes called the L-E-L, binds the H-C-V E-two glycoprotein — and if you can occlude or lock that loop you can block entry regardless of viral genotype. Because the target is a host factor with a very small binding surface, and because C-D-eighty-one has a conformational cycle where a hydrogen bond between histidine one-fifty-one and tyrosine one-twenty-seven and a disulfide between cysteines one-fifty-seven and one-seventy-five together maintain a closed state at neutral pH — with endosomal acidification triggering opening for fusion — the design goal is not just to bind, but to bind in a way that locks the closed state.

The pipeline is textbook. R-F-diffusion generated de novo backbones sixty to eighty amino acids long, conditioned to dock against a defined hotspot patch at residues one-sixty-two, one-sixty-nine, one-eighty-two, and one-eighty-six on the C and D helices of the L-E-L. Diffusion noise was set moderately — translational and rotational noise at zero-point-five — and the topology was constrained to a three-helix bundle to bias toward foldable scaffolds. From an initial pool of five thousand backbones, forty-four passed an R-F-diffusion confidence filter with predicted-alignment-error under five-point-nine-nine. Protein-M-P-N-N then generated one thousand sequences per scaffold, and AlphaFold-two was used for in silico validation. Here the authors report a specific methodological pitfall worth noting. Because the C-D-eighty-one L-E-L is a truncated soluble domain in the structural template, hydrophobic residues that are normally shielded by the transmembrane region are exposed in the design context — which pulled many R-F-diffusion outputs into off-target hydrophobic interfaces rather than the intended epitope. Standard AlphaFold i-p-T-M filtering caught many of these but not all. The authors' fix is a multi-model consensus filter: a design was only advanced if at least two of five independent AlphaFold-two models agreed on the same backbone conformation and gave an i-p-T-M above zero-point-eight-three. That structural convergence criterion, rather than a single confidence threshold, is what separated real designs from confident-but-wrong ones. It maps onto the same problem you see designing binders to any truncated soluble receptor construct where the native lipid or membrane environment is not represented in the template.

Twenty candidates were expressed. Fifteen expressed cleanly at tens of milligrams per litre of E. coli culture, at expected molecular weights and high purity. Surface plasmon resonance screening at one micromolar identified eight hits with measurable binding to recombinant C-D-eighty-one L-E-L, and one candidate, called mini-nineteen, emerged as the lead. Its kinetics — an association rate of seven-point-four times ten-to-the-five per molar per second, dissociation rate of three-point-eight times ten-to-the-minus-four per second — yield a K-D of zero-point-five nanomolar. Notably a sibling design, mini-twenty, which shares the same designed scaffold but differs at the sequence level, comes in forty-eight-fold weaker at twenty-four nanomolar — a reminder that sequence-level design still matters even after scaffold selection. Circular dichroism shows mini-nineteen retains its alpha-helical fold up to ninety-five degrees Celsius without a cooperative unfolding transition, which is the extreme thermal resilience that has become almost a signature of computationally designed mini-proteins in general. It also stays monodisperse on gel filtration. Flow cytometry and super-resolution imaging confirm recognition of native cell-surface C-D-eighty-one, and mini-nineteen neutralises H-C-V cell culture with an I-C-fifty of one-point-two nanomolar. Molecular dynamics simulations in the abstract suggest mini-nineteen acts as a structural clamp restricting the fusogenic conformational plasticity, which is consistent with the stated design goal of locking the closed state, though the M-D details are for the full paper.

The reason this matters for antibody engineers is not the target — most of you won't work on C-D-eighty-one — but the specific finding that i-p-T-M alone is not a sufficient filter when your design surface has exposed hydrophobicity from a truncated construct, and the workaround of requiring cross-model backbone convergence. That transfers cleanly to any de novo design task on receptor epitopes where you're working from a partial extracellular construct. It also transfers to antibody-antigen docking pipelines where the antigen has been truncated to a soluble domain — a very common situation for viral glycoprotein head domains.

The third item is an Advanced Science paper published July thirteenth, titled "Somatic evolution of a germline antibody expands its breadth to neutralize early SARS-Co-V-two Omicron variants." First author Yunlong Cao. The published version corresponds to a bioRxiv preprint posted in March, which is what I read the full text of. This paper is worth a look because it delivers something the field has been talking around for a while — a structurally grounded recipe for taking a germline-encoded neutralising antibody that has lost activity against Omicron, and adding a very small number of rationally chosen somatic hypermutations to recover breadth. Four mutations, in this case. That is a concrete number, and the mechanism is worked out at crystal-structure resolution.

The starting antibody is called HB148 — an I-G-H-V-three-fifty-three germline antibody, meaning zero somatic hypermutations relative to germline, isolated from an unvaccinated patient infected during the first wave of the pandemic. In surrogate virus neutralisation, germline HB148 neutralises wild-type SARS-Co-V-two, Alpha, and Delta with N-T-fifty values in the one to two microgram per millilitre range. Beta and Gamma are much weaker at seventy-six and eleven micrograms per millilitre. Omicron B-A-one is completely escaped even at one hundred micrograms per millilitre. A 3.04-ångstrom crystal structure of HB148 in complex with wild-type R-B-D shows the antibody engages the class-one epitope like most other I-G-H-V-three-fifty-three antibodies — heavy chain contributing sixty-nine per cent of the buried surface area, contacting conserved residues Y421, Y453, and A475. The Omicron mutations K417N, S477N, Q493R, and Y505H collectively remodel the paratope surface enough to abolish binding.

The design idea is straightforward: take the coronavirus antibody database, filter to I-G-H-V-three-fifty-three antibodies with defined B-A-one neutralisation data, and look for the somatic hypermutations enriched in the B-A-one neutralisers relative to non-neutralisers. Four positions come out of that comparison — G26E and T28I in C-D-R-H-one, and S53P and Y58F in C-D-R-H-two. All four are grafted into HB148 to give a variant called HB148-M-four, and binding to wild-type and B-A-one R-B-Ds is measured by biolayer interferometry. Individually, three of the four — G26E, S53P, and Y58F — each drop the K-D against B-A-one R-B-D roughly eight- to tenfold. Combined into HB148-M-four, the K-D drops by around one hundred-fold against both wild-type and B-A-one — the effect is roughly additive, which is a useful mechanistic detail because it tells you the four residues are optimising distinct paratope-epitope contacts rather than a single conformational effect. In neutralisation assays, HB148-M-four gets N-T-fifty values between zero-point-one-nine and one-point-five-six micrograms per millilitre against wild-type, B-A-one, and B-A-four-slash-five. Against X-B-B-point-one-point-five, however, neither the germline nor HB148-M-four neutralises even at one hundred micrograms per millilitre — the additional Omicron substitutions in X-B-B remodel the surface further and the four-mutation recipe is not enough.

The generalisation test is where this becomes more than a single-antibody result. The four mutations were introduced into four other I-G-H-V-three-fifty-three antibodies from the literature — C1A-F10, P5A-3C8, BG4-25, and LY-CoV488 — all of which bind wild-type R-B-D but fail to bind B-A-one R-B-D. Three of the four antibodies had their B-A-one binding restored by the M-four graft. C1A-F10 was the exception — its M-four version gained affinity for wild-type but not for B-A-one, so this is not a universal patch. It is a fix that works when the antibody's failure mode against Omicron is compatible with the four-position paratope adjustment, and it does not work otherwise. Structural analysis of the individual mutations shows a specific mechanism: G26E extends interactions between position twenty-six and R-B-D residues 477 and 478, restoring a contact lost to the S477N substitution. S53P and Y58F improve packing at the interface for both wild-type and Omicron R-B-D. In other words, this is a paratope-adaptation strategy that specifically targets the residues that Omicron itself remodelled.

For the antibody-engineering audience two things are worth noting. First, this is a proof of concept for a specific class of computational-plus-structural approach: mine the sequence database for empirically enriched somatic hypermutations in a neutralising subset, then graft that consensus onto a broadly reactive germline as a "breadth-restoration" patch. That is a workflow you can imagine automating and applying to any germline lineage with a bimodal neutralisation profile — some antibodies from that lineage neutralise a challenging variant, others don't, and the enrichment of specific S-H-Ms in the neutralising subset gives you a small candidate set of mutations to try. Second, and more sobering, the four-mutation patch doesn't rescue X-B-B and doesn't rescue every I-G-H-V-three-fifty-three antibody, so the ceiling of any single such patch is limited. But as a way of prioritising which somatic hypermutations to explore for breadth engineering — as opposed to random mutagenesis or brute-force D-M-S — this is a much more focused strategy.

Two limitations worth naming on this last paper. It uses surrogate virus neutralisation and pseudovirus systems throughout; the true-live-virus neutralisation panel is not shown here. And the four mutations were identified by enrichment in an existing sequence database, so this is retrospective consensus mining rather than de novo prediction — the mutations were there in the database because affinity maturation in real B cells had already produced them. That is a strong signal but it is not the same as generating novel breadth-conferring mutations. Combining this kind of consensus mining with modern paratope-scale generative models is likely where the field goes next.
