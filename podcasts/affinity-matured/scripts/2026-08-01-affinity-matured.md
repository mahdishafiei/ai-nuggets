## Script

Good morning. Saturday's edition of Affinity Matured. Three items today, all bioRxiv preprints posted on July thirty-first. First, a large-scale structural benchmark of AlphaFold-three on antibody-antigen complexes from the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston that puts numbers on what AlphaFold-three is actually good at, where it silently fails, and what fraction of its failures are close enough to recover. Second, a topologically engineered non-Y-shaped multispecific antibody scaffold from Hinge Bio that self-assembles via a hinge-integrated collectrin-like domain and translates into a dual-Fc anti-CD19/CD20 candidate heading into the clinic, plus in vivo non-human primate efficacy for an anti-SARS-CoV-two version. And third, a head-to-head benchmark of protein language model embeddings — E-S-M-one-b, E-S-M-two, Prot-T-five, and Prost-T-five — for alignment-free structural similarity in the sequence twilight zone, which is the practical question every antibody-language-model group faces when picking an embedding for a downstream head.

First item. bioRxiv, posted July thirty-first, "A Structural Antibody Benchmark of AlphaFold-three reveals Hallucinated Epitopes and a Bias for Orderness," from the McWilliams School of Biomedical Informatics at the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston — senior author Wenjin Jim Zheng. This paper matters because AlphaFold-three has been the practical default for antibody-antigen docking for over a year now, and nobody has actually pinned down how well it works when you feed it the entire published corpus of antibody-antigen structures with proper negative controls. The setup here is the strongest attempt so far. They pull three thousand four hundred and one non-redundant antibody-antigen complexes from the Structural Antibody Database, restrict the antibody sequences to the variable-fragment length, and annotate the C-D-Rs using a union definition that spans Chothia, Kabat, and I-M-G-T so no potentially relevant residue is dropped. Then they construct two kinds of negative controls. The One-to-One negatives pair each antibody with a randomly sampled human protein between fifty and five hundred residues, verified for low sequence similarity to the true antigen — that gives three thousand four hundred and one non-binding pairs. The Many-to-One negatives take a fixed set of six proteins from six different organisms — the calcium-binding parvalbumin from rat, a Drosophila gene, an E. coli arabinose enzyme, a cow chloride channel, an Arabidopsis defensin, and human C-D-two-seven-four which is PD-L-one — and pair every antibody against all six, giving another twenty thousand-plus non-binding pairs. Total screen: three thousand four hundred and one positives against twenty-three thousand seven hundred and ninety-eight negatives. Every pair is run through AlphaFold-three at ten inference seeds, and the false negatives from that pass are re-run at one hundred seeds to see whether more sampling rescues them.

The binary classifier is a threshold on two AlphaFold-three confidence metrics: Predicted Aligned Error less than two-point-five, or Interface Predicted Template Modeling score greater than zero-point-seven. Applied to the whole benchmark, that gives an overall recall of forty-six percent, a precision of sixty-four percent, an F-one of zero-point-five-three, and an apparent accuracy of ninety percent — but the authors flag that the ninety-percent accuracy number is inflated by the twenty-three thousand true negatives and shouldn't be read at face value. The one-hundred-seed re-run pushes recall to fifty-three percent, which they present as the ceiling on this benchmark. Now the interesting parts, and there are four of them.

Number one: X-ray versus electron microscopy. When you split the positive controls by how the crystal structure was solved, the X-ray-derived antibodies recall at fifty-two percent while the E-M-derived antibodies recall at only thirty percent — a twenty-two point gap. The authors argue this reflects two things that likely combine. X-ray structures are overrepresented in AlphaFold-three's training set, so there is a batch effect the model learned. And X-ray-tractable proteins are systematically less flexible and less disordered than E-M-solved proteins — cryo-E-M is where hard-to-crystallize, dynamic, disordered targets tend to end up. So the E-M gap is partly what the training set doesn't have and partly what the underlying biology makes hard.

Number two: disorderness. This is the paper's most concrete design lever for anyone running AlphaFold-three today. Larger target proteins consistently reduce positive prediction rate, and the mechanism they demonstrate isn't just search-space combinatorics — it's that intrinsically disordered regions actively suppress hallucination onto themselves. Their negative control target PD-L-one — the human C-D-two-seven-four protein — has a long alpha-helix jutting out from an N-terminal disordered region, and when they superimpose all the false-positive antibodies that AlphaFold-three predicts against P-D-L-one, none of them are predicted to bind to that disordered region. The false positives cluster on the ordered globular domain instead. That's a consistent finding across all six Many-to-One negatives: AlphaFold-three can hallucinate a plausible epitope essentially anywhere on the ordered globular surface of a random protein, but it will not hallucinate onto a disordered region. The practical implication is exactly what the authors recommend in the discussion: when you're screening antibodies against a target with substantial disorder, truncate the disordered regions before running AlphaFold-three, because you're currently paying a search-space cost — recall drops with target size — without a false-positive protection benefit, since the disordered regions were already protected.

Number three: what didn't matter. Data leakage did not bias the results. They intersected the set of all one hundred and ninety-five thousand training C-I-F files against every S-Ab-Dab P-D-B they extracted antibodies from, and found that two thousand three hundred and twenty-three of their thirty-four hundred and one positive-control antibodies had leaked into training. When they compared AlphaFold-three's Predicted Aligned Error on leaked versus unseen antibodies within each batch, the distributions could not be statistically distinguished. So AlphaFold-three is not just recalling structures it has memorized; it is doing something more general than that. Similarly, the specific amino acid composition of C-D-Rs and the lengths of C-D-Rs did not correlate with prediction success — the residue-frequency histograms are indistinguishable between predicted binders and predicted non-binders. And when an antibody was predicted to hallucinate a false positive against a random target, the rate of hallucination did not correlate with whether that same antibody was predicted correctly against its true antigen — a Pearson correlation of zero-point-nine-nine-seven between the false-positive rates of the two groups. AlphaFold-three doesn't treat some antibodies as generically sticky. It hallucinates a specific complementary epitope for each antibody it fails on.

Number four: how close the false negatives actually are. Of the fifteen hundred antibodies that AlphaFold-three called as non-binding to their true antigen, they ran DockQ, an epitope-shift metric, and an antibody-displacement metric on all of them. About thirty-four percent of these false negatives had the correct epitope location predicted — the model found the right spot on the antigen surface — but the structural alignment quality was poor, so the confidence metrics penalized it below the binding threshold. This is a big number. It says roughly a third of AlphaFold-three's antibody-antigen binding false negatives could be rescued by a conformation-refinement pass after prediction — molecular dynamics, better sampling around the interface, or fine-tuning AlphaFold-three's recycling parameter — without needing more or better data. The authors frame this as a call to action: build refinement tools that operate on AlphaFold-three's own outputs when the confidence is soft, rather than treating each low-confidence prediction as a hard fail.

Big picture on this one. Fifty-three-percent recall is a specific number to sit with — that's what a well-tuned single-tool AlphaFold-three inference at one hundred seeds returns on a large, non-redundant antibody-antigen benchmark. The three-percent false-positive rate is small but real and comes with the caveat that AlphaFold-three will find you a plausible-looking epitope on almost any ordered globular target, so a top-scoring predicted complex against a novel target is not by itself evidence of biological binding without an orthogonal signal. If you have flexibility in target selection, truncating disordered regions and preferring targets that lean toward X-ray-tractable rigidity gives you materially better prediction rates. If a candidate looks marginal in the confidence metrics but the predicted epitope location makes biochemical sense, do not discard it — invest in refinement first.

Second item. bioRxiv, posted July thirty-first as version two, "Topologically Engineered Tetrahedral Antibody Architectures for Multispecific Therapy," from Hinge Bio in Redwood City with collaborators at the German Primate Center, Charité Berlin, and Vitalant Research Institute — corresponding author Daniel Capon. This is an antibody-architecture paper — the closest recent analogue would be the last few years of dual-Fc and multivalent scaffolds like the Zybody, the Duobody, and various knob-in-hole variants, but this one goes further. The core observation is that all conventional multispecific antibody formats — bispecific I-g-Gs, dual-variable-domain immunoglobulins, tandem s-c-F-vs, tandem V-H-Hs, D-A-R-P-in fusions — impose a planar geometry on the effector arms. The Y-shape of native I-g-G is elegant for a bivalent affinity ligand but starts to steric-clash the moment you fuse additional binders to it, and it constrains the three-dimensional orientation of arms that need to bridge distinct cells or engage clustered antigens cooperatively.

Their solution is to co-express a collectrin-like domain — abbreviated C-L-D — as a hinge insertion in the antibody heavy chain. The collectrin-like domain is the dimerization module of angiotensin-converting enzyme two and of the sodium-glucose co-transporter accessory. Two heavy chains bearing this insertion will pair via their C-L-Ds, and then two of those pairs can further cross-dimerize via a second reciprocal C-L-D interface, yielding what the authors call a superdimer. Structural modeling suggests this superdimer adopts a rigid, tetrahedral-like configuration — hence the paper's name — with two Fc dimers and two additional effector-domain dimers projecting outward in three-dimensional space from a central C-L-D nucleus. Six unobstructed binding arms are possible: four Fabs plus two Fcs, or four extracellular domains plus two Fcs, or mixed combinations.

The initial demonstration uses A-C-E-two — the SARS-CoV-two entry receptor — as the effector arm. An A-C-E-two-seven-forty superdimer, which is two pairs of the A-C-E-two extracellular domain reciprocally cross-dimerized through the C-L-D hinge, neutralizes SARS-CoV-two four hundred and three-fold more potently than the standard planar A-C-E-two-six-fifteen dimer. Against the coronavirus H-CoV-N-L-six-three, it neutralizes eighty-seven-fold better. When they made a bispecific tetrahedral antibody combining A-C-E-two-seven-forty with the variable domain of B-thirteen-A, a therapeutic monoclonal against SARS-CoV-two, it neutralized SARS-CoV-two six hundred and forty-six-fold better than the A-C-E-two-seven-forty dimer alone and twenty-nine-fold better than the parental B-thirteen-A monoclonal. These are large numbers, not small numbers.

The most useful part of this paper for the antibody-engineering audience is not the S-A-R-S neutralization data per se — that's the demonstration — but three structural findings from downstream engineering. First: they show that any conventional Fab domain can be swapped into the tetrahedral scaffold, and they build tetrahedral versions of eight F-D-A-approved or authorized therapeutic antibodies. In all cases the tetrahedral reformat outperforms the parental bivalent I-g-G on pseudovirus neutralization, which suggests the platform is a general potentiation tool, not just an A-C-E-two-specific trick. Second: pharmacokinetics. The tetrahedral architecture does not compromise F-c-R-n-mediated recycling. Terminal half-lives in Tg-thirty-two human F-c-R-n transgenic mice are ten to eleven days for the A-C-E-two bispecific tetrahedrals and sixteen to eighteen days for the pure I-g-G tetrahedrals — competitive with the twelve-to-fifteen days for the parental full-length monoclonals. This is important because most non-native antibody architectures suffer accelerated clearance, and this one apparently doesn't. Third: they demonstrate functional Fc-gamma receptor binding cooperativity across the dual-Fc domains of the anti-C-D-nineteen/C-D-twenty tetrahedral antibody — meaning both Fc arms simultaneously engage Fc-gamma receptors on effector cells, which is not achievable with a single-Fc I-g-G. And to fix the assembly-heterogeneity problem — because the C-L-D dimer has natural dynamic dissociation — they introduce an interface disulfide bond into the C-L-D dimer that abolishes the dissociation and resolves the assembly into discrete, stable quaternary states.

The anti-C-D-nineteen/C-D-twenty candidate is heading toward clinical translation for systemic lupus erythematosus, so this is not just a preprint paper — it's the biophysical writeup behind an active clinical program. For anyone modeling multispecific antibodies with structure predictors or generative-design tools, the tetrahedral topology adds a design axis that current I-g-G-centric generators do not natively cover, and the C-L-D-mediated self-assembly is a well-defined structural constraint that could go directly into a Boltz or Chai template as an alternative to explicit chain-pairing terms.

Third item. bioRxiv, posted July thirty-first, "Evaluating Protein Language Model Embeddings for Structural Similarity in the Protein-Sequence Twilight Zone," from the Department of Computer Science and Computer Information Systems at Auburn University at Montgomery — corresponding author Sutanu Bhattacharya. This one is a workbench paper for anyone building antibody or protein classifiers on top of a frozen protein language model. The question is narrow and practical. If you take two proteins whose sequence identity is thirty percent or lower — the twilight zone, where B-L-A-S-T alignment stops giving you a usable structural-similarity signal — and you pass both sequences through a frozen protein language model, mean-pool the residue-level embeddings to a single vector, and compare those vectors with a similarity metric, how well does that alignment-free score predict actual structural similarity as measured by T-M-align? The benchmark set is twenty thousand four hundred and forty-five P-I-S-C-E-S protein pairs at thirty percent or lower identity, with the T-M-align T-M-min score as the structural ground truth. Four language models: E-S-M-one-b at six hundred and fifty million parameters and twelve-hundred-and-eighty embedding dimension; E-S-M-two at three billion parameters and twenty-five-hundred-and-sixty embedding dimension; Prot-T-five, a T-five encoder-decoder at three billion parameters and one-thousand-and-twenty-four dimension; and Prost-T-five, the same T-five backbone additionally pre-trained on structural three-D-i tokens from the AlphaFold protein structure database at inference. Five similarity metrics on the mean-pooled embeddings: cosine, Euclidean-derived, Manhattan-derived, radial basis function, and dot product.

The headline result is that Prost-T-five, using cosine similarity, gives a Spearman correlation of zero-point-six-seven-six against T-M-min, followed by E-S-M-two at zero-point-six-two-three, Prot-T-five at zero-point-five-five-seven, and E-S-M-one-b at zero-point-four-eight-three. All four beat B-L-A-S-T-P, and — this is the actually useful finding — the advantage of the language model embeddings over B-L-A-S-T-P is most pronounced at the lowest sequence identities. That's the regime where classical alignment breaks down and where the twilight-zone problem actually bites. The second useful finding is on the choice of similarity metric. Cosine similarity is the best metric across all four models. For most of the models, the R-B-F kernel and the Manhattan-derived score are close behind. But the dot product and the raw Euclidean distance are dramatically worse on E-S-M-two — the E-S-M-two Euclidean-derived correlation drops to zero-point-one-six-six and the dot product to zero-point-zero-four-four, from the cosine-similarity value of zero-point-six-two-three on the same embeddings. That's a case where the magnitude of the E-S-M-two embedding carries a lot of noise and normalizing it out is essential. Interpretation for anyone deploying frozen embeddings downstream: cosine similarity is a safe default; do not use dot product or raw Euclidean distance on E-S-M-two.

The Prost-T-five lead over the pure sequence models is the finding worth thinking about most carefully. Prost-T-five was pre-trained with structural three-D-i tokens as an auxiliary objective, and even at inference time on amino-acid sequences with the A-A-to-fold control token — no structural input — the model recovers a better structural-similarity signal than the pure sequence-trained models at the same parameter count. So the training signal transfers through the embedding into a genuinely structure-aware representation. The practical corollary for antibody work is that if your downstream task is structural — remote homology, epitope-similarity clustering, fold classification — Prost-T-five embeddings are the better default than E-S-M-two, at essentially the same inference cost since the two models have similar residue-level embedding sizes. If your task is sequence-level generation or masked infilling, that trade-off is different and outside this paper's scope. And per the paper's efficiency discussion, Prost-T-five's twelve-hundred-and-twenty-four dimensional residue embedding is also cheaper to store and to compute pairwise similarity on than E-S-M-two's twenty-five-hundred-and-sixty dimensional embedding — so on residue-level embedding cost, Prost-T-five is a Pareto improvement.

Those are the three for today. AlphaFold-three's antibody-antigen benchmark from the U-T Health Houston group giving hard numbers on where the model works and where it silently fails, with disorder-truncation and refinement as concrete recovery levers. Hinge Bio's tetrahedral antibody scaffold as a genuinely three-dimensional multispecific architecture with clinical-stage validation and a general potentiation effect across Fab reformats. And the Auburn University at Montgomery evaluation of four protein language model embeddings in the sequence twilight zone, arguing that Prost-T-five plus cosine similarity is the default for alignment-free structural comparison.
