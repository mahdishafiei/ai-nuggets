## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured for the late-night slot of Sunday, July 12th. Thin weekend, third pass — we already covered the multi-state predictor benchmark from Georgia Tech and the Nammu state-space foundation model this morning, and the bispecific H-I-V broadly neutralizing antibody trial and the ProtAug data-augmentation paper this evening. Tonight is a single-item spotlight on a bioRxiv preprint posted today by a single author at U-C Berkeley — an interpretability analysis of E-S-M-3, the multimodal protein language model. It matters to this show because E-S-M-3 is one of the base models people are actively considering for antibody-specific fine-tuning, and the paper answers a very concrete question that anyone extracting embeddings from E-S-M-3 will want the answer to.

The paper is titled "A geometric atlas of how E-S-M-3 organizes modalities across depth," and the author is Jacob Steenwyk in the Department of Molecular and Cell Biology at U-C Berkeley. It was posted to bioRxiv on July 12th. One quick disclosure the paper makes up front — Steenwyk is a scientific consultant to Anthropic — worth naming because the paper touches on interpretability of a competing lab's model.

Some setup on the target of the analysis. E-S-M-3, the E-S-M family's multimodal model from EvolutionaryScale, ingests a protein through several channels at once. The channels are amino-acid sequence, three-dimensional structure encoded as tokens, secondary structure at eight-state granularity, solvent-accessible surface area, and discrete functional annotations from InterPro. Each modality has its own embedding table. Their embeddings are summed at the input into a single residual stream, and then the transformer trunk — forty-eight layers, one-point-four billion parameters, model dimension one thousand five hundred and thirty-six — reads that summed stream. The mechanistic question is straightforward. Do these five modalities occupy separate parts of the representation and stay separate, or does the network mix them together, and if it mixes them, at what depth. The analysis takes a geometry-first descriptive approach. For each protein, run E-S-M-3 once per modality in isolation, with the other channels masked, together with an all-modalities reference. Read out the residual stream at each of the forty-eight layers. Then apply the standard representational-geometry toolbox — silhouette score, linear centered-kernel-alignment, effective rank, and linear probes — to describe where each modality sits at each depth.

Now the findings, and there are five that matter for anyone using E-S-M-3 as a starting point for downstream work.

First: the four physical modalities — sequence, structure, secondary structure, and solvent-accessibility — start in distinct subspaces, stay maximally separated through roughly the first half of the network, and then collapse into a single shared low-dimensional subspace between layers twenty-five and thirty-five. Separation peaks at layer twenty-four with a silhouette score of zero-point-four-two and collapses to a minimum of zero-point-one-five-six at layer thirty-five. The transition is not gradual — there is a sharp knee near layer twenty-five, and by layer thirty-five the five modality clusters that were cleanly separated in a joint principal-component projection have merged into a single cloud. Second: the fusion is ordered, and this is the finding that is most useful practically. The three structure-derived modalities — structure, secondary structure, and solvent accessibility — are aligned with each other from layer zero, with high centered-kernel-alignment values right out of the input. Sequence, the one channel that carries information that cannot be derived from the structure, stays near a centered-kernel-alignment of zero-point-two with the structure-derived modalities until layer twenty-eight, and only then begins to align. Sequence joins last. So if you are using E-S-M-3 in sequence-only mode and pulling embeddings from an early or mid-network layer, you are not yet accessing the shared multi-modal representation that later layers construct. That representation is only available deep in the network, from about layer twenty-eight onward.

Third: the functional-annotation channel never fuses. Every pairing of the function condition with a physical modality stays at a centered-kernel-alignment between about zero-point-zero-one and zero-point-zero-seven across all forty-eight layers, while the structure channel reaches a centered-kernel-alignment of about zero-point-nine-nine with the all-modalities reference. This orthogonality is not because the function representation is degenerate — it retains cross-protein spread. The paper tests two alternative explanations and rejects both. One is granularity — function is whole-protein while the physical modalities are per-residue — but replacing whole-protein function with per-residue InterPro annotations, available for six hundred and thirty-one of the eight hundred and ninety-two proteins in the main dataset, does not change the picture. The per-residue functional annotation also stays largely orthogonal. The other is redundancy — maybe function stays orthogonal because the physical modalities already encode function, so the network faces little pressure to integrate the explicit channel. That prediction fails cleanly. Per-protein alignment between the function vector and the physical subspace is near zero for every protein, and it does not track whether the structure representation correctly decodes enzyme class or not. Proteins where structure fails to recover function align with the physical subspace no more strongly than proteins where structure succeeds. So the functional channel is held on a categorically separate axis regardless of whether that channel's information is redundant with the physical modalities. It is a representational choice, not a limit.

Fourth: the fusion is a learned property, and this is confirmed with a control that is worth naming because the field's default assumption might be otherwise. A model with the same architecture but random, untrained weights in the transformer trunk and the modality embeddings — while keeping the trained structure encoder so the inputs still carry meaning — shows no fusion. Both metrics stay flat across all forty-eight layers. So the depth-dependent shift into a shared subspace comes from training. It is not a mechanical consequence of summing the modality embeddings at the input.

Fifth: the fusion is universal across the tree of life. On a dataset of five thousand five hundred and fifty-five proteins from twelve organisms spanning all three superkingdoms — eukaryota, bacteria, and archaea — every one of the twelve individual organisms reaches its peak fusion at layer thirty-five, with a standard deviation of zero across organisms. The eukaryota, bacteria, and archaea curves are nearly superimposable. Fusion depth is also independent of protein length, with a Spearman correlation of negative zero-point-zero-four, and independent of AlphaFold prediction confidence, with a Spearman correlation of positive zero-point-zero-zero-three. Structural disorder does delay fusion, with the coil fraction correlating at positive zero-point-two-two and the helix fraction at negative zero-point-two-zero — well-folded helical proteins fuse earlier — but the magnitudes are modest.

Two more results worth naming, both from the causal side of the analysis. First, the variance reorganization is controlled rather than collapsed. Effective rank of the whole cloud drops to about three at the layer-twenty-four separation peak, expands to about eighty-five of a possible one thousand five hundred and thirty-six dimensions in the fusion zone, and then contracts. So the representation is neither approaching isotropy nor collapsing to a point — it is reshaping variance from between-condition to within-condition. Second, the paper runs a leave-one-modality-out causal test. Withholding the function input from the full model displaces the fused representation about six times less than withholding structure, sequence, or solvent accessibility. Median relative displacement at the fusion layer is zero-point-zero-two for function versus zero-point-zero-seven through zero-point-one-two for the physical modalities. So the fused physical representation is largely insensitive to the function input — the causal story matches the geometric story.

What are the concrete implications for antibody language modeling? Three. First, if you are considering E-S-M-3 as a backbone for antibody-specific fine-tuning — and there are groups pursuing exactly this — the layer you extract embeddings from is now much more informed. If you want the sequence signal isolated, take an early layer. If you want the shared multi-modal representation that has structural context folded in, take a layer at or past twenty-eight, and layer thirty-five is where the physical modalities are most fully fused. The universality across organisms is a positive signal here — the extraction layer transfers, though whether it transfers specifically to antibodies is not tested and someone should test it. Second, if you are hoping to feed functional-annotation information into E-S-M-3 to shape antibody predictions — say, InterPro or Gene Ontology labels for the antigen, or activity labels for the antibody itself — the network appears to hold that channel on a separate axis regardless. You are not going to get functional labels to modulate the physical representation through the standard input path. Any downstream tool that wants to condition on function will likely need to intervene explicitly, or lift function into the physical channels somehow. Third, this is a study of a general protein multimodal model on a general protein set, not on antibodies. Antibody sequences have distributional properties — heavy hypervariable-region variation, framework conservation, germline structure — that could interact with the fusion pattern in ways that this paper does not test. Whether antibody sequences fuse at the same depth, whether the complementarity-determining region residues integrate on the same schedule as framework residues, whether the paired heavy and light chains interact through the fusion — these are genuinely open questions that the framework laid out here makes easy to ask. Somebody should re-run this atlas on an antibody subset. It is a straightforward experiment on top of what the paper already provides — the code is on GitHub, the pipeline is configuration-driven, and the metrics are simple enough that the antibody-specific version is a one-week analysis. That is the natural follow-up.

A few limitations to keep in mind. The analysis uses one model — the openly available small E-S-M-3 with one-point-four billion parameters. Larger E-S-M-3 models are gated behind an interface, so whether fusion depth scales as a fraction of network depth with model capacity is genuinely unknown. The main dataset uses AlphaFold-predicted structures, but the fusion signature reproduces on a replication set of one hundred and seventy-seven proteins with experimental X-ray and cryo-electron microscopy coordinates, so the effect is not an artifact of predicted geometry. And the paper is careful to name that this is descriptive geometry rather than a mechanistic causal account. There is one causal ablation — the leave-one-modality-out displacement — but the paper does not intervene inside the network to prove that fusion causes any particular downstream behavior. The characterization is what modalities live where at what depth.

To close: a single-item spotlight tonight. Jacob Steenwyk at U-C Berkeley posts a full geometric atlas of how E-S-M-3 organizes its five input modalities across forty-eight layers. Physical modalities begin separate and collapse into a shared subspace between layers twenty-five and thirty-five, with sequence joining last after layer twenty-eight. The functional-annotation channel never fuses — it stays orthogonal at every layer regardless of whether its content is redundant with the physical modalities, and withholding it barely perturbs the fused representation. The fusion is learned rather than architectural, and layer thirty-five is the peak-fusion depth across every one of twelve organisms tested. For anyone using E-S-M-3 as a backbone for antibody-language-model work, this paper is a direct guide to which layer to draw from and what you are getting when you draw from it — and it points at a clean antibody-specific follow-up that is now cheap to run.
