## Script

Welcome to Affinity Matured, your daily brief on machine learning for antibody and protein design. Four items today: a structural immunology paper that turns the membrane-distal domain of the natural-killer-cell Fc receptor into a druggable allosteric checkpoint for antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity, with a direct engineering blueprint; a differentiable enzyme design method that pulls a turnover-number predictor into the sequence-optimization loop and is a template for how to do the same for antibody functional properties; a peer-reviewed Schrödinger benchmark showing that combining a protein language model with a physics-based free-energy method beats either alone for prioritizing stability-enhancing mutations; and a systematic evaluation of ninety-five zero-shot models that documents a hard limit — they cannot predict the functional impact of strong epistasis, which is exactly the regime that matters when you engineer antibodies with multiple co-varying mutations.

First item, and the lead today. On biorxiv, posted July sixth from the Murin group at San Diego Biomedical Research Institute, with Andrew Ward at Scripps, Emily Mace at Columbia, and Joseph Jardine, Michael Zwick, and Dennis Burton–adjacent collaborators — "The membrane distal domain of C-D-sixteen A allosterically regulates natural-killer-cell antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity." The first author is Tania Cid, with Monica Fernandez-Quintero as second. C-D-sixteen A, also called F-c-gamma-R-three A, is the low-affinity activating I-g-G receptor on natural killer cells. It is the receptor that reads the F-c portion of a therapeutic antibody and triggers the killing program that drug developers know as A-D-C-C, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity. Fc engineering — afucosylation, F-c mutations that improve F-c-gamma-R-three-A affinity, glyco-engineered platforms — is a huge industry precisely because pushing A-D-C-C harder is a lever for oncology antibodies. What has been consistently absent from that engineering story is a clean molecular picture of what actually turns the receptor on.

The paper argues the switch lives not at the F-c binding interface, but one domain away. C-D-sixteen A has two extracellular immunoglobulin-like domains, D-one distal from the membrane and D-two proximal, and I-g-G binds at the D-two-D-one hinge. The authors identify a nanobody, called C-twenty-eight, that binds a negatively charged patch on the membrane-distal D-one surface and dose-dependently blocks A-D-C-C against multiple therapeutic antibodies without perturbing the natural-killer cell's direct cytotoxicity — so the effect is antibody-mediated killing specifically, not general N-K cell function. A second nanobody, C-twenty-one, that binds an adjacent D-one epitope has no such effect, which rules out the trivial explanation that any D-one binder gets in the way. Cryo-electron-microscopy structures of the receptor with I-g-G and with the C-twenty-eight nanobody bound show that C-twenty-eight allosterically competes with core-fucosylated I-g-G — the low-A-D-C-C form — and stabilizes a closed D-one conformation that resembles the unliganded receptor even when the F-c is bound. Molecular dynamics simulations agree: occupying the D-one epitope rigidifies the I-g-G binding site, and stabilizes the receptor overall in a way that I-g-G binding alone does not.

There are two additional results that make this more than a structural biology paper. First, in resting natural-killer cells and in A-D-C-C-activated cells, the C-twenty-eight nanobody restricts C-D-three-zeta phosphorylation. That is the earliest signaling event on the receptor's cytoplasmic tail. The paper reads this as tonic inhibitory control that lives upstream of the signaling cascade, meaning D-one is not just a passive scaffold — it gates whether receptor engagement gets propagated. Second, using M-I-N-F-L-U-X super-resolution microscopy, they show that C-D-sixteen A forms dimers on the natural-killer-cell surface with an approximate nine-nanometer spacing, and that geometry is not altered by the A-D-C-C-enhancing L-forty-eight-H polymorphism — the human variant that shows up in immuno-oncology genotyping panels. The authors draw a structural analogy to the high-affinity I-g-E receptor F-c-epsilon-R-one, which is held inactive as a cholesterol-stabilized dimer, and propose that C-D-sixteen A dimerization through D-one contacts is a conserved autoinhibitory mechanism across F-c receptors.

Here is the engineering payload, and the reason this is the lead. They introduce structure-guided disruptions of the C-twenty-eight epitope into the D-one surface of C-D-sixteen A expressed in N-K-ninety-two cells — a natural-killer cell line used in cellular immunotherapy. Those cell-line variants show enhanced A-D-C-C potency and faster killing kinetics. For the antibody engineering audience the framing is worth restating: current Fc-engineered A-D-C-C boosters work on the antibody side. This paper says there is a matched lever on the receptor side, and it is a defined patch of surface residues on the effector cell that you can potentially disrupt — by mutation, by a small molecule if a druggable pocket presents, or by a receptor-engineering step in an off-the-shelf N-K cell therapeutic — and get more killing per antibody bound. That reframes A-D-C-C from a property of the antibody alone to a property of an antibody–receptor system where the receptor is engineerable. The paper is a preprint and the animal-model data are cell-line level, so this is a molecular blueprint rather than a clinical claim, but the mechanism is now specified well enough to design against.

Second item. Also on biorxiv, posted July sixth from John Zhang's group at Shenzhen University of Advanced Technology, with collaborators at Nanjing and Zhejiang — "CatESO: differentiable enzyme sequence optimization guided by substrate-aware K-cat prediction." First author Zhenjia Gan. The problem set-up will be familiar: turnover number, written K-cat and pronounced k-cat, is one of the two numbers you care about when you engineer an enzyme for a biocatalytic step, and it is famously hard to design for. Measured K-cat data are sparse compared to structural or binding data, and even when a deep-learning model predicts K-cat reasonably well, the standard pipeline is to generate sequences by some other method — an inverse-folding model, a diffusion model, a language-model sampler — and then score them post hoc with the K-cat predictor. That treats the objective as a filter rather than a driver.

CatESO puts the K-cat predictor into the design loop. The trick is continuous sequence relaxation: instead of hard tokens, the sequence at each position is a distribution over amino acids, and gradients from the K-cat predictor flow back through that distribution to update it. Two co-objectives are optimized alongside predicted K-cat. Evolutionary plausibility comes from E-S-M-two — the design is penalized for drifting into low-likelihood sequence space under the protein language model. Structural integrity comes from E-S-M-Fold — the p-L-D-D-T confidence of the folded prediction is a soft constraint that keeps the design foldable. So a single end-to-end gradient descent walks the sequence toward higher predicted turnover on a specific substrate while keeping it inside the evolutionary manifold and structurally credible.

The benchmark is seven out-of-distribution enzymes spanning enzyme commission classes one through seven, meaning oxidoreductases through translocases — the paper spans the E-C tree, not just one family. Across these, CatESO raised model-predicted K-cat for the vast majority of designs, with a median predicted fold change of one point five two, and every variant kept a p-L-D-D-T above seventy — the standard threshold for a confidently folded prediction. They compare against two strong baselines: an R-F-diffusion-three paired with Ligand-M-P-N-N pipeline, and Zym-Ctrl, a language-model-based enzyme designer. CatESO struck a better balance between predicted activity and structural confidence than either. No wet-lab measurement of the designed variants is reported in the preprint — the "improvement" throughout is model-predicted K-cat and model-predicted foldability. That's the honest limitation of a purely differentiable design paper without an accompanying experimental validation loop.

Why include this on a show about antibodies. The result to internalize is not about enzymes. It is that if you have a differentiable predictor of any protein-level functional property — binding affinity, developability, viscosity, aggregation, self-association, expression titer — and it is well-behaved enough that gradients through it point in a useful direction, then the CatESO pattern is a general recipe. You wrap the predictor in a continuous-relaxation optimization, add an E-S-M-family evolutionary regularizer and a folding regularizer, and turn the predictor from a filter into a driver. For antibody discovery, the field mostly still does structure-based generative design plus post-hoc developability scoring. This paper is a compact demonstration that you can pull the developability objective — or the binding objective — inside the loop, at least for properties whose predictors are differentiable. The paired R-F-diffusion-Ligand-M-P-N-N baseline being outperformed is a milestone worth flagging on its own.

Third item. In Protein Engineering, Design and Selection, published online July sixth from Elizabeth Rhodes at Colorado Boulder with Guido Scarabelli, Jennifer Jou, Kimberly Sprenger, and Elly Oloo at Schrödinger — "Prioritizing stability-enhancing mutations using the E-S-M protein language model in conjunction with physics-based M-M-slash-G-B-S-A predictions." M-M-slash-G-B-S-A, spelled out for the ear, is molecular mechanics with generalized Born surface area — a fast implicit-solvent free-energy method that has been an industry workhorse for decades in small-molecule and increasingly in protein energetics. The paper is a benchmark. The dataset is one hundred seventy-four thousand nine hundred forty-five mutations across one hundred eighty distinct proteins — that is genuinely large by protein-stability benchmark standards — and the question is which of two triaging tools better prioritizes single-point mutations that improve thermodynamic stability.

The headline numbers. The M-M-slash-G-B-S-A method alone achieves a mean R-O-C area-under-the-curve of zero point six eight five across the benchmark. The E-S-M protein language model, used in its log-odds form — that is, the ratio of the language model's likelihood of the mutant amino acid to the wild-type at that position — achieves zero point five nine seven alone. But a combined re-ranking, using both methods in a complementary way, gets to zero point seven four three. The gain over M-M-slash-G-B-S-A alone is roughly six percentage points of A-U-C, which is a meaningful jump on a benchmark this size. The paper's diagnosis is that the two methods have different failure modes: E-S-M's biases are evolutionary — it knows about mutations that resemble natural variation and less about mutations that don't — while M-M-slash-G-B-S-A's biases are energetic and geometric — it captures interaction changes at the mutated site but is blind to broader sequence context. The complementarity is what generates the combined lift.

Why this matters for antibody engineering, and why it belongs in this show and not just in a general protein-design outlet. Stability triage is a canonical antibody developability step. When you affinity-mature or humanize a variable region, some subset of your candidate mutations destabilize the domain, and the standard workflow is to run a triage — expressed antibody thermostability, D-S-F, chromatography — that costs time and material. A computational triage that is even seventy-four A-U-C on a large mutational benchmark is a serious pre-filter, and it is orders of magnitude cheaper. There are two caveats worth stating. First, the benchmark is generic proteins, not antibodies specifically, and antibody variable domains have their own stability idiosyncrasies — the disulfide constraints, the F-R-C-D-R interfaces, the paired heavy-light interactions — that a benchmark like this does not stress. Second, single-mutation A-U-C is the easy case; combined mutations are the case you actually run in antibody engineering, and that is not what was scored. Still, the result — that neither the P-L-M alone nor the physics alone dominates, but the combination beats both by a clean margin — is exactly the kind of practical, benchmarked result that the field needed a published baseline for.

Fourth and final item. A version-two update posted July sixth to a February biorxiv preprint from Olga Kalinina's group at the Helmholtz Institute for Pharmaceutical Research Saarland, with Fyodor Kondrashov at Okinawa I-S-T — "Beyond additivity: zero-shot methods cannot predict the impact of epistasis on protein properties and function." Anastasia Kolchina is first author. This one is a systematic negative result and it is worth listening to precisely because it draws a bright line under a limitation that the antibody-language-model community sometimes glosses over.

The setup: they evaluated ninety-five zero-shot models — zero-shot meaning no task-specific supervised training on the label of interest, so protein language models scored by mask-fill likelihood, structure-based methods, and evolutionary methods, the whole current toolkit — on datasets from Protein-Gym, the standard mutational-effect benchmark. The question is not "can these models predict single-mutation effects" — that is the easy case and the answer is broadly yes — but rather "when you combine multiple mutations that are known to be epistatic, does the model predict the combined effect." Epistasis, as a reminder, is when the effect of one mutation depends on the presence of another; if two mutations each shift a functional readout by amount X on their own, epistasis is any deviation of the combined shift from two X.

The finding is that current zero-shot methods do well on single mutations and on non-epistatic combinations, and they fail on strongly epistatic combinations. The failure is not subtle — the paper's argument is that these models are essentially additive at the level of predicting mutational effects, and that additivity is what breaks. The implication for our audience is direct. Affinity maturation is epistasis. When you mutate two contact residues in a paratope, the effect on binding is rarely the sum of the two singles. When you humanize a variable domain, framework substitutions and back-mutations interact with C-D-R sequence in ways that are the definitional case of epistasis. A zero-shot P-L-M that scores each proposed mutation independently and adds up the scores will be systematically wrong on the combined variant, and the size of the error will scale with the strength of the epistatic coupling. This paper says, quantitatively across ninety-five models, that this is the state of the art and it does not solve epistasis. Practically: if you are using a zero-shot P-L-M to rank multi-mutation antibody variants, you should validate on epistatic held-out combinations, not on the median single-mutation A-U-C, or you will be measuring the wrong thing. And it is a call to the modeling community — capturing joint mutational effects is the next hill, not a solved problem.
