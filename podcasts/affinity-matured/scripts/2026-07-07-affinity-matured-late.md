Welcome back to Affinity Matured — this is a short late-afternoon add-on to today's earlier episodes. One preprint went up on bio-archive this morning that lands squarely in the crosshairs of AI antibody engineering and broadly neutralizing influenza work, and it's worth a dedicated segment even though it's brand-new enough that only the abstract has rendered so far. I'll be explicit about that: the take here is drawn from the abstract only, and I'll note where that leaves questions open.

The paper is titled "Breaking the Synthesis Barrier for AI-Designed DNA Libraries," out of E-T-H Zurich, first author Scott Sussex, with corresponding authors Sai Reddy — the antibody-engineering group that has shipped a run of language-model, generative, and screening-driven antibody papers over the last few years — and Andreas Krause, whose lab works on Bayesian optimization and probabilistic machine learning. That pairing tells you what to expect: this is a method that sits between a generative sequence model on one side and a real wet-lab screen on the other, and treats the library itself as the thing to optimize.

The problem statement is genuinely important, and it's one that has been quietly limiting how much AI antibody design actually gets tested at scale. Modern generative models — protein language models, inverse-folding models, diffusion-based binder designers — can propose specific sequences that are predicted in silico to be effective. But if you want to test more than a handful of those sequences, you run into what the authors call the synthesis barrier: deterministic D-N-A synthesis of thousands or millions of specific sequences is expensive, and beyond a certain scale it becomes prohibitive. Meanwhile, on the wet-lab side, high-throughput multiplexed screens — think yeast display, mammalian display, deep mutational scanning readouts — can measure the function of billions of sequences in a single experiment. To feed those screens, the field has historically used randomized libraries: NNK codons at particular positions, degenerate primers, error-prone P-C-R. Those are cheap and scale to enormous diversity, but they're chosen heuristically. You sacrifice control for scale, and most of the diversity you buy is wasted on sequences you didn't actually want to test.

Their proposal, called P-G-L-D — Policy Gradients for Library Design — is to close that gap by making the stochastic library itself a differentiable, optimizable object. You parametrize a randomized library in a synthesis-aware way — meaning the parametrization respects what a D-N-A synthesis technology can actually make and how much it costs — and then you use policy gradients to optimize the parameters of the library distribution against a specified objective function. The objective could be a predicted-fitness function from a generative model, or a signal from a previous round of screening, or a coverage-and-diversity target. Because the library is stochastic but its parameters are learned, you get something in between the two poles that have dominated the field: it's not a fixed list of hand-picked sequences, but it's also not a blind degenerate library — every base at every position has been tuned to concentrate mass where the model or the prior round of data says it should be.

Two applications get called out in the abstract. First, lab-in-the-loop design of multi-round high-throughput experiments — so you screen, feed the results back, re-optimize the library parameters, and synthesize the next round. That's the closed loop the field has wanted for a while. Second, large-scale in-vitro D-N-A sampling from generative models — which is the "generative model proposes, synthesizable library disposes" version. Instead of just picking the top-N sequences your model likes, you're building a library that reflects the model's distribution as faithfully as your synthesis technology allows.

The headline demonstration is the one that puts this in Affinity Matured's territory: they use P-G-L-D to design a library of roughly ten to the sixth — so on the order of a million — unique sequences that they synthesize for about seven hundred U-S dollars, to explore the mutation space of a broadly neutralizing influenza antibody. A million sequences at seven hundred dollars is a per-sequence cost that puts serious combinatorial mutational scanning of a broadly neutralizing antibody within reach of an academic budget. If it holds up, this is a way to interrogate the fitness landscape around a b-n-Ab at a scale that until now would have been prohibitively expensive to specify without paying for random diversity you didn't want.

Now, the honest limitations from an abstract-only read: we don't yet know from the abstract what the objective function was for the flu antibody library — was it a predicted binding score, a diversity target around specific C-D-R positions, a language-model likelihood? We don't know whether the library was actually screened yet, or what the enrichment looked like. We don't know how the synthesis-aware parametrization handles constraints like paired heavy and light chain co-variation, or whether it's currently restricted to a single variable region. Those are the questions I'll be looking to answer once the full text renders. But the framing — treat the library, not just the sequence, as the design variable, and connect it end-to-end to both the generative model and the screen — is the right framing, and the flu b-n-Ab demonstration is exactly the kind of concrete application this line of work has been missing.
