## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured. Three items today, and they cluster tightly around a single problem — the geometry of where an antibody sits on its antigen, and how far off deep-learning models still are from getting that geometry right. First, Bepi-Pocket and Disco-Pocket, from the Nielsen group in Copenhagen, indexed to PubMed today as a Protein Science paper — the idea is to take an antibody-agnostic B-cell epitope predictor, use it to place a soft distance restraint on Chai-one at each of thirty inference runs, and watch what happens. The performance gain over Chai-one with just random-seed variation is substantial, and the paper's most interesting result is not the headline accuracy number — it is that antigen modeling quality dominates everything else. Second, L-F three-D Ro-P-E, from Soochow University, on arXiv this Sunday — a small architectural change that swaps sequence-order rotary position encoding inside a transformer for a residue-local three-dimensional rotary encoding, applied to antibody-specific epitope prediction, and gets state-of-the-art Matthews correlation coefficient on the A-S-E-P benchmark under both splits. Third, a preprint from the Amy Keating lab at M-I-T that arrived this morning, on seed-guided R-F-diffusion for de novo binder design — the specific case is a designed antitoxin for the bacterial toxin Rel-E, and the framing is that current diffusion-based binder generators produce topologically narrow, mostly helical designs, and that guiding backbone generation with P-D-B-derived seed fragments unlocks substantially more diverse, more extensive interfaces.

Two of these — the Nielsen paper and the Soochow paper — are full-text reads. The Keating lab preprint went up this morning and the bioRxiv full-text render has not caught up yet, so what I say about it is grounded in the abstract, the funder disclosure, and the author list. First item. Protein Science, published online with an August indexing date, and the associated bioRxiv preprint updated to version two in late September 2025. "Pocket Restraints Guided by B-Cell Epitope Prediction improves Chai-one Antibody–Antigen Structure Modeling," from Joseph Clifford and Morten Nielsen at the Technical University of Denmark. The context is that Chai-one — and its close relatives AlphaFold-three and Boltz-two — have brought antibody-antigen structure prediction into a regime where a fraction of complexes come out with correct interfaces on the first attempt, but the failure mode has an unusually specific shape. When you re-run Chai-one on the same antibody-antigen pair with thirty different random seeds, the model does not explore thirty different binding modes — it collapses to one or two, and if that one is the wrong site on the antigen, no amount of re-sampling recovers the correct interface. The commercial deployment of these tools papers over this by having users supply experimental restraints — a known epitope patch, a mutagenesis hotspot — and both Chai and Boltz accept those as soft pocket restraints during inference. But most real prospective users do not have experimental restraint data; they have a sequence.

The paper's proposal is that antibody-agnostic B-cell epitope predictors — specifically Bepi-Pred three-point-oh and Disco-Tope three-point-oh, both trained on solved antibody-antigen structures — can play the role of that missing experimental restraint. Bepi-Pred three-point-oh is sequence-based, a feedforward network on top of E-S-M-two embeddings; Disco-Tope three-point-oh is structure-based, an X-G-Boost classifier over E-S-M-inverse-folding embeddings from a predicted antigen structure. Neither one uses antibody information — they score antigen residues for how likely any B-cell antibody would recognize that residue. The Chai-one pocket-restraint machinery accepts a single residue on the antigen side and a chain on the antibody side, and asks the model to keep some atom of the specified chain within a distance cutoff of the specified residue. So the pipeline is straightforward: rank antigen residues by the epitope-predictor score, and for each of thirty Chai-one inference runs, use the next-ranked residue as the restraint, requiring both the light and heavy chains to sit within ten angstroms of it. Because different top-scoring residues drive Chai-one into different binding pockets, you get thirty structurally distinct proposals instead of thirty near-copies.

The benchmarking dataset is one thousand six hundred twenty-eight non-redundant antibody-antigen complexes from S-Ab-Dab, filtered to Chai-one's two-thousand-forty-eight-residue input limit. Success is a Dock-Q score above the CAPRI thresholds — acceptable at zero-point-two-three, medium at zero-point-four-nine, high at zero-point-eight. The primary comparison is Chai-one with thirty random seeds versus Chai-one plus Bepi-Pocket or Disco-Pocket at thirty restraint iterations, each producing one hundred fifty structures per complex. In the baseline no-M-S-A configuration where Chai-one uses only its internal E-S-M-two embeddings, Bepi-Pocket beats seed variation by four points at the acceptable threshold, four points at medium, and two points at high — modest gains, but structurally clean gains. When multiple-sequence-alignment input is added — which by itself roughly doubles Chai-one's success rate by improving antigen modeling — the numbers become forty-eight percent acceptable, forty percent medium, twelve percent high for Bepi-Pocket versus forty-two, thirty-three, twelve for Chai-one with M-S-A alone. Disco-Pocket then pushes those to fifty, forty-one, and fifteen. So the absolute deltas from restraints are five to eight points across CAPRI tiers, on top of a doubling from adding M-S-As, in a benchmark where the leading tools sit around forty to fifty percent acceptable.

Two things about this paper are the actual take-home. First, epitope redundancy — the median antigen-intersection-over-union across the one hundred fifty structures generated per complex — is more than five times lower for Bepi-Pocket than for seed variation, going from zero-point-five-six down to zero-point-one. The model is exploring diverse epitope sites when guided, and collapsing onto redundant sites when it is not. Second, and this is the finding that shapes how you should read the whole method, the benefit of the pocket restraint scales with how well Chai-one can model the antigen structure on its own. Restricted to the subset of complexes where the antigen root-mean-square deviation from crystal is under one angstrom, Bepi-Pocket delivers a ten-point acceptable-Dock-Q gain over seed variation; on complexes with antigen R-M-S-D over four angstroms, the gain shrinks to two points. If the antigen is wrong, no amount of pocket-restraint guidance can put the antibody in the right place. So the practical priority for anyone running Chai-one or AlphaFold-three on a novel target is antigen modeling quality first, and epitope-guided restraints second — which also means anyone with high-quality structural data on the antigen, even without paired antibody information, is in a substantially better position than someone starting from just the antibody-antigen sequence pair.

One more result worth naming. The authors held out an independent test set of one hundred five antibody-antigen complexes released after the training cutoffs of Bepi-Pred, Disco-Tope, and Chai-one — the "After" subset — and re-ran the comparison. Absolute accuracy on this subset dropped substantially — the peer-review discussion in the Protein Science version calls this "nearly halved" — but the relative gain of Bepi-Pocket and Disco-Pocket over seed-based Chai-one remained consistent. This matters because it separates two things that could have been confused: the field-wide accuracy inflation from deep-learning models training on the same P-D-B data everyone benchmarks against, and the specific incremental value of a method built on top of that base. The pocket-restraint value is robust; the base accuracy is not. Both truths belong in the same sentence.

The code is on GitHub under the mniel-Lab organization, free to use. For anyone running Chai-one on new antibody-antigen sequence pairs today, the smallest workflow change with the largest expected accuracy gain is: predict the antigen in isolation with a decent M-S-A, predict Bepi-Pred or Disco-Tope epitope scores from that structure, and run thirty Chai-one iterations with the top-thirty scored residues as pocket restraints. That is not a new architecture — it is a wrapper — and it moves your acceptable-Dock-Q hit rate by five to ten points depending on the antigen. Small paper, actionable finding.

Second item. arXiv, category cs.A-I, posted August second. "Inter-Residue Geometry Attention for Antibody-Specific Epitope Prediction," from Chuanliu Fan, Nan Yu, Junjie Wu, and Guohong Fu at the School of Computer Science and Technology and the Institute of Artificial Intelligence at Soochow University. This is a pure methods paper, and it targets the same task the Nielsen paper touches from the epitope-prediction side — given a paired antibody and antigen, classify each antigen surface residue as an epitope contact or not.

The observation the paper starts from is a modeling-choice mismatch that is not usually named explicitly. Standard transformer attention uses positional encoding derived from token order — most commonly rotary position embedding, or R-o-P-E, which rotates query and key vector pairs by an angle proportional to token index difference. For language modeling this is fine. For folded proteins it is not, because two residues that are twenty positions apart in sequence can be one angstrom apart in three-space, and antibody-antigen recognition is entirely a function of the three-space geometry, not the sequence separation. Existing epitope-prediction methods — WALLE, PEP-Net — add structural information as an auxiliary node feature through a separate graph encoder or a point-cloud encoder, but they still leave positional encoding inside the transformer tied to sequence order.

The authors' proposal is to replace the sequence-order rotary encoding entirely with a three-dimensional rotary encoding that is expressed in each residue's own backbone frame. For each residue, they construct a local coordinate frame from the nitrogen, alpha-carbon, and carbonyl carbon atoms of the backbone. For any pair of residues i and j, the displacement from residue i's alpha-carbon to residue j's alpha-carbon is expressed in residue i's local frame, giving three coordinates — one along each local backbone axis. Each of those three axes gets its own bank of rotary channel pairs at multiple spatial frequencies, and the standard rotary matrix is applied at an angle equal to frequency times displacement. So instead of rotating query and key channels by "how many tokens apart are these two residues" the model rotates them by "how far, and in which local-backbone direction, is one residue from the other." The paper calls this Local-Frame three-D Ro-P-E, or L-F three-D Ro-P-E. Crucially, because the frames co-rotate with the protein when the whole complex is rotated globally, the resulting attention logits are invariant to arbitrary global rigid motions — the model does not have to learn that invariance from data augmentation, it is baked in.

The evaluation is on A-S-E-P, the standard antibody-specific epitope benchmark, which is one thousand seven hundred twenty-three curated antibody-antigen complexes from the Antibody Database, split three ways — one thousand three hundred eighty-three training, one hundred seventy validation, one hundred seventy test — under two splitting protocols. The easier ratio split keeps epitope-to-surface ratios balanced across the three sets; the harder group split ensures that test-set epitope groups do not appear in training. Under the ratio split, L-F three-D Ro-P-E hits an M-C-C of zero-point-four-one, versus zero-point-four-oh for PEP-Net — the previous best M-C-C baseline — and zero-point-three-two for a global-frame three-D variant that keeps three-D geometry but uses the arbitrary P-D-B axes as its coordinate frame. Under the harder group split, L-F three-D Ro-P-E hits zero-point-one-seven M-C-C versus zero-point-one-six for PEP-Net-plus-language-embedding and zero-point-one-six for the global-frame variant. The gaps are small in absolute terms — antibody-specific epitope prediction is a genuinely hard task and every method sits in the same low-M-C-C regime — but the ranking is consistent across metrics and across splits, and the ablation is instructive: swapping one-D sequence R-o-P-E for global-frame three-D R-o-P-E without the local-frame trick only gains a couple of M-C-C points, and then swapping global-frame for local-frame gains a few more. Simply injecting three-D coordinates is not enough; the geometry has to be expressed in a frame that co-rotates with the protein.

The paper's most useful downstream experiment is a mutation-ranking analysis on six therapeutic-antibody targets from the Absci Ig-Design benchmark — targets like A-C-V-R-two-B, C-five, F-X-I, I-L-thirty-six-R, T-S-L-P, and T-N-F-R-S-F-nine — each with a library of heavy-chain complementarity-determining-region variants of a parental antibody, all with surface plasmon resonance-measured K-D values. For each variant, the authors predict the heavy-chain variable domain with Ig-Fold, superimpose it onto the parental complex, run L-F three-D Ro-P-E to get antigen epitope probabilities, and score the variant by how well those probabilities align with the ground-truth parental epitope. They then correlate the alignment score with negative-log-K-D. Compared to generative-model log-likelihood baselines like Diff-Ab-X-L, AntiFold, and Ig-L-M, which flip sign across targets and provide no transferable ranking signal, L-F three-D Ro-P-E is the only method with positive Spearman correlation across all six targets, statistically significant on the largest cohort. The correlations individually are modest — the largest is zero-point-three for T-S-L-P — but the consistency of sign is what matters, because that is what tells you whether an epitope-alignment score can be used as a transferable filter for variants in prospective library design. Read at face value: this is a small architectural change with a clean invariance argument that seems to learn structurally meaningful antibody-antigen compatibility, not just epitope-surface statistics.

Third item. bioRxiv, posted this morning, August fourth. "Seed-Guided De Novo Design Expands the Structural Diversity of Antitoxin Protein Binders," from the Amy Keating lab at M-I-T with collaborators at the Broad Institute — first author Dustin Britton, last author Amy Keating. Because the preprint went up this morning and the bioRxiv full-text rendering pipeline has not finished, I am reading from the abstract and metadata; the body of the paper is not yet publicly accessible. So a shorter and more measured take than the first two items.

The framing is the current failure mode of diffusion-based de novo binder design. R-F-diffusion, AlphaProteo, and their close relatives are extremely effective at producing functional binders against simple, mostly-alpha-helical target surfaces — the target surfaces the field has spent the last two years benchmarking on. Where these methods struggle is extended interaction surfaces with multiple discontinuous binding pockets, and non-helical topology requirements. Diffusion models trained on the P-D-B are biased toward producing three- and four-helix bundles because those are structurally the most stable and the most common in the training data, and that bias means the generated binder ensemble tends to be topologically narrow — most successful designs look like small helical bundles with a couple of loops.

The Keating lab's proposal is to guide backbone generation with P-D-B-derived seed fragments — small, three-dimensional structural motifs pulled from the P-D-B on the basis of geometric complementarity to the target surface. The seed becomes a fixed motif that R-F-diffusion has to scaffold around, in the same motif-scaffolding regime that R-F-diffusion was designed to support — the innovation is that the motifs are not user-provided or from the natural binder, they are algorithmically selected from the P-D-B by matching local surface complementarity to the target. The test case is Rel-E, a bacterial toxin, whose natural antitoxin Rel-B engages two distinct interfaces with high surface complementarity. That's a deliberate choice — a target where a two-site extended interaction surface is known to be biologically important, and where the reference binder Rel-B pep is a well-characterized small peptide binder they can compare against.

The results, from what the abstract discloses. Seed-guided R-F-diffusion produced backbones with substantially higher structural diversity and more target contacts than R-F-diffusion alone — that is the topological-diversity claim, and unfortunately without seeing the figures I cannot tell you whether "substantially" means a small shift in the topology histogram or a genuinely different design landscape. One thousand four hundred two designs were experimentally screened in a high-throughput bacterial survival assay, which is the appropriate readout for an antitoxin against a bacterial toxin — the toxin kills the host cell, a functional antitoxin binder rescues it. Multiple functional binders came out of the screen, including variants with nanomolar to low-micromolar affinity, and one design with Rel-E neutralization activity comparable to Rel-B pep itself — meaning the designed binder is not just functional but competitive with the natural antitoxin. Structure prediction and mutational analyses of the top designs support that they engage Rel-E through seed-derived contacts, not by mimicking Rel-B's binding mode — the designs adopt distinct topologies that reach the target surface through different geometries. Molecular dynamics and hydrogen-deuterium exchange further suggest that one of the highest-affinity designs undergoes a conformational change upon binding — an induced-fit interaction rather than a rigid lock-and-key — which is a genuinely novel outcome for a de novo design. And separately, the successful designs showed reduced cross-reactivity to Rel-E orthologs compared with Rel-B pep — the extended, seed-derived interfaces are more selective, presumably because they engage more idiosyncratic surface features that vary between orthologs.

For the antibody-engineering audience, the direct relevance is that R-F-antibody, which is the antibody-specific fork of R-F-diffusion, inherits the same topological-diversity problem — the generated complementarity-determining-region loop geometries and the antibody-target interfaces are constrained by what the diffusion prior has seen in the P-D-B. Seed-based motif scaffolding is a natural way to inject external constraints into that prior, and while this paper does not touch antibodies directly, the demonstration on Rel-E with a bacterial-toxin binder is a clean methodological proof-of-concept. Watch for a follow-up applying the seed-guided approach to antibody or nanobody design against extended surface epitopes — the H-I-V envelope trimer glycan shield, the influenza hemagglutinin stem base, the SARS-CoV-two spike receptor-binding domain interface, all of which are exactly the sort of extended, non-helix-compatible surfaces where existing diffusion methods have struggled.
