## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. This is the night edition for July thirteenth — after a thin morning and a stronger afternoon, the day has delivered a third batch of substantive items. Three tonight. First, a five-hundred-million-parameter decoder-only D-N-A language model for B-cell-receptor sequences that outperforms both a state-of-the-art antibody language model and a much larger general-purpose protein language model at repertoire embedding, applied to identifying multiple-sclerosis-associated B-cell-receptors from over eleven million archival sequences. Second, an R-F-diffusion-plus-Protein-M-P-N-N motif-scaffolding pipeline that converts a natural weak binder — a fragment of the N-Myc transactivation domain — into single-digit-nanomolar miniprotein inhibitors of Aurora-A kinase, with crystal structures explaining what makes some designs work and others not. Third, an interpretable machine-learning framework that identifies shared and vaccine-specific immune architectures underlying antibody responses across influenza, hepatitis-B, and yellow-fever cohorts totalling over a thousand participants.

The first item is a bioRxiv preprint posted July thirteenth, titled "Language Model Embedding Classifiers Enable Identification of Multiple Sclerosis-Associated B-Cell Receptors and Repertoires." First author Graham Peet, corresponding authors Arjun Krishnan and Wendy Macklin, at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus in Aurora. This is one of the more thoughtfully engineered antibody-language-model papers to come across the queue recently, and there are three specific pieces of the methodology worth internalising.

First, the data. The group did an exhaustive reanalysis of every human R-N-A-sequencing run in the N-C-B-I Sequence Read Archive matching "multiple sclerosis" as of December twenty twenty-four. Metadata was sparse and often had to be recovered from the original publications by hand. They ran T-R-U-S-T-four — a repertoire-assembly tool designed for unselected bulk R-N-A sequencing — over the filtered files, and combined the output with public unpaired B-cell-receptor data from the Observed Antibody Space repository and several in-house multiple-sclerosis and neuromyelitis-optica sequencing sets. The result is roughly eleven-and-a-half million B-cell-receptor heavy-chain sequences from about eight hundred distinct patients, with two-point-eight million high-quality sequences carrying full V, D, and J gene-segment assemblies. What matters here is not the raw sequence count but the heterogeneity — the samples span many tissue sources, sequencing technologies, geographies, and clinical states, which is the exact kind of diversity that most published antibody-language-model training sets lack.

Second, the model. Rather than start from an existing antibody protein language model like Ab-Lang or AntiBERTy, the group trained a new foundation model from scratch, and made three unorthodox choices. First choice: they trained at the nucleotide level rather than the amino-acid level — a D-N-A language model rather than a protein language model — reasoning that D-N-A tokens preserve information about codon bias and silent somatic mutations that get erased by translation to protein. Second choice: they used a decoder-only architecture based on downscaled Mistral, which is unusual for embedding work — most B-cell-receptor language models are encoders — but keeping the decoder allows the embedding model to double as a next-token predictor without discarding half the parameters. Third choice: they scaled up. Prior B-cell-receptor language models are on the order of ten to a hundred million parameters. The group trained three variants — one hundred million, three hundred million, and five hundred million parameters — and picked the largest, which they call B-C-R-Mistral. The training corpus was compact — about two million heterogeneous sequences selected by iterative random sampling in seven-mer Euclidean space, an efficient way of building a diverse pretraining set without the compute cost of full-corpus training.

Third — and this is the practical result — the model was benchmarked against two strong baselines: E-S-M-two at six-hundred-fifty million parameters, a larger general-purpose protein language model, and Ab-RoBERTa, a recent state-of-the-art antibody protein language model. Benchmarks measured relative distances in embedding space between sequences from patients with the same versus different diseases, plus cosine similarity across same-disease-different-patient sequence pairs. B-C-R-Mistral outperformed both baselines on those clustering tasks, and — importantly — a protein-tokenised variant of the same architecture trained on the same corpus performed worse than the D-N-A variant, supporting the claim that nucleotide-level tokenisation is doing real work rather than being redundant with amino-acid tokenisation.

The downstream application — classifying multiple-sclerosis-associated B-cell-receptors — is where the pieces come together. A three-layer multilayer perceptron was trained on last-token embeddings to assign each sequence to one of five categories: healthy, autoimmune, infectious, other-disease, or multiple sclerosis. The training strategy included a custom loss the group calls "dynamic exclusion." It progressively excludes multiple-sclerosis sequences that get confidently classified as non-multiple-sclerosis from the loss calculation, on the argument that in a real repertoire most sequences are not disease-specific and forcing the model to explain them corrupts the signal. This is analogous to semi-hard example mining, and it raised classification precision on held-out test data by a meaningful margin. Cross-validation folds were grouped by original study — not by patient — which is a stricter form of leave-one-out and revealed considerable study-driven artifacts in earlier draft models. The paper is unusually explicit about these methodological failure modes, which is refreshing.

At the patient-repertoire level, the group used a clever trick to convert variable-length repertoires into fixed-dimensional inputs. One hundred and twenty-eight "waypoints" are optimised in the model's hidden-state space using a joint reconstruction and contrastive objective, and each repertoire is then encoded as a one-hundred-and-twenty-eight-dimensional vector of mean softmax-normalised distances from those waypoints. A gradient-boosted forest — X-G-Boost — trained on waypoint embeddings combined with per-sequence multiple-sclerosis-probability predictions achieved R-O-C area-under-the-curve above zero-point-eight for distinguishing multiple sclerosis from healthy or infectious repertoires, with lower but still meaningful performance against other autoimmune diseases. Performance scaled with per-patient sequence depth — repertoires with under a hundred sequences were nearly useless, repertoires with over a thousand were where the signal held.

Two things anchor the work as more than a methods demonstration. First, the model correctly ranked three of four known myelin-binding immunoglobulin sequences — antibodies previously cloned from multiple-sclerosis cerebrospinal-fluid plasmablasts and shown to bind conformational proteolipid-protein-one on oligodendrocytes — above an isotype control by multiple-sclerosis-association probability, without those sequences ever having been in the training set. Binding was independently validated by flow cytometry against O-four-positive oligodendrocytes. Second, applied longitudinally to patients before and after treatment with fingolimod or natalizumab, the model detected treatment-consistent drops in disease-associated repertoire fraction, matching the known effects of those drugs on circulating B-cell populations. Neither longitudinal comparison reached statistical significance individually because of small paired-patient counts, but the direction is consistent across drugs.

Limitations flagged by the group include the archival nature of the data — undersampled repertoires for many patients — and the absence of paired light chains and T-cell-receptors, both of which likely encode complementary information. Code will be released upon publication; model weights and processed datasets go to Zenodo. For the audience of this show, the transferable pieces are nucleotide-level tokenisation for antibody-repertoire language models, the dynamic-exclusion loss trick for training on repertoires with dominant non-disease background, and the waypoint-based fixed-dimensional repertoire encoding. All three should be portable to therapeutic-antibody discovery workflows where the target is not a disease-association classifier but, for example, an antigen-specific-binder classifier trained on sorted enrichments.

The second item is another bioRxiv preprint posted July thirteenth, titled "Selective miniprotein inhibitors of Aurora-A kinase designed using interaction-motif scaffolding." First author Jennifer Miles, corresponding author Richard Bayliss at the University of Leeds, with contributions from the Gergely lab at the University of Oxford. This is a wet-lab-heavy application of the standard R-F-diffusion-plus-Protein-M-P-N-N-plus-AlphaFold-two design stack to a hard target, and the reason it belongs on today's list is the specific set of methodological details that separated the potent binders from the failures — with crystal structures of both the successful and unsuccessful designs.

The problem. Small-molecule Aurora-A inhibitors have historically struggled with selectivity because Aurora-A and its paralog Aurora-B differ by only one amino acid in the A-T-P-binding pocket, and most compounds hit both. The group asked whether targeting the substrate-recognition surface rather than the A-T-P pocket could give selective inhibition. There are no crystal structures of Aurora-A bound to a substrate peptide, but there is a published structure of Aurora-A bound to a fragment of the N-Myc transactivation domain, residues 61 to 89, which engages what would be the P-plus-one pocket of the substrate-binding site. That N-Myc fragment binds Aurora-A weakly — around twelve micromolar in fluorescence polarisation — but the interaction is structurally defined, which makes it a viable starting point for motif scaffolding.

The design pipeline. R-F-diffusion was used to scaffold two variants of the N-Myc helix: residues 73 through 84, the core interfacial region, and residues 73 through 89, the full helix. Aurora-A residues in contact with N-Myc were provided as hotspots. Between ten and fifty flanking residues were added at each terminus by diffusion. Roughly thirty-nine hundred backbones were generated across the two motif sets. Protein-M-P-N-N assigned one or two sequences per backbone. AlphaFold-two with the initial-guess protocol validated the designs, with filters on interface predicted-aligned-error below ten and mean binder p-L-D-D-T above eighty-four. That gave a two-and-a-half to three per cent hit rate. Seventeen designs were selected for wet-lab testing — a strikingly small screening batch by the standards of most de novo binder papers.

The wet-lab results. Fifteen of the seventeen designs expressed in E. coli. Eight of the fifteen inhibited Aurora-A kinase activity in an A-D-P-Glo assay well enough to fit an I-C-fifty. The top two, called D-B-S-one and D-B-S-five, had I-C-fifties of twelve and sixteen nanomolar, respectively. Isothermal titration calorimetry gave dissociation constants ranging from zero-point-nine nanomolar to seven-hundred-eighty-seven nanomolar across the successful binders. D-B-S-one was the tightest, at sub-nanomolar. That's roughly a thousand-fold gain in affinity over the starting N-Myc peptide.

The methodological punchline comes from the crystal structures. The group crystallised nine of the seventeen designs in complex with a kinase-dead cysteine-free Aurora-A variant, at resolutions between one-point-seven-nine and two-point-nine angstroms. Comparing successful and unsuccessful designs revealed a convergent structural signature. Nearly all high-affinity designs — despite architectures ranging from three-helix bundles to five-helix bundles — engaged Aurora-A through a common combination of two features: extensive interactions between an appended C-terminal helix and the alpha-G helix of the kinase, plus a hydrogen bond or main-chain contact to the kinase's activation loop. Weaker designs had shorter C-terminal helices that bent away from the alpha-G surface, or lacked activation-loop contacts. Making a single point mutation, histidine-fifty-five to alanine, in the highest-affinity three-helix bundle D-B-L-five raised its I-C-fifty from thirty-one to four-hundred-twenty-five nanomolar — a more than ten-fold loss from removing one hydrogen bond to the activation loop. That kind of single-mutation confirmation of a design hypothesis is unusual and useful.

Selectivity was excellent. In an equivalent A-D-P-Glo assay against Aurora-B, the designed binders were essentially inactive, with the notable exception of D-B-S-one which retained some Aurora-B inhibition — but at least a hundred-fold less potent than against Aurora-A. Cellular assays in HeLa cells transfected with G-F-P-tagged binder constructs showed that both D-B-L-five and D-B-S-one blocked Aurora-A autophosphorylation at threonine two-eighty-eight at the spindle pole, while only D-B-S-one — the less selective of the two — had a measurable but weak effect on the Aurora-B substrate marker, histone-H-three serine ten. Immunoprecipitation followed by mass spectrometry, using G-F-P as a pulldown handle, showed that the three most selective binders enriched Aurora-A and its scaffold partner T-P-X-two as the only shared hits, whereas the promiscuous D-B-S-one pulled down over two hundred other proteins including the related kinases M-A-R-K-two and M-E-L-K.

For the audience of this show, three methodological pieces are worth taking away. First, the design-to-hit ratio here is very good — seventeen designs, thirteen with measurable inhibition, one with sub-nanomolar affinity — which is quite different from the more typical hundred-designs-to-one-hit ratios seen in some de novo binder papers, and it argues that motif scaffolding from a natural weak binder is a lower-variance design strategy than de novo backbone generation. Second, the pattern of convergent alpha-G-helix engagement plus activation-loop stabilisation is a specific hypothesis about what makes a substrate-recognition-surface binder into a potent inhibitor, and the group provides both crystal-structure and single-point-mutation evidence for it. Third — and this is the antibody-relevant point — the group argues explicitly that designed miniproteins can complement or replace antibodies as affinity-capture reagents in immunoprecipitation, because they can be raised against arbitrary target surfaces and conformational states rather than being limited to what's immunogenic and what a hybridoma will produce. That is a real functional overlap with the antibody engineering the audience of this show works on, and worth watching.

The third item is a bioRxiv preprint also posted July thirteenth, titled "Learned Immune Architectures of Durable Antibody Responses Across Vaccines." Corresponding authors Adriana Tomic and Pawel Przytycki at Boston University. This is not an antibody-engineering paper — it's a systems-vaccinology paper that predicts which people will mount high antibody titers to which vaccines from pre- and post-vaccination transcriptomic profiles. Two reasons it belongs on tonight's list. First, the machine-learning finding — biologically-informed feature aggregation beats raw gene-level input by a substantial margin — is worth understanding for anyone building predictors on transcriptomic or other high-dimensional biological features. Second, the paper identifies specific immune modules that predict high antibody titers, which is directly relevant for anyone thinking about vaccine antigen design or antibody discovery from human vaccine cohorts.

The setup. The group used the harmonised Immune Signatures Data Resource — a curated pan-vaccine transcriptomic archive assembled through the Human Immunology Project Consortium — and pulled the one thousand thirty-two participants who had matched pre-vaccination and roughly-day-twenty-eight post-vaccination transcriptomic profiles plus day-twenty-eight antibody-titer data. Three vaccine cohorts survived cohort-size filtering: influenza, seven hundred seventy-six participants; hepatitis-B, one hundred sixty; and yellow-fever, ninety-six. The three vaccines cover very different immune-durability regimes — annual-boost seasonal influenza, subunit hepatitis-B with long-lived humoral memory, and single-dose yellow-fever with essentially lifelong protection — which is useful because it lets the group look for both shared and vaccine-specific predictive signatures.

The methodological finding. Random-forest classifiers were trained independently within each vaccine cohort to predict high antibody responders. When features were the raw approximately ten-thousand-gene expression vectors, held-out area-under-the-curve was near-random, around zero-point-five-four to zero-point-five-six. Aggregating those genes into pre-defined Blood Transcriptional Modules — of which there are roughly three hundred, corresponding to known immune-cell types, pathways, and signalling programs — raised area-under-the-curve to zero-point-seven-eight for hepatitis-B, zero-point-seven-zero for influenza, and zero-point-eight-one for yellow fever. That's a fourteen-to-twenty-five point absolute improvement, and it comes purely from replacing gene-level features with pathway-level aggregates before training. This aligns with a well-known pattern in bio-M-L — when the number of features vastly exceeds the number of samples, biology-informed dimensionality reduction consistently outperforms end-to-end learning on the raw feature space. The practical implication for antibody researchers is straightforward: if you're building a predictor on a small-cohort transcriptomic dataset, don't feed the raw expression matrix to a black-box model.

The biological findings. Permutation-based feature-importance analysis identified the dominant predictive modules in each cohort. Hepatitis-B was driven by T-helper-two differentiation and integrin-mediated leukocyte migration, consistent with the known Th-two-biased humoral response to alum-adjuvanted recombinant-protein vaccines. Influenza was driven by C-D-four T-cell modules, M-two macrophage polarisation, and c-Myc-driven cell-cycle programs, consistent with a proliferative expansion of antigen-specific lymphocytes. Yellow-fever was driven by B-cell-receptor signalling and B-cell developmental modules, along with mucosal-associated-invariant T-cell and natural-killer-cell modules, consistent with the broad innate-plus-adaptive engagement induced by the live-attenuated Y-F-seventeen-D vaccine. Cross-pathogen concordance analysis then identified four transcriptional modules that were shared predictors across all three vaccines, providing a small candidate set for follow-up. Surrogate decision trees were used to extract interpretable if-then rules from each random-forest — a nice compromise between accuracy and interpretability that avoids the SHAP-alone trap of losing hierarchical structure.

Limitations. The models predict day-twenty-eight antibody titer, which is a peak-response endpoint, not a long-term-durability endpoint despite the paper's framing. The three cohorts are demographically heterogeneous and the group does age but not sex residualisation. The four "shared" modules are identified by pairwise concordance across independently-trained models, not by joint modelling, so they are shared in the sense of being consistently predictive rather than mechanistically joint. For antibody engineers, the operationally useful piece is the Blood Transcriptional Module list — the specific modules that ranked highest for antibody-titer prediction are candidate stratification biomarkers for pulling deep-sampled repertoires from expected high-responders, which is a plausible way to enrich for potent memory B-cells in downstream discovery pipelines.

That's the night edition. Three papers, three very different methodological messages. Nucleotide-level decoder-only D-N-A language models can outperform larger protein language models on antibody-repertoire tasks. Motif-scaffolded miniproteins seeded on a natural weak binder can hit single-digit-nanomolar affinity and single-paralog selectivity from about a dozen designs, and crystal structures pinpoint activation-loop plus alpha-G-helix engagement as the transferable design signature. And biologically-informed feature aggregation into transcriptional modules is what makes systems-vaccinology transcriptomic predictors actually work. Thanks for tuning in — back tomorrow.
