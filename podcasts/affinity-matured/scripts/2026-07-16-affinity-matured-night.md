## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your night briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is Thursday, July sixteenth, and this is the third and final edition of the day. Two smaller items, both peer-reviewed journal papers indexed on PubMed within the last day. Both are peptide language modeling — not antibody-specific, but squarely inside the protein language model bucket this show tracks, and both make claims about representation choices that are worth thinking about if you work with modified or macrocyclic modalities.

First, a new peptide language model from the Okuno lab at Kyoto called HELM-BERT, which pretrains directly on a chemistry notation that keeps monomer identity and covalent topology explicit. Second, a shorter item from a Shandong University group called PeptideSGCL, which pairs a protein language model with a graph transformer through a contrastive learning objective for peptide property prediction.

Let's take HELM-BERT first, because it is the more substantive of the two and it touches on a question that comes up whenever you work with antibody-drug conjugates, cyclic peptide payloads, or any modality with non-canonical chemistry: which representation should the language model see?

The paper is titled "HELM-BERT: Topology-Aware Representations for Chemically Modified Peptides." First author is Seungeon Lee, corresponding author is Yasushi Okuno, all at the Graduate School of Medicine at Kyoto University. The peer-reviewed article was published in the Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling and indexed on PubMed yesterday. A preprint went up on arXiv at the end of December two-thousand-twenty-five, so the underlying work has been visible for about seven months, but the published version is where you should look for the numbers.

The framing of the problem is worth restating in plain terms. If you want to build a language model over therapeutic peptides — cyclic peptides, stapled peptides, peptides with non-canonical amino acids, peptide-drug conjugates — you have two dominant representation choices, and both are compromises.

The first choice is atom-level notation like SMILES. SMILES encodes chemistry at the atom-and-bond level. It handles any modification, any noncanonical residue, any macrocycle, because it treats the molecule as a graph of atoms. The problem is that SMILES obscures monomer structure and cyclic topology. A ten-residue macrocyclic peptide is a very long SMILES string, and the ring closure is scattered across the string as a pair of matching digits. A language model trained on SMILES has to learn to reconstruct the concept of "residue" and "macrocycle" from atoms upward, which is expensive in data.

The second choice is amino-acid-level notation, which is what every protein language model uses — one letter per residue. This is compact and semantically clean for canonical proteins, but it cannot represent noncanonical residues, chemical modifications, cross-links, or arbitrary connectivity. ESM and its cousins are effectively out of scope for anything other than the twenty-standard-amino-acid alphabet with linear peptide bonds.

HELM-BERT takes a third road. HELM stands for Hierarchical Editing Language for Macromolecules, and it is the notation used inside a lot of pharma peptide registration workflows. HELM has a monomer alphabet — including all canonical residues plus a large library of non-canonical monomers — and an explicit connection language that records which monomer is bonded to which other monomer, including cross-links, macrocyclizations, and conjugation points. So a cyclic peptide with a stapled disulfide reads, in HELM, as a list of monomers plus an explicit set of connections. Monomer identity is a token; connectivity is a token. The atom-level detail lives in the monomer library, off to the side; the model does not have to reconstruct it.

The architecture is DeBERTa — the encoder-only transformer with disentangled attention, an enhanced mask decoder, and n-gram-induced encoding. The choice matters for two reasons. First, disentangled attention represents each token as two vectors — a content vector and a position vector — and computes attention over both separately. That is a good match for HELM sequences, where the position of a monomer inside the primary sequence is one signal and its identity in a very large monomer vocabulary is another. Second, the enhanced mask decoder and the n-gram-induced encoding help with masked-token modeling on short sequences with local structural patterns — which peptides are, compared to full-length proteins.

The pretraining corpus is thirty-nine thousand and seventy-nine unique chemically diverse peptides, spanning both linear and cyclic structures. That is small by protein-language-model standards — ESM2 was trained on the entirety of UniRef50, hundreds of millions of sequences — but the point of HELM-BERT is not to compete with foundation-scale protein LMs on canonical protein tasks. It is to work in the regime where SMILES-based models struggle: chemically modified therapeutic peptides, where public data volumes are small and the vocabulary of modifications is large.

Now the results. Two downstream tasks. The first is cyclic peptide membrane permeability prediction — a task everyone doing therapeutic-peptide medicinal chemistry cares about. Under the random-split evaluation reported in the published paper, HELM-BERT achieves a mean coefficient of determination of zero-point-six-six-eight. Under a Murcko scaffold split — which is a stronger test that forces the training and test sets to have disjoint core scaffolds — HELM-BERT retains the best mean performance across the models evaluated. The scaffold split matters here, because random splits systematically overestimate model performance on tasks with clustered chemical series. If the split is scaffold-aware and HELM-BERT still comes out on top, the representation is doing real work, not just memorizing training scaffolds.

For comparison, when they tested SMILES-based encoders like MoLFormer-XL and PeptideCLM on the same task, those models trailed HELM-BERT in the primary evaluation. When the authors ran an architecture-matched SMILES control — same encoder capacity, only the input notation swapped — the gap narrowed under full fine-tuning but stayed clear in frozen-representation settings, meaning linear probes on top of the frozen embeddings. That is a specific and interesting result. It says the pretraining representation quality — the actual embedding geometry — is more informative for HELM than for SMILES, and the SMILES side compensates only when you allow fine-tuning to reshape the representation. If you were going to use these embeddings off the shelf as features for a downstream task with limited labeled data, HELM-BERT would give you a better starting point.

The second downstream task is peptide-protein interaction prediction, evaluated on the Propedia and ChEMBL benchmarks. HELM-BERT is described as competitive with protein-language-model baselines on peptides composed of natural amino acids — the regime where protein LMs are supposed to shine — and it retains its advantage on modified peptides. So HELM-BERT does not sacrifice natural-peptide performance for modification-awareness; the notation is a strict generalization.

There is also an embedding analysis that shows HELM-BERT preserves macrocyclic topology in a linearly accessible form. That is the phrase the paper uses, and what it means concretely is that a simple linear probe on top of the frozen embeddings can recover topological features — is this peptide cyclic, how many cross-links does it have, what is the ring size — with high accuracy. SMILES-based encoders do not do this as cleanly, because the topology is scattered across the token sequence rather than encoded in an explicit connection statement.

Two things the audience should pull out from this paper.

The first is a design pattern. If you have a modality that falls between small-molecule chemistry and canonical protein sequence — antibody-drug conjugates, peptide-drug conjugates, cyclic peptide therapeutics, non-natural-amino-acid antibodies — the representation you feed the language model matters more than the model size. Pretraining a modestly sized encoder on a notation that makes the structural constraints explicit outperforms a much larger encoder trained on a notation that obscures those constraints. HELM-BERT is thirty-nine thousand peptides at what amounts to a base-BERT scale, and it beats atom-level encoders that were trained on orders of magnitude more data. Notation is not neutral.

The second is the practical availability. The authors have released code on GitHub under the clinfo organization at Kyoto and posted model weights on Hugging Face. That means you can pull the checkpoint and use HELM-BERT embeddings as features in your own peptide screening pipeline this week, if you have HELM-formatted data or you can convert your monomer inventory into HELM. That last point is a real friction if your registration system does not already use HELM — but there is a growing set of tooling for HELM conversion, some of which was covered in an earlier chemRxiv paper on HELM-ifying peptide libraries.

One caveat worth noting. The dataset scale — thirty-nine thousand peptides — is small compared to a serious protein LM pretraining run. There is no reason to believe HELM-BERT is a foundation model; it is a domain-specific encoder that works well on tasks in its distribution. When you take it out of the therapeutic-peptide regime — for example, applying it to native full-length proteins — you should expect the ESM family to still dominate. The right way to use HELM-BERT is as a peptide-specific feature extractor for modified-peptide problems, not as a general protein encoder.

Let's move to the second item.

The paper is called "PeptideSGCL: Structure-Enhanced Graph-Transformer Encoding and Dual-Level Contrastive Learning for Peptide Property Prediction." First author is Ge Cai, corresponding authors are at Shandong University in Jinan and Macao Polytechnic University. Published in A-C-S Synthetic Biology and indexed on PubMed yesterday. The specific tasks are hemolytic activity prediction and non-fouling prediction for peptides — two binary classifications that matter for peptide-drug safety and translational potential.

The architecture is a multimodal framework with three moving parts. The sequence encoder is ProtBERT — the two-hundred-fifty-million-parameter BERT-style protein language model trained on UniRef100, so an existing off-the-shelf protein LM. The structural encoder is a hierarchical stack that alternates graph neural network layers with transformer layers. The graph layers capture local topological patterns; the transformer layers capture long-range structural dependencies. There is also a parallel graph spatial channel attention module, which is a specialized attention block over the graph features intended to amplify task-relevant structural signal.

The contrastive learning setup is what distinguishes this paper from prior peptide-modality-fusion work. Most previous multimodal peptide models used cross-modal contrastive learning — pull the sequence representation and the structure representation of the same peptide together, push different peptides apart. That handles cross-modal alignment but does nothing for intramodal discriminability, which is the ability of the model to separate hemolytic from non-hemolytic peptides within either modality alone. This paper adds a second contrastive objective, an inter-intra hybrid supervised contrastive loss, which uses the task labels to pull same-class peptides together and push different-class peptides apart, in addition to the cross-modal alignment. So the joint embedding space is optimized both for sequence-structure agreement and for downstream class separability.

The results are reported as "overall performance superior to baseline models on both hemolysis and non-fouling prediction," which is a modest but honest claim. The evaluation matrix in a paper like this typically covers accuracy, F1, MCC, and AUC against a handful of published peptide-property predictors, and the win is usually a few percentage points on each. That is where I would expect this paper to land. There is no wet-lab validation in this work; it is a method paper.

The reason to notice PeptideSGCL is not any headline result but the recipe. If you are building a multimodal predictor over antibody CDR loops — sequence embeddings from an antibody language model like AbLang or IgLM paired with structure embeddings from a graph model over IgFold or ABodyBuilder predictions — the dual-contrastive setup translates directly. Cross-modal alignment plus intramodal class-supervised contrast is a reasonable default when your downstream task is a discrete developability call — soluble versus aggregation-prone, expressible versus not, cross-reactive versus not. The Shandong group did not do this on antibodies, but the framework is generic.

Two closing observations that tie today's three episodes together.

The first is the arc of representation choice. This morning we covered the Baker lab's transmembrane beta-barrel work, where the design result depended on building a class-specific dataset by distilling AlphaFold predictions and then conditioning the generator on class-specific structural features. This afternoon, the Nozach-lab cross-reactive antibody work used deep-mutational-scanning-derived labels for classifier training over a specific antibody, and a targeted classification objective — three-way affinity bins — rather than generic regression. This evening, HELM-BERT argues that peptide language modeling gains more from a notation that makes structural constraints explicit than from raw model scale. Different scales of the same lesson: the modeling problem is easier when the representation and the training signal are aligned with the structural regularities of the modality you are working in.

The second is that the peptide-language-model literature is starting to converge on transformer encoders pretrained on modality-specific notation, with structural encoders bolted on via contrastive learning. HELM-BERT is the notation-first move; PeptideSGCL is the structure-fusion move. Both are useful ingredients if you eventually want to build an antibody-conjugate representation that handles paylo-loaded antibodies, or a stapled-peptide-scaffold representation for constrained CDR loops.
