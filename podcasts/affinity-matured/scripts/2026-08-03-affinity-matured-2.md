Welcome back to Affinity Matured for a bonus midday edition. Three items today, all bioRxiv preprints posted in the last thirty-six hours. First, Nesso-one, a technical report from Valence Labs and Recursion that introduces an open-source coarse-grained cofolding model for binding-affinity prediction, running more than ten times faster than Boltz-two while matching or beating its accuracy on public and out-of-distribution assays. Second, a nanobody-guided epitope-mapping paper from the Tzarum lab at Hebrew University of Jerusalem in collaboration with the Mansun Law lab at Scripps and the Steyaert nanobody platform in Brussels — the study builds a hundred-plus-member camelid nanobody library against the hepatitis C virus envelope glycoprotein E-two, and uses hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry to split those nanobodies into three structurally distinct classes, one of which validates antigenic region one as a functional neutralization site that had long been considered non-neutralizing. Third, P-G-L-L-M, a preprint from the Debora Marks and George Church labs that benchmarks thirteen general-purpose large language models against ninety-five published protein predictors on two hundred seventeen variant-ranking tasks from ProteinGym, and finds that the current frontier language models — Claude Opus five and G-P-T-five-point-six — rank variants at a level competitive with many specialized sequence-based predictors, without ever being trained on protein data specifically.

Because all three preprints were posted in the last day, the bioRxiv full-text HTML is not yet fully rendered — I read the Nesso-one and hepatitis-C papers from the PDFs and P-G-L-L-M from its abstract, leaderboard site, and code release. I will flag the depth where it matters.

First item. Nesso-one, from Valence Labs and Recursion, released on bioRxiv August third. The paper is a technical report rather than a full research paper — the format signals that this is a product release accompanied by open-source code and weights, and the emphasis is on inference speed and benchmark placement rather than a new methodological innovation. The central claim is that Nesso-one takes roughly one second per prediction on a single H-one-hundred G-P-U, which the authors report is more than ten times faster than Boltz-two, the leading open-source cofolding-based affinity model. That speedup is what unlocks the paper's downstream framing — Nesso-one lets you screen up to twenty times more compounds under the same compute budget, which meaningfully expands the chemical space accessible to high-throughput virtual screening.

The architectural story is where this paper matters for the broader protein-modeling community. Nesso-one adopts Boltz-two's training data and optimization framework — the same BindingDB and ChEMBL affinity data curation, the same decoy generation, the same data-leakage constraints removing sequences with ninety-percent or greater similarity to the standard F-E-P-plus benchmark — but strips out the heavy-atom diffusion module. The trunk in Nesso-one only updates pairwise token representations, where each token is a residue center — C-beta for most amino acids, C-alpha for glycine — plus ligand heavy atoms. Triangle attention and triangle multiplication operations run at the token level, giving the model geometric reasoning about distances between residue centers and ligand atoms without ever generating an atomistic pose. There is no diffusion module iteratively refining side-chain placements. The pairwise representation is optimized directly with a categorical cross-entropy loss over distance bins, and the affinity module is a separate, smaller Pairformer that consumes the trunk's distance predictions plus frozen protein language model embeddings from E-S-M-two.

That's the key architectural move — Nesso-one drops the atomistic diffusion generative component entirely, on the argument that atomistic resolution is unnecessary or even harmful for binding affinity prediction. The authors point to prior evaluations showing that Boltz-two's affinity predictions can be uncorrelated with the quality of its generated poses — meaning the atomistic detail Boltz-two spends compute on isn't what's driving affinity accuracy. That is a striking empirical observation, and it justifies the coarse-graining. A recent closed-source model called TerraBind made a similar argument, but TerraBind uses proprietary ligand embeddings and is closed-source. Nesso-one's contribution is running the same paradigm openly, with weights trained on public data, and demonstrating that the coarse-grained approach transfers.

The removal of multiple sequence alignments is another architectural decision worth calling out. Boltz-two, like AlphaFold-two before it, uses M-S-A features to give the trunk evolutionary context. Nesso-one eliminates that entirely and substitutes E-S-M-two embeddings, following the growing family of M-S-A-free models. The reasoning is straightforward — M-S-A search is a fixed per-target cost that dominates inference for high-throughput screening, and pre-trained protein language model embeddings capture much of what the M-S-A was providing. The report notes that when the timing comparisons are done, they exclude fixed costs — E-S-M-two loading for Nesso-one and M-S-A search for Boltz-two — but even at inference-only, Nesso-one runs about ten times faster, with the gap growing to over twenty-fold on larger proteins.

On structural benchmarks — FoldBench, PoseBusters, Runs-and-Poses — Nesso-one matches TerraBind's numbers on ligand root-mean-square-deviation success rate at two angstroms, which is the standard cofolding pose-quality metric. That's important because it shows the coarse-grained trunk still learns high-quality geometric representations even without atomistic refinement. On affinity benchmarks, the paper draws a distinction between in-distribution and out-of-distribution evaluation. Boltz-two's original benchmark suite — F-E-P-plus, C-A-S-P-sixteen, OpenFE — is shown to share high chemical similarity with the BindingDB and ChEMBL training data. Consequently, both Boltz-two and Nesso-one look strong on those benchmarks in a way that reflects in-distribution generalization rather than true zero-shot performance. This is an honest methodological framing that most cofolding papers don't foreground.

The out-of-distribution evaluation is on the recent OpenBind affinity benchmark, plus twenty-five internal Recursion biochemical assays. On OpenBind, Nesso-one outperforms Boltz-two by a notable margin, but the authors caveat that on the same OpenBind, even Nesso-one only modestly improves over ranking compounds by molecular weight. That's not a critique of Nesso-one specifically — it's a critique of the current state of the field on real-world medicinal chemistry generalization. Both Boltz-two and Nesso-one are pushing on a hard problem where every current model, including the strongest ones, still falls short of the F-E-P-plus-style physics-based methods on true out-of-distribution chemistry. Nesso-one is the fastest option, matches or beats the leading open-source alternative, and remains competitive on the hard cases — but zero-shot generalization to novel chemotypes is not solved.

The paper also shows selectivity examples, where Nesso-one correctly separates the binding affinities of identical compounds between on-targets and closely related off-targets. That's a specific capability worth calling out because off-target selectivity is one of the most useful things an affinity model can do for downstream medicinal chemistry. The examples in the report are anecdotal rather than systematic, but they demonstrate that the model isn't just memorizing target-agnostic drug-likeness features.

For antibody-engineering audiences, Nesso-one is a small-molecule cofolding model — the immediate applicability is drug discovery on small-molecule ligands, not on antibody-antigen complexes. But the architectural precedent matters. Coarse-grained cofolding without atomistic diffusion, using E-S-M-two embeddings and a lightweight Pairformer trunk, is a paradigm that transfers naturally to protein-protein and protein-peptide affinity prediction — which is what we actually need for antibody-antigen affinity work. Boltz-two already supports protein-protein complexes, and the same acceleration argument applies for antibody design campaigns where you want to screen thousands of designed variants against a target. Watch this space — the open-source coarse-grained cofolding paradigm now has a public reference implementation, and the community will extend it to biologics. Code and weights are at github dot com slash recursion-pharma slash nesso.

Second item. bioRxiv, posted August third, "Distinct mechanisms of antibody-mediated hepatitis C virus neutralization revealed by nanobody-guided epitope mapping," from the Tzarum lab at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem, with Mansun Law and Erick Giang at Scripps Research contributing the E-two antigens and reference antibodies, the Steyaert group at V-I-B-V-U-B in Brussels running the nanobody library construction, and Petr Pompach at the Czech Academy of Sciences running the hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry. This is a substantial antibody-discovery-and-structure paper — sixty-four purified nanobodies, biolayer interferometry affinity measurements, neutralization assays across fourteen genotype-diverse hepatitis C virus reference isolates, and structural epitope mapping by hydrogen-deuterium exchange.

The biological problem. Hepatitis C virus remains a major global-health challenge — hundreds of thousands of deaths annually from cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver failure, with new infections continuing especially in people who inject drugs. Direct-acting antivirals are highly effective for treatment, but re-infection and access issues mean a preventive vaccine is still needed. The dominant target of neutralizing antibodies is the envelope glycoprotein E-two, and about thirty percent of acutely infected patients spontaneously clear the virus in association with an early, broadly neutralizing antibody response. So the target is validated — the problem is engineering an immunogen that consistently elicits that broadly neutralizing response.

The specific engineering challenge is antigenic diversity. E-two has eight genetically distinct genotypes and multiple antigenic sites, and the human broadly neutralizing antibody response is dominated by the V-H-one-sixty-nine germline gene family. That family converges on a specific antigenic region called A-R-three, which sits on the E-two neutralization face and overlaps with the C-D-eighty-one receptor-binding site. V-H-one-sixty-nine dominance is a double-edged sword — it validates A-R-three as a broadly neutralizing site, but it also means the human immune repertoire has narrow structural sampling of E-two's full antigenic landscape, and a vaccine that only elicits V-H-one-sixty-nine-encoded antibodies will miss other functional epitopes.

This paper attacks that narrow-sampling problem by turning to camelid heavy-chain-only antibodies — nanobodies. The argument is that llama and alpaca V-H-H germline genes have a completely different structural repertoire than human V-H-one-sixty-nine, and their smaller paratope size — about fifteen kilodaltons versus one hundred fifty for a conventional I-g-G — lets them access recessed, conformationally flexible, and cryptic surfaces that a conventional antibody can't reach. That makes nanobodies well-suited as epitope-discovery probes.

The workflow. Two llamas were immunized: one with the soluble E-two ectodomain from the H-seventy-seven prototypic strain, which includes hypervariable region one and the stalk region; and one with a truncated E-two core called E-two-c-three, which lacks the flexible N-terminal and C-terminal regions and two glycosylation sites. Six consecutive injections per animal, and phage-display libraries built from the peripheral blood lymphocytes. Sequencing yielded one hundred six distinct E-two-specific clones, grouped by C-D-R heavy-chain-three length and eighty-percent sequence identity into forty-eight nanobody families. Sixty-four representative nanobodies were expressed in E. coli as His-tagged fusions from the p-M-E-S-y-four vector, purified by affinity and size-exclusion chromatography. Twenty-four of those cross-bound the native E-one-E-two complex across multiple genotypes on E-L-I-S-A, and a further-selected panel of twelve was taken forward for detailed characterization. Binding affinities measured by biolayer interferometry ranged from two nanomolar to five hundred thirty nanomolar dissociation constant — solid nanomolar affinities for a first-generation library, comparable to human broadly neutralizing antibodies against E-two.

Neutralization was tested against a fourteen-isolate reference panel spanning multiple hepatitis C virus genotypes and tiers of neutralization sensitivity. Family thirteen nanobodies emerged as the strongest cross-neutralizers, achieving greater than fifty percent neutralization across most of the panel at both twenty micrograms per milliliter and two micrograms per milliliter — moderately potent, broadly cross-reactive. Families one and eight were middling, and a couple of clones showed minimal activity.

Where the paper delivers its main contribution is on epitope mapping. The authors ran competition E-L-I-S-A assays against a panel of well-characterized human broadly neutralizing antibodies — A-R-three-A, H-C-one-A-M, and H-E-P-C-three targeting antigenic region three; A-P-thirty-three targeting antigenic site four-twelve; and a non-neutralizing antibody called E-one targeting antigenic region one — plus soluble C-D-eighty-one large extracellular loop as a receptor mimic. Then they backed those competition profiles up with hydrogen-deuterium exchange mass spectrometry, which measures the rate at which backbone amide hydrogens exchange with deuterium — a rate that is highly sensitive to solvent accessibility and therefore to whether an epitope is occluded by a bound antibody. The E-two-c-three protein alone was compared to E-two-c-three complexed with each nanobody at fixed time points; regions with reduced deuterium uptake in the complex are the footprint of the bound nanobody. Peptic digestion covered ninety-eight percent of the E-two-c-three sequence, so the mapping is comprehensive at the peptide level.

The hydrogen-deuterium exchange data split the twelve nanobodies into three structurally distinct classes. Class one binds the beta-sandwich region of E-two, primarily engaging beta-strand five and the loop connecting beta-strands six and seven — a surface immediately adjacent to the C-D-eighty-one binding loop. The mechanistic story here is subtle. When C-D-eighty-one engages E-two, the beta-five strand undergoes a conformational rearrangement. So a nanobody that binds beta-five doesn't sit on top of the C-D-eighty-one binding site directly — but it interferes with the conformational change required for productive C-D-eighty-one engagement. That's a distinct neutralization mechanism from direct receptor blockade, and it maps to antigenic region one, which had long been considered non-neutralizing. Recent work by the Ogega group identified a low-neutralizing antibody called h-cab-seventeen that also targets this region. This paper independently confirms A-R-one as a functional neutralization site and expands the number of characterized nanobodies binding there.

Class two nanobodies target the canonical neutralization face — front layer, beta-strand five, and the C-D-eighty-one-binding loop — which is exactly where the classic broadly neutralizing antibodies A-R-three-A, H-C-one-A-M, and H-E-P-C-three bind. Class two nanobodies compete strongly with those human broadly neutralizing antibodies and with the C-D-eighty-one receptor mimic. This is where the paper's most potent and broadly cross-neutralizing nanobodies sit — family thirteen falls in class two. Interestingly, hydrogen-deuterium exchange shows these class-two nanobodies engage the antigen in a way similar to a specific subset of human broadly neutralizing antibodies — the m-Ab-one-one-nine-eight and m-Ab-one-three-eight-two family, and the rhesus macaque bnAb R-M-eleven-forty-three — rather than to the V-H-one-sixty-nine consensus. The paper reads this as evidence that convergent binding modes on the neutralization face can arise from very different germline sources.

Class three nanobodies bind a short linear epitope on the back layer, on beta-strand eleven, overlapping with antigenic region two — a region known to be low-neutralizing. These serve as a negative-control class within the study, and they weakly or don't neutralize.

The implication for A-I-driven antibody design and vaccine immunogen design is substantive. First, the paper validates the general strategy of using camelid nanobody libraries as epitope-discovery probes to overcome germline-encoded immunodominance biases. This is not new as a concept, but it is now demonstrated concretely for a virus where the V-H-one-sixty-nine dominance had left half the antigen underexplored. Second, the paper provides structural anchor points — three classes of epitope footprints on E-two — that a computational immunogen designer can now target directly. The pipeline for structure-guided immunogen design has been established for respiratory syncytial virus pre-fusion F and for human immunodeficiency virus envelope; hepatitis C virus E-two has lagged, and one reason is that the antigenic landscape was defined mostly by V-H-one-sixty-nine-encoded antibodies at A-R-three. Now that A-R-one is validated as functional and has a defined structural footprint with a specific neutralization mechanism, immunogen designers have a concrete second target for stabilization and epitope presentation on E-two. Third, family thirteen's cross-neutralization plus its novel A-R-three-adjacent binding mode gives a template scaffold for an antibody-guided immunogen — the kind of germline-targeting or lineage-designed approach that has been productive for H-I-V envelope. And fourth, the paper is a proof of concept that nanobody sequences engineered against a viral glycoprotein can uncover epitopes that human antibody-discovery pipelines simply missed — the same lesson generalizes to other antigenically diverse pathogens where human immune sampling has convergent germline biases.

Third item. bioRxiv, version two posted August third, "P-G-L-L-M: Benchmarking General-Purpose Language Models for Protein Variant Ranking," from Rohit Arora, L-T Chen, Michael Du, Debora Marks, and George Church. Marks and Church are corresponding — Marks runs a protein variant-effect-prediction lab at Harvard Medical School that has produced most of the ProteinGym benchmark suite, and Church needs no introduction. Because this is a v-two update posted today, the full text isn't rendered on bioRxiv yet — I read the abstract, the leaderboard site at proteingymllm dot com, and the GitHub for the benchmark harness.

The setup is elegant. ProteinGym is the community benchmark for protein variant-effect prediction — several hundred deep-mutational-scanning assays covering diverse protein families, standardized so that method developers can compare their models on equal footing. The current specialist models — E-S-M-two, VenusREM, ProtSSN, TranceptEVE, and dozens of others — score highly on ProteinGym, and the field has long assumed that variant-effect prediction is a specialized task that requires either evolutionary alignments, structural priors, or protein-specific pretraining. P-G-L-L-M asks a different question: what if you just prompt a general-purpose large language model? Give it the wild-type protein sequence, tell it what assay is being measured, and ask it to rank fifty candidate mutant sequences by predicted fitness — with no multiple sequence alignment, no structure, no protein-specific fine-tuning.

The benchmark builds on two hundred seventeen ProteinGym substitution assays. Each task is identical in shape — one wild-type sequence, one assay description, fifty mutants, rank them. Thirteen general-purpose language models were evaluated. Ninety-five published protein predictors were rescored on the same candidate sets with the same Spearman-rank-correlation metric — a critical detail, because when specialist models report their numbers on the full ProteinGym they are averaging over the full mutant space of each assay, and comparing a fifty-mutant subsample against that average is not apples-to-apples. P-G-L-L-M forces the specialists to score the same fifty mutants the language models saw, and reports Spearman correlation between predicted and measured fitness.

Headline results. Claude Opus five leads the primary leaderboard at Spearman rho of zero-point-four-oh-six, narrowly ahead of G-P-T-five-point-six at zero-point-four-oh-two, though when the two are compared only on assays where both models submitted scores, G-P-T-five-point-six is actually a bit higher. Claude Opus four-point-eight lands at zero-point-three-five-six. Gemini three-point-five Flash at zero-point-three-four-three. G-P-T-five-point-five at zero-point-three-three-six. Kimi K-three at zero-point-three-one-four. Claude Opus five outperforms forty-nine of ninety-five biomolecular comparators — including forty-one of forty-six sequence-only methods — and approaches E-S-M-two at six hundred fifty million parameters, which scores zero-point-four-one-one on this benchmark. But the leading specialist predictor, VenusREM, still sits noticeably ahead at zero-point-five-two-three.

Two interpretive results are worth calling out. First, test-time compute scaling helps the language models — you can improve variant-ranking accuracy by giving the model more inference budget through longer reasoning chains — but the gains taper before the language models catch up to VenusREM or the strongest specialist predictors. There's headroom, but not unlimited headroom. Second, and this is the more surprising finding, sequence-only specialist predictors depend heavily on the depth of the multiple sequence alignment they can build for a given target — proteins with deeper M-S-A get better predictions. Language models don't show that dependence. Their variant-ranking accuracy is roughly the same whether the target protein has a large evolutionary family or a small one. That has a direct practical implication for antibody engineering — antibody variable regions are famously bad at M-S-A construction because their diversity is driven by somatic hypermutation and V-D-J recombination rather than by species-level evolutionary conservation. Sequence-only specialist predictors that need deep alignments underperform on antibody sequences. Language models, at least in principle, do not carry that liability.

The authors also address the contamination-risk question, which is the standard first objection to any language model benchmark. Fifty-nine deep-mutational-scanning assays from nineteen studies whose scores first became public after January of 2026 — after the training cutoff of the tested language models — were assembled as an independent contamination-controlled set. On that set, the same performance trends hold: the language models perform at roughly the same rank against the specialist baselines, indicating that the primary benchmark isn't being carried by memorization of leaked training data.

What does this mean for antibody-engineering workflows? Three things. First, general-purpose language models are now competent enough to serve as tool-free variant-effect scorers in cases where the target has poor evolutionary coverage — antibody variable regions specifically. They will not beat a purpose-built specialist model like VenusREM, but they will outperform many sequence-only methods on the exact class of proteins where those methods struggle. Second, the compute cost of a language-model call to rank a batch of mutants is still substantially higher than an E-S-M-two forward pass — inference-time compute scaling helps but doesn't come free. Deploying language models as production variant-effect scorers requires understanding that trade-off. Third, and this is the more speculative reading, the P-G-L-L-M result suggests that a lot of the residual gap between general-purpose language models and specialist protein predictors sits in the specialist models' M-S-A processing and structure priors. Language models are already competitive on the ranking task itself — the gap is in what the specialists know that the language models don't. The obvious research direction is language-model plus M-S-A retrieval — give the language model the alignment as tool output — and see what the combined performance looks like. The Marks and Church labs are exactly the group to do that follow-up work, and it will not surprise me if a v-three of this paper eventually reports that number.

That's the three for the bonus edition. Nesso-one is the most immediately actionable — if you are running Boltz-two for binding-affinity prediction anywhere in your pipeline, whether for small-molecule or protein-protein work, the open-source coarse-grained cofolding paradigm now has a public reference implementation, and it is over ten times faster. The hepatitis C virus nanobody paper is the strongest antibody-engineering pick — a substantive expansion of the E-two antigenic landscape, structural validation of antigenic region one as a functional neutralization site, and a nanobody library that could seed structure-based immunogen design for hepatitis C the way similar libraries did for respiratory syncytial virus and human immunodeficiency virus. And P-G-L-L-M is the take that will provoke the most conversation — general-purpose language models are now competitive with many specialist protein predictors on protein variant ranking, they don't need multiple sequence alignments, and the headroom to catch the specialist state of the art is meaningful but not enormous.
