Welcome back to Affinity Matured for a second bonus edition today. Three items, and they line up around an unusually clean theme — how you elicit and engineer broadly protective antibody responses, viewed from three angles. First, a fresh paper posted today in Emerging Microbes and Infections from the Wuhan Institute of Virology, describing a bivalent H-nine-N-two influenza vaccine whose hemagglutinin and neuraminidase antigens were computationally optimized with the Epigraph algorithm to cover the epitope diversity of the global H-nine-N-two viral population. Second, a Briefings in Bioinformatics paper indexed to PubMed today from the Rajapakse lab at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, introducing a mixture diffusion model for multimodal antibody design — the argument being that the standard unimodal diffusion approach to complementarity-determining region generation collapses too aggressively to a single conformation, and that swapping in a mixture density parameterization on both position and orientation updates lets the model explore alternative high-affinity binding conformations. Third, a bioRxiv preprint from the Barton Haynes and Anthony Moody groups at Duke with Stephen Harrison at Harvard, Aaron Schmidt at the Ragon Institute, and Thomas Kepler at Boston University — cross-institutional Duke Human Vaccine Institute work — showing that rhesus macaques immunized with a plain, unadjuvanted, unengineered H-one hemagglutinin trimer readily develop hemagglutinin stem-directed neutralizing antibodies, and that one of those antibodies protects mice from lethal H-one-N-one and H-five-N-one challenge. Two of these are engineering-first — a computationally optimized immunogen and a next-generation diffusion model. The third is a foil, and a useful one — a reminder that a plain trimer, without any of the more elaborate stem-focusing tricks the field has spent a decade developing, can already elicit protective stem antibodies in a nonhuman primate.

First item. Emerging Microbes and Infections, published online August third. "A bivalent inactivated influenza vaccine incorporating epitope-optimized surface proteins confers cross-protective immunity against H-nine-N-two influenza virus," from the State Key Laboratory of Virology and Biosafety at the Wuhan Institute of Virology, Chinese Academy of Sciences, with Shanxi Medical University as a partner. The paper is aimed at a specific unmet need in flu-vaccine engineering. H-nine-N-two is an avian influenza subtype that circulates widely in poultry across Asia and the Middle East, causes major economic damage to the poultry industry, and — because it can spillover into humans through occupational or live-market exposure — represents a low-probability but nontrivial pandemic risk. Existing candidate vaccine viruses like the World Health Organization-recommended AL-thirty-nine cover part of the H-nine-N-two antigenic space, but the virus has continued to evolve, and any single strain-based vaccine has a persistent breadth problem — it protects against strains close to it in sequence space and loses potency as circulating viruses drift.

The approach the authors take is to use Epigraph, a graph-based computational vaccine-design algorithm originally developed by Bette Korber and colleagues at Los Alamos for H-I-V and pan-filovirus vaccines. Epigraph works by treating a database of viral protein sequences as a directed graph of overlapping k-mers — typically nine-residue windows, chosen because that is the canonical length of a peptide loaded by class-one major histocompatibility complex for T-cell recognition — and then constructing a small number of synthetic protein sequences that jointly maximize the fraction of natural nine-mers in the population that are covered at least once. The output is a set of artificial antigens that look like real proteins at the fold level but statistically over-represent the epitope diversity of the input population. Two epigraph sequences typically cover the population much better than any single natural strain does — this is the same design principle as mosaic antigens, but Epigraph is faster and, under certain constraints, produces mathematically optimal coverage. Epigraph has been in use for a decade in H-I-V vaccine work; its application here to a chimeric H-nine-N-two live influenza-derived immunogen is what makes this paper interesting.

The authors constructed two Epigraph-designed antigen sets covering H-nine-N-two hemagglutinin and neuraminidase — one set generating a first pair of hemagglutinin and neuraminidase sequences, and a second set generating a complementary pair, together maximizing joint epitope coverage across the global H-nine-N-two dataset. They then used reverse genetics to rescue two chimeric influenza viruses — c-H-A-N-A-one and c-H-A-N-A-two — each carrying one Epigraph-designed hemagglutinin and one Epigraph-designed neuraminidase, and combined the two rescued viruses into a single bivalent inactivated vaccine called c-H-A-N-A. The comparator was AL-thirty-nine, the current World Health Organization candidate vaccine virus for H-nine-N-two. In mouse immunization studies, the bivalent c-H-A-N-A vaccine elicited more potent cross-reactive antibody responses and stronger T-cell immunity than the AL-thirty-nine comparator, and in a lethal heterologous H-nine-N-two challenge model, mice given c-H-A-N-A were protected — with significantly reduced pulmonary viral loads — while comparator groups fared worse. The paper positions c-H-A-N-A as a candidate universal H-nine-N-two vaccine platform.

Where the paper is thin from what is publicly disclosed in the abstract is on structural characterization of the Epigraph-designed antigens themselves — does the algorithm's coverage-optimization goal produce hemagglutinin trimers that fold correctly and mimic the neutralizing-epitope conformations of natural strains, or does it introduce compensating substitutions that shift the antigenic hierarchy in unpredictable ways? The mosaic-antigen literature suggests that carefully designed multi-epitope antigens can fold well and retain both T-cell and B-cell reactivity — but this requires validation on a per-antigen basis, and the current abstract doesn't specify what fraction of the H-nine-N-two population is covered at what epitope level, or how the coverage compares to the diversity actually seen in animal challenge experiments. I will read the full paper when Taylor and Francis makes it accessible, but for today the take-home is a clean one — computationally designed multi-epitope influenza antigens, delivered as a chimeric inactivated bivalent, protect mice against heterologous H-nine-N-two challenge better than the current W-H-O candidate. For a listener working on the antibody-design side of the vaccine problem, the interesting thread is that the Epigraph-designed antigen serves as an immunogen — it is the substrate that shapes what B-cell repertoire the animal generates. Any downstream engineering — humanization, affinity maturation, developability triage — is only as good as the polyclonal response you get from the animal, and immunogen design is the front end of that pipeline. Watch this space — Epigraph-style computationally optimized antigens have a long H-I-V track record, and their adoption into influenza vaccine engineering is a plausible next step for the field.

Second item. Briefings in Bioinformatics, volume twenty-seven, issue four, published July third and indexed to PubMed August third. "Mixture diffusion model for multimodal antibody design," from Vasanth Durvasula, Tiara Natasha Binte Sayuti, and Jagath Rajapakse at the College of Computing and Data Science at Nanyang Technological University in Singapore. This is a pure methods paper, and it sits squarely in the current frontier of diffusion-based antibody design.

The setup. Complementarity-determining region loops on antibodies are structurally flexible and adopt diverse conformations to achieve high-affinity antigen binding. The last few years have seen a wave of diffusion-based generative models for antibody design — DiffAb from the Song group, dyMEAN, AbMEGD, DIFFFORCE, and their close relatives — that co-model sequence and structure. All of them, the authors argue, share a common limitation: they parameterize the sequence-to-structure denoising transitions using unimodal distributions. In practice that means at every diffusion timestep, when the model updates a residue's position or orientation, it draws that update from a single-mode Gaussian. Over many timesteps and many residues, that produces smooth trajectories but tends to collapse the output distribution onto one conformational mode. For canonical C-D-R conformations that is fine — the loop has one dominant fold and the model can find it. For non-canonical modes, particularly on C-D-R heavy-three where the conformational space is much broader, a unimodal denoiser will systematically underexplore alternative binding-competent conformations. And the antibody design literature already tells us that exceptional binders often live in low-probability regions of the loop conformational space — the mutants that get isolated after directed evolution are frequently the ones that adopt a non-obvious loop geometry.

The mixture-diffusion approach the authors introduce, called M-D-M-A-D, replaces the unimodal Gaussian on both positional and rotational updates with a K-component mixture. For positions, they parameterize a K-component Gaussian mixture in the residue-local frame, with per-component means and a shared isotropic variance. For orientations, they use a K-component mixture on quaternion updates, with a cosine-similarity proxy for the geodesic distance on the rotation group. The training loss is the negative log-likelihood of a mixture density network on both branches. On the sequence side they keep multinomial diffusion — a categorical denoising process — but with an analytic Bayes posterior update that they show independently improves on DiffAb's heuristic sequence update, and that they treat as a controlled variant in their ablations.

The empirical setup is on the Structural Antibody Database — the standard antibody-antigen complex resource in the field. The authors take the DiffAb-released S-Ab-Dab snapshot, cluster by fifty-percent C-D-R heavy-three sequence identity, and hold out five entire clusters — nineteen total antibody-antigen complexes — as a strict test set. All methods share the same OpenMM plus Rosetta relaxation protocol on generated designs. On amino-acid recovery, M-D-M-A-D outperforms the baselines across most C-D-R loops. For C-D-R heavy-one recovery goes from seventy-nine-point-seven percent for the best baseline — DiffAb with the analytic posterior — to eighty-three-point-seven percent for M-D-M-A-D. For heavy-two the jump is larger — sixty-eight-point-seven to seventy-eight-point-seven. Heavy-three, the hard loop, goes from forty-point-eight to forty-two-point-nine percent — a real gain but a small one, consistent with the intrinsic difficulty of that loop. Light-one recovery jumps from seventy-eight-point-four to eighty-three-point-six. Light-three is essentially tied. On binding-improvement metric — the fraction of generated designs with a Rosetta interface-energy change more favorable than the wild-type — M-D-M-A-D on C-D-R heavy-three is thirty-two-point-four percent versus twenty-four-point-nine for the best baseline, which is the largest binding-related gain in the table and is on the loop where you would most want it. On heavy-one and light-three the baselines edge out or match M-D-M-A-D on binding improvement, which the authors call out as a real limitation of the mixture parameterization when applied uniformly across loops.

The mixture-capacity sweep is the most interesting part of the ablations. The authors show that going from K equals one — which is the standard unimodal DiffAb regime — up to K equals four monotonically improves amino-acid recovery for the heavy-chain C-D-Rs. Cluster count and cluster entropy of generated designs also grow with K, meaning the model is exploring more distinct conformational modes rather than collapsing back onto the canonical one. Best-of-N root-mean-square deviation stays comparable across K, so the mixture doesn't sacrifice the closeness-to-native metric — it broadens the ensemble around it. At K equals eight, light-three canonical hit rate peaks at sixty-point-three percent, but for most C-D-Rs K equals eight is over-capacity and starts hurting recovery. The paper's practical recommendation is K equals four as a moderate-capacity default.

There are two things that come out of this that are worth naming. First, the mixture-density parameterization is technically simple — it does not require a new architectural component, it swaps out the head of the denoising network for a mixture density network, and it interoperates with every existing invariant-point-attention encoder in the field. Any lab already running DiffAb, AbMEGD, or a close relative could in principle adopt the mixture head without retraining the encoder. Second, there is no wet-lab validation in this paper. The authors are explicit — their entire evaluation is on amino-acid recovery, root-mean-square deviation, best-of-N metrics, and Rosetta-based interface energies, none of which are experimental. That matters. The gap between "generates diverse plausible C-D-R conformations" and "generates diverse C-D-R conformations that translate to real binding affinity in a wet lab" is exactly the gap that TriFlow — the paper we covered this morning — is currently working through, at small experimental scale. M-D-M-A-D sits one step earlier in the maturity curve — a promising methodological improvement, waiting for wet-lab confirmation. Watch for follow-ups from Singapore or from any of the many groups running diffusion-based C-D-R design pipelines.

Third item. bioRxiv, posted July twenty-first, indexed to PubMed on August first. "Elicitation of stem-directed antibodies in rhesus macaques by a conventional hemagglutinin immunogen," lead author Songjuan Gu, with senior authors including Barton Haynes and Anthony Moody at the Duke Human Vaccine Institute, Stephen Harrison at Harvard, Aaron Schmidt at the Ragon Institute of M-I-T and Harvard, Thomas Kepler at Boston University, and Barney Graham formerly of the Vaccine Research Center. This is a Duke-anchored, cross-institutional study of a genuinely surprising immunological result — one that partly contradicts the received wisdom that has driven a decade of stem-focused immunogen engineering.

The biological context. Broadly protective influenza vaccines have been a grail-level goal in the field since the discovery of hemagglutinin stem-directed monoclonal antibodies — antibodies like C-R-six-two-six-one, F-I-six, and their close relatives — that bind the conserved hemagglutinin stem region and neutralize across influenza A group one and in some cases across groups. Because the stem is more conserved than the head, stem-directed antibodies are the leading candidate mechanism for a universal flu vaccine. But the human immune response to a natural infection or a standard trivalent or quadrivalent vaccine is dominated by head-directed antibodies, and the stem response is a small minority of the total. So the field has spent the last decade engineering headless hemagglutinin constructs, hyperglycosylated head decoys, chimeric hemagglutinins with irrelevant heads, ferritin-nanoparticle stem-only displays, and structure-guided stabilizing mutations — all aimed at redirecting the antibody response toward the stem.

What this paper reports is that the redirection may be less necessary than the field assumed. The authors immunized rhesus macaques with an ordinary, unengineered, monovalent H-one hemagglutinin trimer. Every immunized macaque developed high serum titers with broad reactivity against both H-one-N-one and H-five-N-one viruses. Seven monoclonal antibodies isolated from these animals strongly blocked binding of the canonical stem antibody C-R-six-two-six-one to H-one, confirming that the isolated monoclonals target the same stem site that has been the field's engineering target. Cryo-electron microscopy of one of the strongest isolates — antibody D-H-seven-two-six-point-one — bound to H-one confirmed the stem-directed footprint at atomic resolution, and showed the epitope overlaps with the footprint of known human stem antibodies. And in mouse challenge experiments, D-H-seven-two-six-point-one robustly protected animals from lethal H-one-N-one and H-five-N-one virus infection — cross-subtype protection from a monoclonal derived from an animal that received a plain, uncomplicated trimeric hemagglutinin.

The implication the authors draw is measured but pointed. Routine influenza vaccination — including today's standard trivalent inactivated vaccines — may already elicit stem antibodies at titers sufficient for meaningful protection against zoonotic H-five-N-one influenza, particularly through Fc-mediated effector functions in addition to direct neutralization. The paper doesn't argue that engineered stem-focused immunogens are useless — the authors' own institutions have contributed heavily to that engineering literature — but it does argue that the baseline immunogenicity of the conserved stem, in a permissive host like a rhesus macaque, is higher than the human literature had suggested. Whether the same is true in humans, and how much of the difference is species-level differences in B-cell repertoire versus differences in dosing and adjuvant regimens, is left for future work.

For the antibody-engineering audience, there are two useful takeaways. First, the D-H-seven-two-six-point-one antibody is a new addition to the small but growing library of experimentally cross-protective stem-directed antibodies from a non-human source, and the cryo-electron microscopy footprint gives structural coordinates that can seed structure-based immunogen design work — the Ragon Institute and Duke are natural downstream users of exactly that data. Second, the paper is a useful check on the assumption that broad protection requires elaborate immunogen engineering. It suggests that the informative-scientific-outcome axis for a universal flu vaccine may sit somewhere between the conventional-trimer end of the spectrum and the fully-engineered stem-nanoparticle end — not necessarily at the far-engineered extreme. That framing has implications for what "sophisticated" means when evaluating computationally designed antigens like the Epigraph-optimized c-H-A-N-A vaccine we just discussed — the right benchmark is not the naive strain but a well-formulated conventional trimer, and the delta may be smaller than initially assumed.
