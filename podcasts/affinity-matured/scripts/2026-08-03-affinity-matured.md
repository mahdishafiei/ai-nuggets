## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured. Three items today. First, TriFlow — a new sequence-design model from the Jian Zhou and Qian Cong groups that combines a RoseTTAFold-style three-track architecture with discrete flow matching, outperforms Protein-M-P-N-N on the shared benchmark, and when swapped into BindCraft boosts in-silico binder success rates across a five-hundred-target sweep. Second, an anti-parainfluenza antibody paper from the Mousa lab at Florida State that maps two human monoclonals against the parainfluenza-three hemagglutinin-neuraminidase, cryo-E-M solves the epitope of the more interesting one, and demonstrates cross-protection to parainfluenza-one via a related antibody in a hamster challenge model. Third, a bioRxiv benchmark from the Pierce and Orban groups at the University of Maryland that puts AlphaFold-two and a panel of other deep-learning and physics-based tools through an experimental fold-switching test bed, and finds that mutation-induced fold-state prediction remains partially unsolved even for state-of-the-art models.

First item. bioRxiv, version two posted August first, "Modeling the structure-conditioned sequence landscape for large-scale protein design with TriFlow," from the Zhou lab at the University of Chicago and the Cong lab at U-T Southwestern Medical Center — corresponding author Jian Zhou. The paper is framed against the current sequence-design orthodoxy, which is Protein-M-P-N-N and its close relatives. Those methods use message-passing graph neural networks over the residue graph, and — as the authors point out — the local receptive field is a real limitation. Message passing propagates information through the graph over multiple layers, but the effective attention window stays local. That is fine for the many design tasks where the important structural context is nearby residues, but it hurts on tasks where a residue needs to reason about something structurally distant — for example, a binding-interface residue that has to be compatible with a long-range hydrogen-bonding network or a symmetry constraint on the other side of the protein.

TriFlow's architectural claim is that you can do better by using a three-track evoformer-like backbone — the same single, pair, and 3D representation topology that RoseTTAFold and AlphaFold-two use for structure prediction — but redirected at sequence design. There are two engineering choices worth calling out. First, they drop RoseTTAFold's biased axial attention and use invariant point attention plus triangle multiplicative updates. Triangle updates are the pair-track operation that lets a pairwise feature between residues i and j be updated using pairwise features between all triplets i-k-j — the mechanism that gave AlphaFold-two its long-range geometric reasoning. Keeping triangle updates but dropping axial attention is a compute-and-memory decision — triangle updates are quadratic in sequence length, and the memory savings from dropping axial attention are what makes it possible to train at reasonable batch sizes. Second, and this is the generative story: they replace auto-regressive decoding with discrete flow matching. Discrete flow matching linearly interpolates between a fully masked sequence at time zero and the native sequence at time one along a continuous-time Markov chain. At inference, you start from all-masked and step forward — but critically, at each step you decode multiple residues in parallel, not one at a time. Sequences of any length are generated in as few as ten inference steps, and the reported inference footprint is seconds per sequence versus hours per backbone for ColabDesign-style pipelines.

Yesterday we covered EffieDes — the Toulouse neuro-symbolic paper that also argued against the auto-regressive decoding paradigm, but attacked it with a very different weapon: a Potts model plus a symbolic solver. TriFlow attacks it a third way: keep the neural network, but change the decoding schedule from left-to-right token sampling to iterative parallel refinement. There is now a genuine three-way race for the future of inverse folding, and the failure mode of the incumbent — early residues locked in without knowledge of downstream residues — is showing up as the shared complaint across all three challengers.

On to the benchmarks. On the shared Protein-M-P-N-N test set — 1,392 structures held out from the same P-D-B splits — TriFlow reports higher native sequence recovery than Protein-M-P-N-N, E-S-M-I-F, and PiFold. Exact numbers are in the figures rather than the text, so treat this as a directional claim rather than a specific delta, but the sweep is against every deep-learning inverse folder in the standard comparison set. The more useful benchmark for anyone doing binder design is the ColabDesign backbone benchmark, where TriFlow was fed backbones ranging from one hundred to one thousand residues generated by ColabDesign, and the metric was refoldability — take the designed sequence, structure-predict it, and check that the predicted structure matches the target backbone. TriFlow outperforms all comparison models on refoldability across all lengths, and the gap widens at longer lengths. This is exactly what you would predict from the global-attention architecture claim: local context alone gets you through short designs, but long proteins need long-range reasoning, and Protein-M-P-N-N's message-passing depth caps out.

The binder-design experiments are where the paper earns its billing as a large-scale study. On a five-hundred-target computational sweep, the authors ran a full R-F-diffusion pipeline — generate diverse backbones for each target, use a sequence designer to write sequences on those backbones, then score with a structure predictor for pass or fail. Across 16,216 backbones and 129,728 designed sequences in total, TriFlow beat Protein-M-P-N-N on 143 targets and lost on 68 — a two-to-one ratio in TriFlow's favor. When they swapped ColabDesign for R-F-diffusion as the backbone generator and compared to SolubleMPNN, TriFlow won on 200 targets and lost on 93. On a smaller therapeutic-target set of twenty-four proteins, replacing SolubleMPNN with TriFlow inside the BindCraft pipeline improved the structure success rate for all but one target. Two things stand out. First, TriFlow is not just a drop-in replacement for Protein-M-P-N-N — it beats it on the majority of targets, but there is a nontrivial minority where the older model wins, and the paper is honest about that split rather than claiming universal dominance. Second, the improvements compound: TriFlow inside BindCraft, inside R-F-diffusion pipelines, gives you a better result than any of the individual components alone.

Experimental validation is where the paper is thinner and the authors are candid about it. They tested designs against three targets — P-D-L-one, B-B-F-fourteen, and Cas-nine — and got binders for two of the three. The measured affinities were sub-micromolar for P-D-L-one at roughly five-hundred-thirty-five nanomolar dissociation constant, and nanomolar for B-B-F-fourteen at fifty-four nanomolar. The success rate matches what BindCraft alone achieves — so at this small experimental scale, TriFlow's in-silico advantage does not yet translate into a wet-lab-visible win. The authors are explicit that this is a preliminary experimental validation of a small number of candidate designs, and that the real test is scaling the wet-lab campaign. For now, treat the paper as strong on in-silico benchmarks and provisional on wet-lab.

The paper's final flourish is a systematic design of binders against thirty-one human class-one cytokines — the receptor-signaling cytokines including the interleukin family — where the pipeline was tasked with generating specific binders that hit the on-target cytokine while avoiding off-target family members. They got binders for all thirty-one cytokines except I-L-seven. And here is the interesting compute-scaling result: specificity scores improved monotonically as the number of designed sequences per target increased, with the largest jump between one and thirty sequences per target. That is a directly useful operating characteristic for anyone thinking about how much inference-time compute to allocate — the specificity return on additional sampling is real and it plateaus in a predictable place. For antibody-engineering audiences, the cytokine result is the most immediately relevant piece of the paper. Anti-cytokine biologics are one of the largest therapeutic categories in the industry — checkpoint inhibitors, anti-I-L-six for autoimmune disease, anti-I-L-thirteen and I-L-four-alpha for atopic disease — and off-target family-member binding is a persistent developability and safety problem. Being able to computationally optimize the specificity axis at design time, and being able to trade compute for specificity in a predictable way, is a genuinely useful capability.

Software and generated resources are available at triflow.zhoulab.io. The training data expands beyond the P-D-B splits Protein-M-P-N-N used, incorporating interacting-domain pairs predicted by AlphaFold Database — the kind of large-scale interaction data that ProteinFlow, Latent-ProteinLM, and a few others have started drawing on as well. If you are doing binder design and running R-F-diffusion plus BindCraft today, TriFlow is worth benchmarking against your Protein-M-P-N-N baseline on your specific target set — and given the two-to-one win-rate at scale, it is likely worth adopting for a nontrivial fraction of your pipeline.

Second item. Journal of Virology, posted online July thirty-first, "Molecular basis for protection and cross-protection by human antibodies targeting the parainfluenza virus hemagglutinin-neuraminidase protein," from the Mousa lab in Biomedical Sciences at Florida State University. Parainfluenza viruses are a top cause of respiratory illness in children, immunocompromised adults, and the elderly. There are currently no licensed vaccines or targeted antivirals for parainfluenza-three or parainfluenza-one, which are the two subtypes responsible for most disease burden. The two large surface glycoproteins on parainfluenza — the fusion protein and the hemagglutinin-neuraminidase, or H-N — are both potential targets for neutralizing antibodies, and the H-N is arguably the more interesting one because it combines receptor binding through sialic acid engagement with an intrinsic neuraminidase activity that cleaves sialic acid from cell-surface glycans. Anti-H-N antibodies can block receptor binding, block neuraminidase, or block conformational transitions that trigger fusion, so the epitope choice determines the mechanism of neutralization.

The authors identified and characterized two new human monoclonal antibodies, called 5217-2 and 5217-9, against the parainfluenza-three H-N. Both bind recombinant P-I-V-three H-N, recognize P-I-V-three-infected cells, and neutralize in cell culture. Where they differ is in epitope. Antibody 5217-9 targets a previously defined neutralizing site, and confirms that site as a therapeutically actionable epitope. Antibody 5217-2 targets a new site — one that had not been previously characterized — and this is where the paper gets structurally interesting. The authors solved a cryo-electron microscopy structure of the 5217-2 Fab in complex with the P-I-V-three H-N, revealing an epitope adjacent to the receptor-binding site on H-N. Adjacent is not overlapping — the antibody is not directly blocking the sialic acid binding pocket — but the proximity is enough that steric interference could plausibly disrupt receptor engagement, which is one candidate mechanism for the neutralization the authors observe.

They also solved a companion cryo-electron microscopy structure of an earlier antibody, called P-I-V-three-H-N-oh-nine, in complex with H-N. That structure confirmed the epitope of a previously described antibody that had been shown to be partially protective in animal models. Together, the two structures expand the antigenic landscape of parainfluenza-three H-N to include two experimentally validated neutralization epitopes with defined structural footprints — the classical site 5217-9 targets, and the newly defined site 5217-2 targets adjacent to the receptor-binding pocket.

The protection experiments are where the paper delivers the wet-lab-validation payoff. In a hamster challenge model of parainfluenza-three, prophylactic administration of 5217-2 significantly reduced lung viral titers compared to control. Separately, an older antibody — P-I-V-three-H-N-oh-five — had been previously shown to cross-neutralize parainfluenza-one in vitro, and the authors extended that to an in-vivo test: in a parainfluenza-one hamster challenge model, P-I-V-three-H-N-oh-five reduced lung viral titers, confirming its cross-protective activity in an animal model. So the paper contributes two things simultaneously: a new epitope on parainfluenza-three H-N and a new protective antibody against it, and confirmation that cross-neutralization at the biolayer-interferometry level translates to actual protection in a parainfluenza-one animal model.

For AI-for-antibody audiences, the practical implication is on the immunogen-design side. Structure-based vaccine design for parainfluenza has been held back by an incomplete map of neutralizing epitopes on H-N, and by uncertainty about which epitopes support cross-subtype protection. The new structural coordinates from these cryo-electron microscopy complexes give computational vaccine designers a concrete target to try to stabilize and present — either the classical 5217-9 epitope, the newly mapped 5217-2 epitope adjacent to the receptor-binding site, or the P-I-V-three-H-N-oh-five epitope that carries cross-protective activity to parainfluenza-one. That is the same trajectory Peter Kwong's group at V-R-C and the Baker lab have run for R-S-V pre-fusion F and for H-I-V envelope: cryo-electron microscopy structure of a well-neutralizing antibody complex, then de novo scaffold design to present the epitope in a rigid, native-like conformation for immunogen use. Parainfluenza is a smaller field with fewer resources, but the pipeline is now unblocked for H-N — that is the take-home for anyone thinking about structure-based immunogen design for pediatric respiratory viruses.

Third item. bioRxiv, posted August second, "Benchmarking Deep Learning Predictions of Mutation-Induced Fold Switching," from the John Orban and Brian Pierce groups at the Institute for Bioscience and Biotechnology Research and the University of Maryland, with the National Cancer Institute as a partner. The problem it addresses is one of the sharpest open questions about what AlphaFold-two and its descendants actually learned. Structure prediction on wild-type sequences from natural proteins is largely solved — AlphaFold-two, AlphaFold-three, Chai, Boltz, ESMFold all get to sub-two-angstrom accuracy on most globular domains. What is not solved, and what has real practical consequences for anyone designing or engineering proteins, is prediction of the effects of individual mutations on the structural state.

The fold-switching system the authors benchmark on is the classical GA/GB model, first developed by Orban's group over the last two decades. GA is a three-helix bundle. GB is a four-strand-plus-one-helix mixed alpha-beta fold. What makes them a fold-switching pair is that a small number of point mutations at key positions can convert a sequence stably in the GA fold into a sequence stably in the GB fold, or into a sequence that populates both folds in equilibrium, or into an unfolded state. Because the two folds are structurally completely different — different topology, different secondary-structure composition — this is a genuinely severe test of a model's ability to reason about the fold-defining consequences of individual residue identities.

The authors constructed a systematic N-M-R-characterized dataset of GA/GB variants. They introduced substitutions at three key positions that individually tip the equilibrium toward the three-helix GA fold, toward the four-strand-plus-helix GB fold, toward a mixed population, or toward the unfolded state. And critically, they measured fold-state populations by N-M-R, which is one of the very few solution-state techniques that can quantitatively resolve equilibrium populations between two distinct folds in the same sample. This gives them a quantitative experimental benchmark at the mutation level — not just "does the sequence adopt fold A or fold B" but what fraction is in each state, and how does substitution at position X shift that fraction.

They then ran the benchmark against a panel of deep-learning tools and physics-based methods. AlphaFold-two-based algorithms — presumably including multiple-M-S-A-subsampling protocols, C-F-clust and similar approaches that have been shown to sometimes recover alternate conformations from single sequences — were the strongest performers, and were able to predict mutant effects at individual sites. The paper's authors frame this as evidence that AlphaFold-two-based models have some understanding of the physical consequences of residue substitutions, not just a memory of the P-D-B distribution. But — and this is the honest limitation the paper leads with — the success is variable and position-dependent. At some positions the tools track the experimental fold-state shift well. At other positions they miss. And when the same predictions are compared to experimentally measured stability changes, the position-dependent success pattern shows up again, with general challenges the paper flags as remaining work.

The reason this matters for antibody-engineering work is that antibody variable regions, and especially C-D-Rs, are structurally polymorphic. The kind of position-dependent, mutation-level prediction failure this paper documents is exactly the failure mode that limits how much you can trust AlphaFold-two or its descendants when you sample mutations in a directed-evolution loop. If the model gets structural changes right at some positions and wrong at others, and you don't know in advance which is which, you cannot straightforwardly use predicted structure or predicted structure-based scores as a fitness function. This paper provides a quantitative, N-M-R-anchored, systematic experimental benchmark against which any new method — AlphaFold-three, Boltz-two, Chai-one, ESMFold-two, or any of the many diffusion-based structure predictors — can be evaluated on the specific hard task of mutation-induced structural change. The authors' framing is measured and honest — they describe capabilities and limitations, not a definitive verdict — and the dataset itself is arguably the more durable contribution than any single method comparison. Antibody-engineering audiences should watch whether this benchmark, or its successors, gets adopted by the structure-prediction community the way CASP has been. Until it is, mutation-effect predictions from any of these models should be treated as directional signals in a design loop, not ground truth.

That is the three for today. TriFlow is the headline — a serious new challenger to Protein-M-P-N-N with a global-attention architecture, discrete flow matching, and a five-hundred-target computational win over the incumbent, with cytokine-family specificity design as the practical demonstration. The Mousa-lab parainfluenza-three antibody paper delivers a new cryo-electron microscopy-mapped neutralizing epitope on H-N and cross-protection data in a parainfluenza-one animal model — the kind of structural-immunogen input that antibody-informed vaccine design pipelines need. And the Orban and Pierce fold-switching benchmark is a hard test case where AlphaFold-two-based models show some understanding of mutation effects but not enough to trust position-by-position. If you are doing binder design and running R-F-diffusion plus BindCraft today, TriFlow is the read. If you are thinking about pediatric-respiratory-virus immunogen design, the parainfluenza paper is the read. And if you are evaluating any structure predictor for mutation-effect prediction in an antibody or protein-engineering loop, the fold-switching benchmark is the read.
