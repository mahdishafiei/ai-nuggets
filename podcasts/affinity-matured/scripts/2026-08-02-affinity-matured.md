## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured. Three items today, all published in the last two days, and all pointed at practical problems in antibody and nanobody engineering. First, a generative neuro-symbolic framework called EffieDes that beats auto-regressive inverse folding — including ProteinMPNN — on native sequence recovery, and validates itself experimentally by de novo designing a nanomolar nanobody against an immune-evasive SARS-CoV-two variant. Second, a computational nanobody-discovery pipeline out of Harbin Medical University that couples AlphaFold-two structures with physics-based ensemble docking and MM slash GBSA re-scoring, and hits roughly seventy percent binder recovery among top-ranked candidates against three cancer targets. And third, a Genentech Antibody Engineering paper in MAbs showing that PEG-stabilized self-interaction nanoparticle spectroscopy — PS-SINS for short — predicts high-concentration viscosity of I-g-G-one therapeutics well enough to serve as a real early-stage developability filter, including on samples with only a single Protein-A purification step.

First item. Advanced Science, posted online July thirtieth, "A Generative Neuro-Symbolic AI for Protein Sequence Design," from the TBI institute at the University of Toulouse with collaborators at MIAT, INRAE, and LISM in Marseille — corresponding authors Thomas Schiex and Sophie Barbe. The framing is the sharpest critique yet of what has become the standard paradigm in inverse folding. Auto-regressive samplers — Structured Transformer, GVP, ProteinMPNN — decode residues one at a time, conditioning each choice on the residues already fixed. The chain rule of probability says this is theoretically equivalent to sampling from the joint distribution, so long as you sample at temperature one. In practice everyone runs ProteinMPNN at temperatures between zero-point-one and zero-point-two because those temperatures give higher A-lphaFold p-L-D-D-T, and once you drop temperature you have drifted away from the joint. Early residues get locked in — an alanine chosen at position twelve, before the network has any information about what will need to happen at position eighty — and there is no backtracking. If the early alanine precludes a stabilizing hydrogen bond that a lower-probability arginine would have enabled, you never find out. The authors call this a failure to think ahead, and they cite the same limitation in large language models on planning, path-finding, and Sudoku benchmarks.

Their fix is to separate the neural fitness estimator from the sampler. The neural component, called Effie-N-N, takes a backbone structure and predicts a pairwise interaction score for every residue pair within fifteen angstroms — twenty by twenty amino-acid score matrices, one per pair. Sum those pairwise terms and you get a Potts model over the whole backbone. Potts models are the classical statistical-physics representation used in Rosetta's energy function and in coevolutionary sequence models. Crucially they express the full joint distribution as a decomposable graphical model, so you can plug them into a symbolic solver — a stochastic optimizer or an exact automated reasoning prover — and search for the maximum a posteriori sequence globally, without ever sampling residues one at a time. External constraints — sequence composition rules, multi-state design objectives, positional identity constraints for symmetries — can be added without retraining the neural network, which is a substantial engineering advantage over models where every constraint requires a new fine-tune.

The benchmarks. On Ingraham's single-chain dataset, EffieDes hits thirty-three percent native sequence recovery, versus seventeen-point-nine percent for Rosetta — striking because Rosetta uses an explicit side-chain atomistic representation and EffieDes uses only a learned implicit one. Against auto-regressive deep-learning methods on the same test set — Structured Transformer, GVP, ProteinMPNN — EffieDes still comes out ahead. When trained instead on ProteinMPNN's multi-chain soluble-protein dataset, the improvements carry over. Confusion is dominated by chemically similar pairs — leucine versus isoleucine, aspartate versus glutamate — and predicted amino-acid frequencies match natural distributions. So far, this is a stronger inverse-folder.

The two experimental validations are where the paper earns its keep. The first is a multi-state design problem — engineering orthogonal pairs of proteins that share a common fold but self-assemble only into heteromeric pairs, not into their natural homomeric assembly. This is exactly the kind of task where auto-regressive samplers have to hack their way in with linear combinations of logits, drifting further from any probabilistic guarantee. EffieDes bakes the multi-state constraint into the symbolic solver natively. The second, and the one closest to the interests of this audience, is the de novo design of a selective nanobody targeting SARS-CoV-two — specifically an immune-evasive variant. The nanobody was designed at the sequence level from a previously unseen C-D-R-loop structure and experimentally characterized as nanomolar-affinity and selective. This is a de novo binder against a functionally relevant target, from a neuro-symbolic framework, achieving affinity comparable to what R-F-antibody and related structure-based methods have been claiming — but with a fundamentally different generative principle. If the sequence-diversity claim holds up under broader benchmarking, this is the first serious challenger to the auto-regressive orthodoxy in inverse folding since ProteinMPNN itself, and it comes with a clean story about why you would want to move past next-token prediction for constrained biological problems.

Second item. Chemical Science, posted online July twenty-first and PubMed-indexed August first, "Integrating AlphaFold-two with physics-based ensemble docking for high-efficiency nanobody discovery," from the Department of Immunology at Harbin Medical University with the College of Pharmacy at Harbin — corresponding authors Bin Sun, Wenjing Xing, and Jia Ma. The problem here is squarely operational rather than architectural. When you build a nanobody library from an immunized camelid, the resulting c-D-N-A pool is enormous — ten-to-the-ninth to ten-to-the-tenth unique clones — and the standard panning workflow selects on binding enrichment through iterative phage-display rounds. That works, but it is biased toward the most abundant post-panning clones and loses functional binders that were less amplified. The question is whether you can rank candidate sequences from N-G-S data through structural computation, and get to functional binders faster and with more sequence diversity than random clone-picking gives you.

The pipeline the authors propose has three stages. First, N-G-S-derived candidate nanobody sequences are folded with AlphaFold-two to produce predicted V-H-H structures. Second, each candidate is docked against the target antigen using an ensemble docking strategy rather than a single antigen structure — this matters because antigen conformational flexibility is often what determines whether a plausible binder actually engages the epitope, and single-conformation docking systematically misses the shifts. Third, top docked poses are re-scored using M-M slash G-B-S-A binding free-energy calculations, which explicitly compute solvation and conformational entropy contributions rather than trusting the docking score alone.

The engineering choice they make is worth calling out. Before deploying the workflow, they benchmark it against the current defaults — AlphaFold-three for sequence-only complex prediction, HDOCK, Z-DOCK, and Clus-Pro for physics-based docking — on a curated set of nanobody-antigen co-crystal structures from the S-Ab-Dab database. Under their sampling settings, HDOCK recovers the native binding pose within two angstroms r-M-S-D fifty-two percent of the time as the top-one pose, versus twenty-six percent for Z-DOCK, eighteen percent for AlphaFold-three, and one to four percent for Clus-Pro variants. They flag the AlphaFold-three comparison as exploratory — only ten seeds per interaction, which is not a fair benchmark of AlphaFold-three in isolation — so this should not be read as a definitive AlphaFold-three-versus-HDOCK claim. What it is is a justification for their choice: HDOCK plus M-M slash G-B-S-A re-scoring outperforms sequence-only AlphaFold-three under the compute budget a small immunology lab actually has.

The deployment test is three cancer-relevant antigens — mesothelin, P-D-one, and Nectin-four. For each, the pipeline was applied to N-G-S sequences from immunized camelid libraries, top-ranked candidates were expressed in E. coli, and binding was assessed by flow cytometry, E-L-I-S-A, and surface plasmon resonance. At least seven of every ten top-ranked candidates showed strong flow-cytometry binding to antigen-overexpressing cells with no signal on vector controls — a paired-cell design that controls for background — and representative candidates reached nanomolar K-D values by S-P-R. Importantly, the pipeline recovered functional nanobodies from N-G-S copy-number ranks well below the most abundant post-panning clones, meaning it accesses sequence diversity that random monoclonal clone-picking simply does not see. The whole workflow runs on a single workstation with an R-T-X forty-eighty G-P-U — twelve minutes for ensemble docking and six minutes for M-M slash G-B-S-A re-ranking per V-H-H candidate — and the code is public on Git-Hub. This is not a foundation-model paper, but it is a straightforward, reproducible, small-lab-deployable pipeline that closes a real bottleneck for anyone running camelid immunization campaigns.

Third item. M-Abs, published July thirtieth, "Self-interaction nanoparticle spectroscopy predicts high-concentration viscosity of therapeutic I-g-G-one antibodies," from Genentech's Analytical Development, Pharmaceutical Development, and Antibody Engineering groups in South San Francisco — first author Santosh Paidi with senior author Paul Carter. This is a developability paper aimed at one of the more painful problems in translating a discovery-stage antibody to a clinical formulation. Subcutaneous delivery, which is where the market is going for chronic-dose biologics, requires high-concentration formulations — typically over one hundred and fifty milligrams per milliliter — and at those concentrations some I-g-G-one antibodies become too viscous to inject through a standard needle. Viscosity is driven by nonspecific self-association in solution and is notoriously hard to predict from sequence or from low-concentration measurements. The gold-standard readout is direct rheometry on the high-concentration formulation, which requires milligram quantities and is low-throughput — so by the time you find out a candidate is unmanageably viscous, you have already invested substantial process-development effort.

The authors evaluate a colloidal-assay approach called self-interaction nanoparticle spectroscopy, or S-I-N-S, in two variants. Charge-stabilized S-I-N-S, or C-S-S-I-N-S, uses gold nanoparticles stabilized by surface charge and reads out the perturbation caused by the antibody's self-interaction. P-E-G-stabilized S-I-N-S, or P-S-S-I-N-S, uses P-E-G-coated nanoparticles that screen the charge contribution and read out a more purely hydrophobic self-interaction signal. They characterize ninety-six I-g-G-one antibodies across both assays, alongside dynamic light scattering and a panel of in-silico descriptors including surface hydrophobicity, patch analysis, and charge properties. Then they train random-forest binary classifiers to predict whether a candidate is high or low viscosity at concentration.

The result. C-S-S-I-N-S correlates strongly with computed charge — meaning it does not add much orthogonal information beyond what an in-silico charge analysis already gives you. P-S-S-I-N-S is the useful signal. Integrating P-S-S-I-N-S with in-silico descriptors and dynamic light scattering significantly improves the random-forest classifier's accuracy on binary viscosity prediction. And when P-S-S-I-N-S is measured in multiple buffers, the buffer-specific signals carry complementary information — enough that you can achieve comparable accuracy without needing the dynamic light scattering readout at all. That is the throughput win: dynamic light scattering on ninety-six samples is real instrument time, and if you can drop it, the assay footprint shrinks.

Two more findings sharpen the practical story. First, on F-c-region variants of two clinical antibodies — trastuzumab and omalizumab — P-S-S-I-N-S scores follow a strong logarithmic relationship with high-concentration viscosity, with Pearson correlations of zero-point-seven-two and zero-point-eight-five respectively. That is a direct mechanistic link between what P-S-S-I-N-S measures and what the formulation team ends up limited by — not just a correlation across a diverse panel, but a within-molecule relationship as F-c mutations are dialed in. Second, and this is the deployment-relevant claim: P-S-S-I-N-S performs reliably on samples with only a single column of Protein-A purification. That is the material state at very early stages of antibody engineering, before an antibody has gone through polishing steps. Being able to make the viscosity call from one-column material shifts developability filtering upstream, into the phase where you can still afford to discard candidates cheaply.

The scope caveat: this is I-g-G-one only, and both the training set and the validation examples are conventional monoclonals. Whether P-S-S-I-N-S transfers to bispecifics, extended-C-D-R-H-three antibodies, or unconventional formats is not addressed, and given how strongly the extended-loop-developability paper we covered yesterday leaned on hydrophobic-patch predictors, it is at least plausible that P-S-S-I-N-S has more to say about those formats than a machine-learning-only workflow could. But as a first line of viscosity triage on standard I-g-G-one pipelines — from Genentech's own Antibody Engineering group, with ninety-six molecules and real trastuzumab and omalizumab variants in the validation — this is a well-characterized, deployment-ready tool.

That is the three for today. EffieDes is a foundations paper — a serious challenge to auto-regressive inverse folding, backed by an experimentally validated de novo SARS-CoV-two nanobody. The Harbin AlphaFold-two plus ensemble-docking pipeline is workflow engineering — a small-lab-deployable framework that gets seventy percent hit rates on functional nanobody discovery across three cancer targets. And the Genentech P-S-S-I-N-S paper is a practical developability tool that can be run on Protein-A one-column material and gives a real correlation with high-concentration viscosity. If you are doing generative antibody design and reading this week's inverse-folding literature, EffieDes is worth reading in full. If you are running camelid immunization pipelines, the Harbin paper is worth cloning. If you are thinking about high-concentration formulation early, the Genentech paper is worth incorporating into your triage. That's it for today — thanks for listening, and I'll be back tomorrow.
