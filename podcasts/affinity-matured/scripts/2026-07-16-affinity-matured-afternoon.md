## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your afternoon briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is Thursday, July sixteenth, and this is the second edition of the day. Two items — both directly in the wheelhouse of this show, and both from bioRxiv this week. First, a deep-mutational-scanning-plus-deep-learning strategy from a French group at Paris-Saclay to engineer species cross-reactivity into a fully human anti-P-D-L-one antibody, using only four or five mutations rather than the thirteen to fifteen that conventional maturation demanded. Second, a paper from the Sahtoe and Knowles labs at Hubrecht and Cambridge on de novo protein binders against the aggregation-driving segment of alpha-synuclein, an intrinsically disordered target that has resisted conventional ligand discovery for years. Both are technically substantial and both have wet-lab validation that goes beyond binding assays.

Let's start with the cross-reactive antibody paper, because it directly touches the daily craft of anyone doing antibody engineering with a therapeutic pipeline in mind.

The paper is titled "Machine Learning-Guided Engineering of High-Affinity Cross-Reactive Antibodies with Minimal Mutations," corresponding author Hervé Nozach of C-E-A Paris-Saclay, posted to bioRxiv today. The problem they attack is one that a lot of listeners have run into personally: an antibody raised against a human antigen fails to recognize the corresponding mouse ortholog, which limits its evaluation in preclinical models. The workarounds — surrogate mouse-specific antibodies, or transgenic mice expressing the human antigen — each have their own well-known blind spots. What you actually want is a single antibody that binds both orthologs with therapeutic-grade affinity, so you can carry the same molecule from mouse efficacy studies into first-in-human trials. Yeast display and phage display can do this, but they tend to produce heavily mutated hits — thirteen to fifteen substitutions relative to the parental sequence in this study — which reduces humanness, may compromise developability, and doesn't tell you which of those mutations actually matter.

The starting molecule is C-four, a fully human anti-P-D-L-one antibody with nanomolar affinity for human P-D-L-one and only weak binding to mouse P-D-L-one — dissociation constant in the two-hundred to three-hundred nanomolar range. The three big P-D-L-one blockers — Avelumab, Atezolizumab, and Durvalumab — recognize very similar overlapping epitopes on the human antigen, but Durvalumab does not cross-react with the murine ortholog. So the target species barrier is real for this epitope class.

The workflow has three phases, and understanding each of them is the point.

Phase one is standard deep mutational scanning. They expressed C-four as a Fab on the surface of yeast, made a single-mutant library across the variable heavy and light chains using S-O-E-P-C-R with N-N-K codons, and sorted by F-A-C-S for improved binding to either the mouse or the human antigen. The critical detail here is that they used multi-layer heatmaps to identify positions where a substitution simultaneously improved mouse binding and preserved or improved human binding. Those hotspots are centered on C-D-R-H-one, H-two, H-three, and framework three, spanning about twenty positions. This is deep mutational scanning done the way it should be done for a cross-reactivity problem — with joint constraints on both antigens, not one at a time.

Phase two is a combinatorial library over those twenty positions, sorted at progressively lower mouse P-D-L-one concentrations. From twenty randomly picked colonies, ten were expressed as full I-g-G in mammalian cells and characterized by biolayer interferometry. All ten cluster tightly in the iso-affinity plot with sub-nanomolar to low-nanomolar dissociation constants for both human and mouse P-D-L-one — a thousand-fold improvement over parental C-four for the mouse antigen. The best of these is called D-M-S-dot-one. But the number of substitutions per clone ranges from thirteen to fifteen, and the sequence logo shows that some residues are conserved across all clones — obvious hotspots — while others vary or are only present in some clones. This is exactly the situation where you cannot tell from the sequences alone which mutations are doing the work.

Phase three is the deep learning part, and this is where the paper's contribution lies. Rather than fit a regression model to affinity, they framed the problem as three-way classification: parental-like binding, enhanced binding, or strongly enhanced binding. The three F-A-C-S gates from the combinatorial sort — minus, plus, and plus-plus — give you the labels for free. Deep sequencing of the sorted populations gave them seventeen thousand, twenty thousand, and twenty-six thousand unique sequences in the three gates respectively — a well-balanced training set. They compared several architectures — random forest, X-G-Boost, and a Conv-Next-style convolutional neural network — and settled on the C-N-N because it could be trained directly on the full sequence and would therefore pick up on serendipitous mutations outside the designed positions. An ensemble of three models trained on different downsampled subsets of the data gave test accuracies of eighty-eight to ninety-two percent on the three-class problem.

Now, the interesting design step. Rather than run generative sequence design, they took the ten highly-mutated D-M-S variants and enumerated all possible subsets of their mutations — one hundred and twenty thousand sequences in total — then classified each one with the C-N-N ensemble. They filtered for sequences the ensemble unanimously predicted as belonging to the highest-affinity class, with confidence above eighty percent. Only nine sequences met the criterion; the minimum mutational load was four substitutions. This is an interpretability-first search: the model tells you which mutations are individually load-bearing versus dispensable, without you having to run mutation-effect predictions in isolation and lose the couplings.

The four-mutation variants — the D-L series — were expressed and characterized. Their dissociation constants for both human and mouse P-D-L-one are indistinguishable from the fifteen-mutation D-M-S variants, clustered in the same region of the iso-affinity plot. Humanness — a metric of similarity to the human germline repertoire — is ninety-five percent for the four-mutation D-L variants, compared to eighty-six to eighty-nine percent for the D-M-S variants. That is a meaningful gain from a developability standpoint. And when they mapped the functional epitope using D-M-S on the antigen — testing the binding of monovalent Fabs from parental C-four and both engineered variants against mutated antigens — the epitope on both human and mouse P-D-L-one remained centered on the same region, overlapping with the P-D-one binding site, meaning both engineered antibodies preserve the mechanism of action.

The final experiment is the one that matters clinically. They ran a C-T-twenty-six syngeneic colorectal cancer model in immunocompetent mice — five groups of ten mice, treated on days zero, three, and seven with Avelumab, parental C-four, D-M-S-dot-one, D-L-dot-one, or an isotype control. Median survival was eleven to eighteen-and-a-half days for the control and parental C-four groups, and twenty-six to thirty-and-a-half days for the engineered antibodies. D-L-dot-one, with only four mutations, matched D-M-S-dot-one, with fifteen. And both matched Avelumab, a fully approved anti-P-D-L-one antibody. Parental C-four, the original starting molecule, was statistically indistinguishable from the isotype control.

Two things worth pulling out for the audience. First, the deep-learning stage isn't doing generative de novo design here — it's doing minimal-mutation search over a combinatorial space defined by an experimental campaign. That's a much easier problem than de novo affinity design, and it uses D-M-S data as its own training signal, which is data that many antibody engineering groups already generate. If you have a phage or yeast display campaign with sequenced sorted populations, this is a workflow you can run on your existing data with fairly modest compute. Second, the interpretability comes from the enumeration step. Rather than trust the model to generate designs directly, they use the model as a classifier over an exhaustively enumerated space, which lets them read out mutational couplings — for example, they identified that either L-fifty-five-V or G-fifty-six-P is required but not both, an interchangeability that would be hard to spot from single-mutation D-M-S alone. That's a design pattern worth remembering: when your combinatorial space is small enough to enumerate, doing so with a well-trained classifier is often more informative than running a generative model.

Let's move to the second paper.

The title is "Potent substoichiometric inhibition of alpha-synuclein aggregation by de novo oligomer-binding proteins." Corresponding authors are Danny Sahtoe at the Hubrecht Institute in Utrecht and Tuomas Knowles at Cambridge. It was posted to bioRxiv two days ago in the biophysics category. Sahtoe was in the Baker lab before setting up his own group; Knowles is the leading name in physical chemistry of protein aggregation kinetics — this is a productive combination.

The target is the non-amyloid-beta component, or N-A-C domain, of alpha-synuclein — residues sixty-one through ninety-five. This is the segment that drives alpha-synuclein aggregation in Parkinson's disease. It is intrinsically disordered in the monomer, meaning it lacks the stable folded structure that conventional small-molecule and antibody discovery relies on. But it adopts an extended beta-strand conformation in mature fibrils. The design premise is to build a compact scaffold that recognizes and stabilizes this beta-strand geometry as if it were a peptide, effectively trapping the target segment in a non-amyloidogenic complex. Similar ideas have been demonstrated for amylin, G-three-B-P-one, and short amyloid-beta segments — this paper extends that concept to a therapeutically important disordered target with rigorous downstream characterization.

The design pipeline is now fairly standard for de novo binder work. They ran approximately thirty thousand R-F-diffusion backbones targeting the N-A-C beta-strand as a target peptide, filtered on secondary-structure completeness by D-S-S-P and solvent-accessible surface area down to about two thousand backbones. Sequence design with Protein-M-P-N-N. Structural filtering with ColabFold, narrowing to ninety-one designs. Manual interface inspection selected twenty-one designs for expression. Twenty-one designs, three hits engaging the target both in vitro and inside living cells — a fourteen percent design success rate to a validated cellular hit, which is consistent with what other well-run R-F-diffusion campaigns have reported for peptide-binding scaffolds.

The three initial hits were called D-F-A-S-Y-N-zero-zero-eight, D-F-A-S-Y-N-zero-one-three, and D-F-A-S-Y-N-zero-one-seven, abbreviated eight, thirteen, and seventeen. Initial dissociation constants ranged from about two hundred nanomolar to about four micromolar. And then comes the affinity maturation step, and this is worth understanding because it's a general trick.

They ran a single round of partial diffusion. Partial diffusion, for anyone new to it, is the trick of noising a designed structure part of the way toward random and then re-denoising it with R-F-diffusion. It functions as a structure-aware local exploration operator — it perturbs the backbone while keeping the general fold intact, and you can re-run sequence design on the new backbones. It is an in-silico affinity maturation. From each parent, they ran a matured library, expressed five to ten variants per parent, and measured binding by biolayer interferometry.

The strongest binder, seventeen-underscore-two, was matured from a dissociation constant of two hundred and thirty-eight nanomolar to one-point-nine-four nanomolar — a hundred-and-twenty-fold improvement from a single round of partial diffusion. The other two showed modest improvements: about one-and-a-half-fold for eight-underscore-four and about nine-fold for thirteen-underscore-nine. So partial diffusion is not uniformly powerful across designs, but on a design where it works, one round can move you two orders of magnitude in affinity without any experimental library.

The characterization is where this paper distinguishes itself from many de novo binder papers. First, solution N-M-R titrations with fifteen-nitrogen-labeled binders and unlabeled alpha-synuclein peptides. Two-dimensional H-S-Q-C or H-M-Q-C spectra of the apo binders were high-quality for eight-underscore-four and thirteen-underscore-nine but lower-quality for seventeen-underscore-two, suggesting some conformational heterogeneity in the apo state. Upon adding the alpha-synuclein peptide, all three showed the peaks sharpen and new resonances appear — the fold rigidifies on binding. For eight-underscore-four, T-A-L-O-S-N-derived secondary structure indices from assigned backbone chemical shifts match the design model — beta-sheets and helices in the expected register — and through-space N-O-E contacts between backbone amides confirm the designed antiparallel beta-sheet geometry. So N-M-R shows that the design model is correct at the secondary and tertiary structure level, at least for the design where the spectra were tractable.

Second, and this is the harder test, they profiled selectivity against related amyloid cores — tau, amylin, and amyloid-beta — by biolayer interferometry. Seventeen-underscore-two showed no detectable binding to any of the three off-targets at the concentrations tested while binding its alpha-synuclein target at one-point-nine-four nanomolar. That is genuine selectivity, and the authors attribute it to the multi-point interface, particularly a flexible C-terminal tail that requires shape complementarity specific to the alpha-synuclein N-A-C sequence. Eight-underscore-four was three to twenty-fold selective for alpha-synuclein, and thirteen-underscore-nine actually bound tau, amylin, and amyloid-beta as tightly as or more tightly than its intended target — because its architecture presents a mostly-hydrophobic exposed surface that accommodates many amyloidogenic cores non-specifically. That's a useful negative result: the architecture of the interface, not just the sequence, determines selectivity, and the interface geometry that maximizes affinity is not necessarily the one that maximizes discrimination.

Third, live-cell target engagement. A HeLa cell fluorescent recruitment assay in which the N-A-C target is membrane-anchored via a pleckstrin homology domain fusion and tagged with red fluorescent protein, and each binder is expressed as an E-G-F-P fusion under doxycycline-inducible control. Pearson correlation coefficients between the two channels — zero-point-five-seven for eight-underscore-four, zero-point-seven-seven for thirteen-underscore-nine, and zero-point-nine for seventeen-underscore-two, tracking the in-vitro affinities. Controls without doxycycline or without the target showed background correlations near zero-point-zero-five. Engagement in the crowded cytoplasmic environment is real.

Fourth — and this is the section that I think is the most novel contribution — they combined binding measurements against monomer, kinetically trapped oligomers, and fibrils with seeded aggregation kinetics to identify which microscopic aggregation step each binder inhibits. Each of the three binders favors a different aggregation state: eight-underscore-four binds monomer and oligomer roughly equally at low micromolar and fibril very weakly; thirteen-underscore-nine binds monomer and fibril comparably at low nanomolar and disfavors the oligomer; seventeen-underscore-two binds all three states but preferentially binds the oligomer at one hundred and twenty-six nanomolar, about twofold better than monomer and fourfold better than fibril. In a thioflavin-T aggregation assay at ten percent substoichiometric binder-to-monomer ratio, all three completely block fibril formation for the full one hundred and eighty hour assay window. But when they varied seed concentration to separate primary nucleation, secondary nucleation, and elongation, the three binders come out with distinct mechanisms of action: eight and thirteen inhibit both elongation and secondary nucleation, while seventeen inhibits secondary nucleation almost exclusively by sequestering on-pathway oligomers, sparing elongation.

That last result is the one worth remembering. Small soluble oligomers of alpha-synuclein are the species most closely linked to neurotoxicity, and selectively engaging them has been difficult with conventional reagents. A designed binder that binds oligomers with roughly two-fold preference over monomer, and completely blocks fibril formation at substoichiometric ratios via secondary nucleation inhibition, is a mechanistically interesting therapeutic candidate. And it came out of a design campaign of twenty-one clones with one round of partial diffusion for affinity maturation.

Now the broader lessons.

First, for anyone doing R-F-diffusion-based de novo binder work: partial diffusion as a single-round affinity maturation trick, on a design that binds at all, can move you from mid-nanomolar to low-nanomolar without any experimental library. It is not uniformly powerful — one design saw one-hundred-and-twenty-fold improvement and another saw one-and-a-half-fold — but the compute cost is trivial. It should be a default step between initial screening and the more expensive experimental affinity maturation.

Second, when you're designing binders against a target with functional consequences beyond just binding — an aggregating protein, a receptor with multiple binding partners, an enzyme with active-site geometry — you want characterization that dissects mechanism, not just affinity. This paper's combination of species-of-state affinities plus kinetic seeded aggregation is the model for that kind of characterization. For antibody work against protein aggregation targets, or against multi-state receptors, the analogous experiments would be state-specific binding measurements paired with functional assays that resolve which state's engagement produces which downstream effect.

Third, and this echoes yesterday's episode on the Baker lab transmembrane beta-barrel work: de novo binder design is now a workflow that can handle intrinsically disordered targets, transmembrane targets, and structurally unconventional substrates, as long as the target admits some workable geometric anchor — a beta-strand conformation, a peptide fragment, an ordered fold. The set of targets where computational design is essentially out of reach is shrinking rapidly.

Two items for Thursday afternoon. The cross-reactive antibody paper from the Nozach group is a well-executed demonstration that D-M-S data plus a classifier trained on sorted-population labels can identify minimally-mutated variants that match the affinity of heavily-mutated ones, with a real in-vivo efficacy readout to back it up. The Sahtoe-Knowles alpha-synuclein binder paper is the current best example of de novo binder design against an intrinsically disordered aggregation target with rigorous mechanistic characterization of the microscopic aggregation steps the binders inhibit. Both are on bioRxiv, both are on the reading list. See you tomorrow.
