**First: what protein language models actually learn — protein fitness or nucleotide mutation bias, tested on the influenza A H3N2 K lineage.**

Protein language models score amino-acid substitutions as pseudo-probabilities, and the field has increasingly used these scores as proxies for fitness in tasks from variant effect prediction to evolutionary forecasting to guided design. The underlying assumption — sometimes stated explicitly, more often just implied — is that these pseudo-probabilities reflect protein-level structural and functional constraints, so a high pseudo-probability means the substitution is likely to be tolerated and a low pseudo-probability means it isn't.

The Glasgow group takes that assumption apart. Their argument is straightforward: protein language models are trained on natural amino-acid sequences that were themselves produced by mutation and selection at the nucleotide level. The mutational supply that generates candidate variants is not uniform across amino acids — it's structured by the genetic code, by strand-specific mutational biases in the underlying nucleotide substitution process, and by which amino-acid substitutions are one or two or three nucleotide changes away from any given codon. If the training corpus reflects that structured supply, then the model's pseudo-probabilities are not purely a measure of protein fitness — they conflate protein structural constraints with the historical accessibility of the underlying mutations.

To test this concretely, they use the rapid emergence of the divergent influenza A H3N2 K lineage. Influenza is a good stress test for two reasons. First, the antigenic drift regime provides a rich, well-documented set of amino-acid changes in hemagglutinin — the H in H3N2 — where selection pressure is strong and observable. Second, because H3N2 diverges rapidly, there's enough diversity within the lineage to actually decouple mutation-supply effects from selection effects statistically.

They benchmark two base protein language models — E-S-M-2 and E-S-M-C — against fine-tuned versions of these models, and they run a parameter sweep that explicitly couples or decouples empirical nucleotide-level mutational supply from the model's amino-acid pseudo-probabilities across forecasting tasks. The findings are worth stating carefully. Base protein language models implicitly learn generic nucleotide-level mutational constraints — they have absorbed some of the codon-accessibility structure from their training data without being told to. Fine-tuning on virus-specific sequence data strongly amplifies this effect. When you're predicting the binary question of which amino-acid substitutions actually get observed at all — the emergence question — adding explicit mutational-supply information on top of the pseudo-probabilities improves accuracy, confirming that the two signals are meaningfully independent and complementary. But when you flip the question to predicting the final circulating frequency of variants that have already emerged, adding mutational-supply information degrades performance — because at that stage selection dominates, and further weighting by mutational accessibility is now double-counting bias that's already been fixed in the observed variant.

They also do amino-acid-level epistatic scanning to disentangle protein structural constraints from genetic-background effects, and they identify a specific pattern in the H3N2 K lineage: an antigenic substitution — isoleucine to lysine at hemagglutinin position 160 — that looks improbable in isolation but is dependent on two co-occurring background mutations, serine to asparagine at position 144 and asparagine to aspartic acid at position 158. That's the kind of higher-order epistatic dependency that a naive pseudo-probability score treated as a fitness proxy would systematically miss.

The take-home for anyone using protein language model pseudo-probabilities in design or forecasting pipelines: these scores are a composite signal, not a pure fitness measurement, and the choice of whether to correct for mutational supply is not neutral — it depends on whether you're asking about which variants will emerge or which have already emerged. For synthetic design pipelines where you want a pure protein-fitness signal, explicit decoupling of these processes gives a cleaner objective. For antibody applications this matters directly: affinity maturation and repertoire evolution are both mutation-supply-limited at the somatic hypermutation stage and then selection-dominated afterwards, and the same distinction — emergence versus post-emergence — applies.

**Second: the AlphaFold Database expands to proteome-scale quaternary structures — one-point-eight million high-confidence protein complexes.**

The second item is an infrastructure update rather than a new model, but for anyone modeling antibody-antigen complexes, protein-protein interfaces, or interactome-scale structural biology, it materially changes what's available off the shelf. The AlphaFold Database — the original AFDB released alongside AlphaFold2 — provided monomer predictions for the observable proteome. Biology, of course, is not monomeric. Antibody-antigen recognition, receptor-ligand signaling, allosteric regulation, and much of the substrate specificity of enzymes are all interface phenomena, and the monomer database left that layer of biology structurally uncharacterized.

The update, a v2 posted this week to bioRxiv from the Google DeepMind AlphaFold team with EMBL-EBI, extends AFDB by predicting complex structures at proteome scale — twenty-three million homodimers and eight million heterodimer candidate pairs, drawn from forty-seven-hundred-and-seventy-seven proteomes including sixteen model organisms and thirty proteomes prioritized by the World Health Organization for global health targets, together with Swiss-Prot. Homodimers were predicted directly from proteome sequences; heterodimer candidates were drawn from the STRING database's physical-interaction annotations, restricted to the prioritized proteomes. Inference used AlphaFold-Multimer through an accelerated ColabFold or OpenFold pipeline, with MMseqs2 on GPU for the multiple sequence alignments against UniRef100 — no metagenomic databases, using a best-hit-per-taxon orthology filter to prevent paralogous sequences from diluting the evolutionary signal for complex prediction.

The methodological contribution is not just running the pipeline at scale — it's the confidence calibration. Beyond the standard predicted local distance difference test and interface predicted TM-score, the authors compute ipSAEmin, the minimum interaction predicted score from aligned errors between the two chains; pDockQ2; and local interaction scores; along with backbone-clash and heavy-atom-clash counts. They validate against a curated ground-truth set of nineteen-hundred-and-sixty-eight PDB homodimers released after September 2021 — post-dating the AlphaFold-Multimer training cut — with twenty-two-hundred-and-eleven PDB monomers as negative controls, and evaluate precision, recall, and F1 across candidate cutoffs. IpSAEmin emerges as the strongest discriminator between true homodimers and monomers-predicted-as-homodimers, with a stable plateau up to a cutoff of zero-point-six, at which point precision is zero-point-eight-six, recall is zero-point-six-six, and F1 is zero-point-seven-four. Their high-confidence definition combines that ipSAEmin cutoff of zero-point-six with a mean pLDDT above seventy and no more than ten backbone clashes.

Applied at scale, that filter yields one-point-eight million high-confidence homodimeric predictions from the twenty-three million candidates — about seven percent retention. For heterodimers, applying the same criteria produces fifty-seven thousand tentatively high-confidence complexes from the eight million candidates, and the authors flag that heterodimer confidence calibration is harder — the high-confidence rate correlates strongly with lower absolute chain-length difference and higher inter-chain sequence identity, meaning the current filter is biased toward homodimer-like heterodimers and a subsequent database release will attempt more representative calibration.

Two structural biology observations worth flagging. First, clustering the one-point-eight-million high-confidence homodimers plus fifty-seven-thousand heterodimers reduces the set roughly eight-fold to two-hundred-twenty-five thousand clusters, and the top one percent of non-singleton cluster representatives account for about twenty-five percent of all entries — high-confidence complex space is concentrated around a limited set of recurrent folds. Second, roughly nine percent of non-singleton clusters contain members from at least two different superkingdoms, suggesting these complexes are conserved building blocks that likely originated in a common ancestor and have been maintained through billions of years of evolution.

The paper also documents specific case studies where multimeric prediction reveals folds that don't emerge in the monomer view — a Dictyostelium transcription elongation factor whose N-terminal domain fold is assembled by domain swapping across two chains, and the monomer prediction gets it wrong. A Fonsecaea membrane protein where the dimeric assembly properly resolves the membrane boundaries that the monomer blurred. And a Mycoplasma HTH-type transcriptional regulator whose fold stabilizes only in the dimer, consistent with its known biology as a DNA-binding dimer.

For antibody-antigen modeling this matters because the heterodimer capability, even at its currently conservative confidence, provides a much larger training and benchmarking substrate than experimental complex structures in the PDB — one to three orders of magnitude more predicted complexes than experimental multimers across most proteomes. The full thirty-one million predictions will be openly bulk-downloadable, with the high-confidence subset directly browsable in the AFDB interface.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.03.27.714458

**Third: expanding all-alpha-helical de novo protein space through rational computational design — from the Woolfson lab.**

The third item is a de novo protein design paper from Derek Woolfson's group at Bristol with a large collaborator list across the UK. Woolfson's group has a long history of rational — as opposed to purely learning-based — protein design work, and this paper stakes out a specific niche in the current design landscape.

The framing is worth understanding. A lot of recent de novo protein design work is driven by generative AI: RFdiffusion, RFantibody, AlphaProteo, ProteinMPNN, and their descendants generate protein backbones or CDRs by sampling from a learned distribution over structures. These methods are extraordinarily productive at making novel proteins, and much of the current binder-design literature depends on them. But the Woolfson lab makes an argument about complementary scope: natural proteins have evolved structural complexity by modular combination of recurring domains, and roughly a quarter of natural protein domains are predominantly alpha-helical structures. If the goal is to expand the accessible space of protein architectures rather than to hit a specific functional target, systematic rational design of new alpha-helical topologies is a different tool with a different failure mode.

Their pipeline is hierarchical. Starting from a target architecture — a defined set of free-standing alpha helices — they connect those helices into topologies using sequence-to-structure relationships for helix-helix packing that are well established from decades of coiled-coil and helical-bundle work. Loops between helices are designed with computational tools, and single-chain sequences are then completed using template-based and AI-based methods. The final designs are assessed with AlphaFold predictions before selecting a small number of candidates for experimental testing.

The experimental scope is what makes the paper concrete. They design thirty-one proteins spanning fourteen different architectures and twenty-five different topologies. Of those thirty-one, twenty-three — about seventy-five percent — express as stable, monomeric, water-soluble proteins. That's a much higher hit rate than most large-scale AI-only design campaigns, and it reflects the tightly constrained nature of the rational pipeline. More striking, over thirty percent — meaning more than ten of the designs — yield X-ray crystal structures that match the intended designs to atomic accuracy. Several of these represent new-to-nature structural arrangements not present in the Protein Data Bank.

Beyond the scaffolds themselves, they functionalize several designs through one-shot design overlays that add ion binding, small-molecule binding, and protein-protein binding functionality. The paper positions these expanded all-alpha-helical scaffolds as a substrate for downstream engineering — meaning if you need a well-behaved, crystallizable, monomeric alpha-helical scaffold with a particular topology and you want to graft in a binding function or a catalytic site, this expanded set of validated scaffolds gives you more starting-point options than the natural repertoire alone.

For an antibody-engineering audience the direct interest is less in the scaffolds themselves — antibodies are beta-rich, not alpha-helical — and more in two other things. First, the demonstration that pipelines combining rational rules with AI structure assessment can achieve very high experimental success rates when the design space is well constrained. Second, the availability of these scaffolds as fusion partners or as target antigens for antibody discovery campaigns — a well-characterized alpha-helical binding surface with a known structure is a useful thing to raise antibodies against.

One caveat on depth: this preprint was posted this week with limited body-text availability at the time of drafting. The specific architectures, the sequence-to-structure rules, and the identity of the functionalized designs are in the full text.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.03.736327
