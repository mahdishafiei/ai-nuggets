## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is July fourteenth. This is a strong day for methodology across the AI-for-protein stack, and it is a light day for antibody-specific results. Five items to walk through — a domain-centric protein language model that beats a five-times-larger E-S-M-2 on remote homology, a Baker-lab platform for de novo kinase modulators that both inhibits and activates focal adhesion kinase, a wide-ranging audit of AlphaFold3 across five application areas that is deliberately more cautious than the AF-2 benchmarks were, an ESM-3 study on how far generative protein models can push topological structure using knotted proteins as a test case, and a small but sharp foundation-models paper showing that structural transfer between natural language and biology is directional, not symmetric.

Let's start with the protein language model item, because it changes the scaling picture. The paper is titled "T-E-D-l-m: domain-centric protein language models with optional structural pre-training," posted to bioRxiv on July thirteenth by Wei, Kandathil, Buchan, and David Jones at U-C-L. The premise is one that anyone who has trained on UniRef has probably thought about but not acted on. Standard protein language models like E-S-M-2 are pre-trained on full-length protein sequences. Full-length sequences interleave multiple domains connected by linkers and interspersed with intrinsically disordered regions. If you are trying to learn per-fold, per-domain regularities — the things you actually want for structure prediction, remote homology, and functional annotation — every gradient step is diluted by tokens that belong to a different fold, or no fold at all. Conventional wisdom is that this washes out with scale. The T-E-D-l-m paper is a specific test of that assumption.

The approach is to pre-train a masked language model on structurally-defined domain segments taken from a resource called The Encyclopedia of Domains — this is where the acronym T-E-D comes from. Instead of feeding the model a full-length protein and asking it to reconstruct masked residues in a mixed-fold context, they feed it a single structurally-annotated domain and ask it to reconstruct masked residues within that domain's specific fold. Two variants. T-E-D-l-m is a straight masked-language-model pre-training on domain segments with the standard objective. T-E-D-l-m-three-D adds a distance-guided contact loss that supervises the attention maps against the actual C-alpha to C-alpha contact geometry of the domain — a structural signal injected during pre-training, not just at inference.

The benchmark that matters is remote homology detection on the C-A-T-H forty-percent-identity split, at inference from sequence alone. At the final layer, the six-hundred-fifty-million-parameter T-E-D-l-m gets an area-under-the-curve at rank one of zero-point-two-eight. E-S-M-2 at three billion parameters, five times larger, gets zero-point-two-two. In other words, domain-centric pre-training beats a five-times parameter scale-up on the exact task where structural sensitivity should matter. T-E-D-l-m-three-D — the variant with the contact loss — reaches zero-point-five-zero, and that is the number that turns heads: Foldseek, which is a structure-based search tool that operates on solved or predicted three-dimensional coordinates, hits zero-point-five-three. T-E-D-l-m-three-D matches Foldseek's remote-homology sensitivity from sequence input alone at inference time, with the structural signal internalized in the model's representations.

Two probes tell you the signal is inside the representations, not just in a trained downstream head. First, attention-map probes show the contact structure surfacing in the attention weights themselves. Second, a categorical Jacobian probe — perturbing input tokens and reading out the effect on other positions' predictions — recovers the same contact pattern. So this isn't a head-only phenomenon: the pre-training objective installs the structural signal in the encoder. Zero-shot molecular-function prediction also improves substantially over E-S-M-2, while biophysical property tasks — things like solubility and thermostability that are dominated by local composition rather than fold — stay comparable. That last observation is a useful ceiling: domain-centric pre-training helps where fold-level information is what you're missing, and it does not help where the signal is composition-level.

The transferable pattern for antibody-language-model builders is worth stating directly. Antibody variable domains are, in the T-E-D sense, exactly one domain — the immunoglobulin V-fold. Every antibody sequence you feed a standard protein language model is essentially one T-E-D domain, or two if you paired heavy and light. That means the T-E-D-l-m result — pre-training on structurally-clean single-domain segments beats pre-training on mixed-fold contexts even at five-times-larger parameter budgets — is telling you that the antibody-specific language models we already have, Ant-i-B-E-R-T-y and Ab-Lang and I-g-L-M, were on the right track by restricting to variable-domain segments. And the T-E-D-l-m-three-D contact loss is a specific mechanism that maps directly onto any antibody-L-M pretraining pipeline where paired coordinates are available — from Structural Antibody Database, from AlphaFold multimer, from Ig-Fold — for supervising the attention against real interface geometry during pre-training.

The second item — the Baker lab paper — is a step forward for de novo binder design against classically hard targets. It's titled "De novo design of selective kinase modulators," posted to bioRxiv on July thirteenth. Bauer and Gyu Rie Lee are the lead authors; David Baker is the senior author. Twenty-plus author list, University of Washington Institute for Protein Design plus collaborators. The bioRxiv body isn't rendered yet — going off the abstract for the numerical details.

The problem. Protein kinases are among the most conserved protein families in the human proteome. Small-molecule kinase inhibitors are one of the largest classes of approved drugs, but selectivity is chronically hard because the A-T-P-binding pocket looks very similar across kinases. Genetically encoded modulators — proteins expressed inside a cell that bind and either inhibit or activate a specific kinase — could in principle achieve selectivity that small molecules struggle with, because a protein-protein interface is larger and can exploit features outside the A-T-P pocket. But designing them de novo has been out of reach.

What the Baker group does is design ninety-six miniprotein binders targeting focal adhesion kinase — F-A-K — that are conformation-selective. That is, each design is targeted not just to bind the kinase domain, but to stabilize one particular conformational state of the kinase — active or inactive. That framing is the key methodological move. If a design binds the active-state conformation preferentially, it should tilt the population toward active and act as an activator. If it binds the inactive conformation preferentially, it should tilt the population toward inactive and act as an inhibitor. In principle, the same design pipeline should be capable of both, and the selection between them is just which state you scaffold against.

From ninety-six designs, thirty-three modulate F-A-K kinase activity in a biochemical assay — a hit rate of about thirty-four percent, which is characteristic of modern de novo binder-design pipelines like R-F-diffusion plus Protein-M-P-N-N with strong filters. The four most potent were characterized more fully. Two are inhibitors with low-nanomolar I-C-fifty values. Two are activators that increase F-A-K activity by more than two-fold. Both effects survive the transition into living cells — when the modulators are expressed intracellularly, they preserve their in-vitro directionalities. Inhibitors keep inhibiting; activators keep activating. That confirms the modulators are hitting the kinase domain in the same way inside a cell that they do in a purified biochemistry assay, and it is what makes them useful as tools.

The last result is the one that argues this is a platform. The authors take the F-A-K inhibitors and re-design them — computationally — to target Src kinase, another tyrosine kinase with high structural similarity to F-A-K. The re-designed Src inhibitors work. Which is to say, once you have a working scaffold and interface geometry for one kinase, you can port it across the kinase family with structure-guided redesign of the interface residues. That is the standard way to build a library of tools that cover many kinases from a small number of core scaffolds — you do the hard geometric design once, and interface-residue refinement is a cheaper operation.

Two things to note. First, the design pipeline itself isn't spelled out in the abstract, which is all bioRxiv has rendered so far for this paper — but from the authorship, from the F-A-K conformational-state framing, and from the Baker-lab methodological pattern that has become standard over the last three years, this is almost certainly R-F-diffusion for backbone generation targeting the F-A-K kinase-domain surface, Protein-M-P-N-N for sequence design, AlphaFold-based validation with filters on interface p-A-E and interface confidence, and biochemical screening of the top-ranked designs. The novelty is not the design stack — it's the conformation-selective framing and the demonstration that both directions of modulation are accessible from the same platform. Second, from the audience's perspective, the F-A-K result is not directly antibody work, but it is genuinely relevant, because F-A-K modulators are exactly the kind of intracellular-target binder that antibodies cannot access — antibodies do not cross the plasma membrane. Genetically encoded miniprotein modulators sit in the design space where they complement rather than compete with antibody engineering, and the platform paper is an argument that the tooling is now good enough to attack conformationally sensitive intracellular targets systematically.

Third item. Titled "Capabilities, specificity gaps and training-data dependence of AlphaFold3 across diverse application areas," posted to bioRxiv on July thirteenth by Follonier, Liu, and colleagues at the University of Fribourg and University of Lausanne, with Pedro Beltrao as senior author. The paper is fresh enough that only the abstract is rendered on bioRxiv today — I'm going off the abstract for specifics — but the core message is clear enough to relay accurately, and it is important for anyone using AlphaFold3 in production.

The framing. AlphaFold2 changed structural biology by giving reliable monomer and multimer predictions with confidence metrics that generalized broadly. The gains were clear and they held up across application areas. AlphaFold3 extends that to an all-atom framework that natively handles nucleic acids, post-translational modifications, and small molecules. In principle, that opens the door to a much larger set of applications — antibody-antigen complexes, T-cell-receptor to peptide-M-H-C recognition, ubiquitinated protein complexes, protein-R-N-A interactions, protein-lipid interactions. The question the Beltrao group asks is: how well does that extension actually translate into task-level performance across those five application areas.

The headline conclusion is that AF-3's expanded scope does not come with AF-2-style broadly-generalisable performance gains. Specifically: AF-3 can perform well in favourable settings, but performance is uneven across application types, and in several tasks accuracy depends on whether related complexes exist in the training set. This is the training-data dependence in the title — you can get good numbers, but they may be driven by memorization of nearby examples rather than by learned general principles, and if your target sits in a region of complex-space that's poorly represented in the training corpus, the numbers can drop sharply.

The most specific criticism from the abstract is on the protein-R-N-A predictions. Model confidence — the internal p-L-D-D-T-style and interface-confidence metrics that AF-3 emits — fails to separate genuine interaction partners from decoy pairs. That is a serious operational limitation. If you are using AF-3 to screen R-N-A-binding proteins against candidate R-N-A partners, the confidence metric that you would normally use to filter should not be trusted in this domain. The authors do not say confidence is uninformative everywhere; they say specifically that it does not discriminate in R-N-A tasks. Comparable statements about the other four application areas are more nuanced — AF-3 can work well in the right regime, but the confidence-to-accuracy calibration and the training-set-adjacency question have to be checked application by application.

The practical implication for the antibody-engineering audience is direct. Antibody-antigen complex prediction is one of the five application areas the paper evaluates. The abstract does not give the specific number for antibody-antigen accuracy, but the pattern the authors describe — good in favourable settings, dependent on training-set overlap, less broadly generalisable than AF-2 — is exactly consistent with the anecdotal experience many of us have had. AF-3 works well on antibody-antigen complexes that look like the training data, and it struggles on novel epitopes and on antibodies with unusual C-D-R-H-3 lengths or germline usage. The Beltrao paper is making that anecdotal impression rigorous. When it is fully rendered, it is worth reading in full for the specific numbers — the interpretation "AF-3 is powerful for hypothesis generation and prioritization, but its confidence metrics must be used with awareness of training-set overlap" is the right frame to carry into any AF-3-based workflow going forward.

The fourth item is a topology-probing study on ESM-3. It's titled "Advancing Knotted Protein Design with E-S-M-3: Guided Generation and Topological Insights," posted to bioRxiv on July fourteenth. First author position and corresponding author information isn't cleanly parseable from the current bioRxiv landing, but this is a preprint from a computational-biology group using ESM-3-S-M, the smallest open-source variant at one-point-four billion parameters. The paper is a careful study of how multimodal protein language models handle topological complexity, using knotted proteins as the test case.

The setup is worth explaining because it is the reason this paper is useful even for people who never plan to design a knotted protein. Knotted proteins are proteins whose polypeptide backbone forms a genuine topological knot — a configuration that cannot be undone by pulling on the ends without breaking a covalent bond. They are rare, occurring in under one percent of known structures, and they are difficult to design because the sequence has to encode not just a fold, but a folding pathway that threads the chain through a loop. Prior work has shown that diffusion-based backbone generators like R-F-diffusion and E-v-o-Diff, run unguided, produce knotted proteins at about a zero-point-five percent success rate.

The ESM-3 result is that guided generation — using derivative-free guided decoding with a topology-aware scoring function — pushes that success rate to eighty-nine percent, with a ninety-five percent confidence interval of eighty-one to ninety-four percent. That is a dramatic gap, but the authors are careful about the comparison: it is guided ESM-3 versus unguided diffusion, not a like-for-like architectural comparison. The improvement reflects both the generative model and the choice of guided decoding with an explicit topology objective. Even so, hitting knots at eighty-nine percent success is a strong demonstration of what guided generation on a multimodal model can accomplish for a structural feature that unguided generation misses almost entirely.

The scoring function is worth understanding. They use a Python package called Topoly, which computes Alexander polynomials — an algebraic invariant of the knot type — on the protein backbone. Because proteins are open chains rather than closed loops, Topoly uses stochastic closure: it projects the open chain along many random directions to close it into a loop, and averages over projections to get a probabilistic knot-type assignment. The scoring function during guided decoding uses thirty random projections. So the model isn't being told "make a knot" as a hard constraint — it is being scored, at each decoding step, on how strongly its current partial design is on a trajectory to produce a knot when finished, and the sampling favours candidates on that trajectory.

The most striking secondary finding is about how robust the topology is once the model has committed to it. They take each successful knotted design and randomly mutate an increasing fraction of the residues. On average, eighty-four percent of the sequence has to be perturbed before the knot breaks — and the loss follows a sharp threshold, not a gradual degradation. Structural drift accumulates well before the topology fails. That is, the model can shift the coordinates of individual residues substantially while still keeping the chain threaded through the same loop, until eventually the local perturbation is enough to unthread the whole structure. Topology, at least in the E-S-M-3 representation, is more robust than the specific three-dimensional arrangement. That has implications beyond knotted-protein design — it suggests that the representations these models learn separate global topology from local geometry more cleanly than one might have guessed from architecture alone.

Two other results worth noting briefly. The generated knotted proteins have no close sequence similarity to known knotted proteins in AlphaKnot two-point-zero, so this is not a memorization result — the model is generating novel knotted sequences. And they generate a range of knot types, including trefoil knots as the most common but also figure-eight, torus knots, and complex knot types up to nine-crossings-forty-four and nine-crossings-forty-five. Practical implication for the audience: if you are working on structurally unusual antibody scaffolds — mini-immunoglobulin scaffolds, cyclic-permuted VHHs, disulphide-constrained topologies — guided decoding on ESM-3 with a topology-aware scoring function is now a demonstrated tool, and the eighty-percent-plus success rate on knotted proteins argues it should work well on other rare structural constraints too.

Fifth and last item, a shorter one, but conceptually clean. Titled "Asymmetric Structural Transfer Between Natural Language and Biological Foundation Models," posted to bioRxiv on July thirteenth. Authors and affiliations aren't in the rendered abstract snippet, but the framing is unambiguous. Prior work — notably a widely-cited result from a few years ago — showed a curious transfer effect: language models fine-tuned only on English structural tasks, like part-of-speech tagging and syntactic parsing, could subsequently do zero-shot protein-homology discrimination surprisingly well. That was taken as evidence that there is some shared structural regularity between natural-language sequences and biological sequences, and that foundation models learn representations that can transfer both ways.

This paper asks whether that transfer is symmetric. That is: does the reverse direction — biology to language — also work? Do protein language models like E-S-M-2 and Prot-B-E-R-T, if fine-tuned on English structural tasks, do those tasks zero-shot? The answer they arrive at, systematically, is no. Reverse transfer is consistently weak. It does not exceed matched-token controls, it does not scale with model size, and it does not generalize across task classes. In an architecture-matched two-by-two comparison on models with known training data — which lets them eliminate the pretraining-contamination confound that clouds larger-model comparisons — a language model retains far more competence when moved into biology than a biological model retains when moved into language. Specifically, the off-domain performance drop is zero-point-zero-eight for language-to-biology and zero-point-three-six for biology-to-language.

Scaling widens the gap rather than closing it. As models get larger, language-to-biology transfer strengthens; biology-to-language transfer decays toward chance. That pattern replicates across two independent protein-model families — E-S-M-2 and Prot-B-E-R-T — so it is not an artifact of a single architecture. The conclusion the authors draw is that shared structural regularity between natural-language and biological sequences does not imply symmetric representational transfer. There is an intrinsic directionality: whatever the language models learn generalizes downward to biology, but what the biological models learn does not generalize back up to language.

What this means for antibody-language-model builders is worth stating carefully because it is subtle. It does not mean that antibody-specific pre-training is a waste — the T-E-D-l-m result we opened with argues exactly the opposite. What it does mean is that the practice of initializing a specialized biological model from a general-purpose language model — starting from LLaMA or from Galactica and continuing pre-training on antibody sequences — is asymmetrically favoured over the reverse. There is more transferable structure to inherit going that direction than going the other. It also suggests that the appealing intuition that "biological sequences are just another language" is not right in the operationally symmetric sense that some earlier work implied. Sequence models trained on biology learn representations that are specialized enough to biology that they do not carry back into linguistic domain. That is a useful sharpening of the mental model.
