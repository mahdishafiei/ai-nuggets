Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is July thirteenth, and the day is genuinely thin on antibody-specific work — the bioRxiv immunology and bioinformatics queues over the last two days turned up mostly virology, single-cell tooling, and non-antibody assays. Two AI-for-protein preprints did land in the window and they are both worth talking through, so we have two items today: an inverse computational-design pipeline that repurposes R-F-diffusion and Protein-M-P-N-N as a proteome-search tool for protein-protein interactions, and a multimodal protein-language-model architecture that generates free-text function annotations from sequence and structure jointly.

The first item is a bioRxiv preprint titled "The Y-T-H-D-C-1 glutamate-rich domain docks to the A-D-A-R-1 Z-alpha domain, linking the N-six-methyladenosine modification of pre-messenger-R-N-As to double-stranded-R-N-A editing." First author Dmitry Gromak, corresponding author Maria Poptsova, at H-S-E University in Moscow, with contributions from Alan Herbert at InsideOutBio in Charlestown, Massachusetts. Posted to bioRxiv on July twelfth.

The biological question here is not the reason this paper matters for the audience of this show. The finding — that A-D-A-R-1, the interferon-inducible double-stranded R-N-A editing enzyme, physically interacts with the m-six-A reader Y-T-H-D-C-1 — is interesting for R-N-A biologists, but the reason to spend time on this preprint is the methodology. The authors describe what they call an "inverse computational design" strategy for mapping protein-protein interactions at a structurally defined surface, and it repurposes the standard de novo binder-design stack — R-F-diffusion for backbone generation, Protein-M-P-N-N for sequence design, ColabFold and AlphaFold3 for validation — as a proteome-search tool. It is a genuinely different use of tools that the audience of this show uses routinely, and it is worth understanding.

The setup. A-D-A-R-1's Z-alpha domain is a small winged-helix motif that canonically recognises left-handed Z-form nucleic acids by contacting the phosphate backbone through a conserved set of basic residues — lysines 169, 170, 181, 182, arginine 174, and tyrosine 177. The group hypothesised that any human protein whose surface geometry and charge distribution mimicked the phosphate backbone of Z-R-N-A should be able to dock into the same basic pocket. Rather than screening a candidate list, they inverted the problem: first design the "perfect" synthetic Z-alpha binder computationally, then search the human proteome for natural sequences that happen to match those synthetic templates.

Concretely, the pipeline is a funnel. R-F-diffusion generated one thousand two hundred backbones across four batches — a mix of unconditional generation, hotspot-conditioned generation targeting those basic residues, backbones biased with a custom potential toward matching the C-two-prime atoms of a reference double-stranded Z-D-N-A structure, and a hybrid combining hotspots and the geometric potential. Protein-M-P-N-N then assigned eight sequences per backbone, yielding roughly ten thousand designs. ColabFold folded each designed sequence together with the Z-alpha domain, and the group applied filters on p-L-D-D-T greater than eighty, interface predicted-aligned-error less than ten angstroms, and — critically — interface backbone R-M-S-D relative to the designed interface. They tried a two-angstrom R-M-S-D cutoff first, which was too strict and gave them only five hits; relaxing to three angstroms gave them eleven high-quality template binders that actually reproduced the intended interface geometry.

That's the first non-obvious methodological detail worth noting for anyone running similar pipelines. The design step and the folding step do not agree by default — most designs pass p-L-D-D-T and interface p-A-E but drift measurably from the coordinates they were designed against. Filtering on interface R-M-S-D against the designed coordinates is what separates real hits from confident-but-wrong hits, and where you set that threshold determines how much of your library survives.

With eleven template binders in hand, the second stage runs those eleven sequences as BLAST-p queries against SWISS-PROT with an e-value cutoff of ten-to-the-minus-five, and gets back about twelve hundred candidate regions across two hundred and ninety-eight human proteins. Each region is co-folded with Z-alpha in a high-throughput ColabFold screen, and scored on four metrics — p-DockQ, p-DockQ-2, i-p-T-M, and i-p-S-A-E — with lower thresholds on all four required to pass. That narrows the field from twelve hundred to seventy-nine candidate proteins, which then go through full-length AlphaFold3 modelling with the complete A-D-A-R-1 p-one-fifty isoform.

Here is the second non-obvious methodological point. In the full-length models, the global confidence scores — i-p-T-M and i-p-S-A-E — turned out to be almost useless. Both A-D-A-R-1 and its candidate partners have extensive intrinsically disordered regions, and the high predicted-aligned-error in those flexible tails washes out the signal from what may be a perfectly well-predicted local interface. Global scores penalised good models. What actually worked was p-DockQ — an interface-local score that looks at contact density at the predicted interface rather than global chain alignment — combined with a hard filter requiring at least ten C-alpha to C-alpha contact pairs within eight angstroms. The authors explicitly argue this: for computational interactome studies involving disordered proteins, interface-local scoring must be prioritised over global confidence, and contact-pair filtering is a useful secondary check against random chain proximity. That is a specific, transferable methodological recommendation, and it maps directly onto the analogous problem in antibody-antigen complex prediction, where flexible C-D-R-H-3 loops and Fab hinges frequently sink global scores on models that are locally correct.

Sorting the seventy-nine candidates by p-DockQ gave twenty-three high-confidence hits with at least ten interface contacts, and Y-T-H-D-C-1 came out on top. The predicted mode of interaction is the actual take-home: a glutamate-rich, "poly-E" disordered region of Y-T-H-D-C-1, residues one-ninety-nine to two-fifty-four, docks directly into the basic recognition pocket of Z-alpha through charge complementarity, mimicking the negatively charged phosphate backbone of Z-R-N-A. Same basic residues — lysines 169, 170, arginine 174 — engaging an acidic peptide segment instead of nucleic acid. The concept of a protein acting as a nucleic-acid mimic is not new — the group notes a prior biochemical study showing the C-terminal aspartate-glutamate-rich domain of M-B-D-three binds Z-alpha with nanomolar affinity in vitro — but here they identified it prospectively by inverse design rather than by biochemical fishing.

The A-I-model prediction was then stress-tested by microsecond molecular-dynamics simulation using Amber with the ff-nineteen-S-B force field. Over one microsecond of unrestrained dynamics, the binary A-D-A-R-1-Y-T-H-D-C-1 complex equilibrated by two hundred nanoseconds and held a global backbone R-M-S-D around eight angstroms — reasonable for a complex with heavy disorder — while the Z-alpha to poly-E interface itself stayed rigid, R-M-S-D below three angstroms for the entire trajectory. That is the signature of a real interface: the interface variance stays well below the global fluctuation, meaning the interaction is not being propped up by the surrounding scaffold. A ternary complex simulation adding a double-stranded R-N-A scaffold also stayed stable, with all four Z-alpha-to-poly-E contacts across the dimeric assembly holding below two-and-a-half angstroms R-M-S-D, and interestingly the deaminase domain of A-D-A-R-1 did not adopt an editing-competent geometry — suggesting the ternary complex may be a catalytically dormant, R-N-A-anchored state rather than an active editing complex.

Two things to flag about this work. First, none of this is wet-lab-validated yet. The authors are explicit that co-immunoprecipitation, surface plasmon resonance, and mutagenesis of the key charged residues are all still to do. So this is a computational hypothesis with strong internal consistency but no biochemical confirmation. Second — and this is the reason it matters for antibody-design listeners — the pipeline is exportable. You can substitute any target surface for the Z-alpha domain, and the same funnel gives you a shortlist of natural human sequences that structurally mimic a designed binder for that surface. That is directly analogous to using de novo antibody design as a proxy for antigen-mimic discovery, or for finding endogenous protein interactors of a therapeutic scaffold's paratope. The transferable pieces — interface-local scoring, interface-R-M-S-D filtering at the design-to-folding transition, contact-count filtering as a secondary check on disordered-region false positives — are worth internalising even if you never touch A-D-A-R-1 or Z-D-N-A. All the designed binder sequences, ColabFold and AlphaFold3 structures, and analysis scripts are deposited at Zenodo.

The second item is another bioRxiv preprint from July twelfth, titled "Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T: Protein Function Prediction via B-L-I-P-2 with Sequence, Structure, and Text." First author Zhuoyang Chen, corresponding author Qiong Luo, at the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology in Guangzhou.

The starting problem here is that computational protein function prediction is dominated by gene-ontology multi-label classification — you concatenate embeddings from a protein language model with a prediction head and output a vector of gene-ontology term probabilities. That works, but it has a rigidity problem. You cannot express novel functional hypotheses, you cannot combine function with subcellular-location or homology context in one output, and you cannot leverage the rich free-text annotations that already exist in Swiss-Prot. The alternative — protein-to-text generation using a language model — has been tried, but existing approaches either use sequence-only encoders or align sequence with text without explicitly incorporating three-dimensional structural information. Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T is an attempt to close that gap: sequence, structure, and text jointly, in a single generative pipeline that outputs Swiss-Prot-style captions.

The architecture is essentially the B-L-I-P-2 vision-language framework rewired for proteins. There are three components. First, the protein encoder is Sa-Prot — a protein language model whose vocabulary fuses standard amino-acid tokens with the Foldseek three-D-i structural alphabet, so sequence and structure are integrated at the token level rather than as separate encoders. The Sa-Prot weights stay frozen throughout training. Second, a Q-Former — a small querying transformer with eight learnable query tokens — sits between the protein encoder and the language model as a cross-modal projector. Third, the language model is Galactica-one-point-three-billion, a decoder-only transformer pre-trained on a scientific corpus, connected via a linear projection with parameter-efficient fine-tuning via LoRA. Base weights of the language model are also frozen.

The Q-Former is where the interesting work happens, and the training is done in two stages that map fairly directly from the B-L-I-P-2 paper. Stage one is representation learning — the Q-Former is trained on three joint objectives: protein-text contrastive learning to align cross-modal representations in a shared embedding space, protein-text matching to give a token-level fine-grained alignment signal, and protein captioning to force the queries to extract text-relevant protein features. Different self-attention masks are applied for each objective — a unimodal mask for contrastive, bidirectional for matching, and multimodal-causal for captioning — so the same Q-Former weights learn all three views. Stage two connects the trained Q-Former to Galactica: the eight aligned query embeddings are linearly projected to Galactica's embedding dimension and prepended to the prompt, and the language model is fine-tuned via LoRA to generate Swiss-Prot-style captions covering the FUNCTION, SUBCELLULAR LOCATION, and SIMILARITY fields.

Training data is what B-L-I-P-2 for proteins actually looks like in practice. The authors take the Swiss-Prot version-three dataset used by Prot-T-three and add AlphaFold-Database predicted structures for each entry — sequences are truncated at one thousand and twenty-four residues, structures come from AlphaFold-D-B version four, and only records with sequence, structure, and text all present are kept. The final split is four hundred and ten thousand training proteins, roughly nine thousand validation, and nine thousand test. Training runs on two A-one-hundred G-P-U-s with fifty epochs in stage one and ten epochs in stage two.

Results. On protein-text retrieval — that is, given a protein embedding, retrieve the correct text description from a pool of candidates, and vice versa — the six-hundred-fifty-million-parameter Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T outperforms the sequence-only Prot-T-three baseline at matched parameter scale on both accuracy and recall at rank twenty, in both retrieval directions. The average across all eight metrics goes from eighty-nine-point-nine-two for Prot-T-three six-fifty-M to ninety-point-eight-three for Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T. A larger sequence-only baseline built on ProstT-five at one-and-a-half billion parameters lands in between at ninety-point-zero-three, so parameters alone are not the story. On the caption-generation task itself, Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T at six-fifty-M gets the best BLEU, ROUGE, and METEOR among all models compared, and second-best on exact match. Ablations remove either the protein-text-matching or the protein-captioning objective from stage one and show that removing the captioning objective causes the largest performance drop — the captioning loss is what makes the queries extract text-relevant features in the first place.

The most interesting result is the field-stratified analysis. The gain from adding explicit structural information — the delta between Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T and the sequence-only Prot-T-three at matched scale — is not uniform across annotation types. Structure helps the most on the FUNCTION field, where knowing the fold and active-site geometry actually changes what you can say about what the protein does. Structure helps less on SUBCELLULAR LOCATION, and marginally on SIMILARITY — because homology-based similarity is fundamentally a sequence-level statement, so adding structural tokens does not add much signal. That is a useful, task-dependent breakdown of when three-D input matters and when it does not, and it lines up with intuition: fold determines function locally, but sequence determines what the protein looks like to a homology search.

Two limitations to name. Because of compute constraints, ablation studies were run only at the thirty-five-million-parameter scale, so we do not have a full picture of how ablation choices scale to the six-fifty-M model. And the comparison with two closely related methods — Prot-ChatGPT and Prot-two-Chat — could not be done head-to-head because Prot-ChatGPT does not have code available and Prot-two-Chat could not be retrained on this dataset within compute budget. So the "best in class" claim is bounded by what could actually be re-run. That is a normal state of affairs in this literature and worth noting when reading the results table.

For the antibody-engineering audience, the transferable pattern is worth thinking about. Antibody function is not straightforwardly captioned — most antibody records in databases describe a target and an isotype rather than a free-text function — but the B-L-I-P-2 recipe of using Sa-Prot as a structure-aware encoder, a Q-Former as the cross-modal bridge, and multi-objective alignment before generation is a general-purpose way to plug a frozen protein language model into a frozen text language model without retraining either one. The audience of this show has been building antibody-specific language models for years — Ant-i-B-E-R-T-y, Ab-Lang, I-g-L-M, p-I-g-Gen, and their descendants — and this kind of B-L-I-P-2-style projector architecture is exactly the kind of glue you would use to connect one of those frozen antibody language models to a general-purpose scientific reasoning model, without having to retrain either half. The specific take-home is that the captioning objective in stage one is what earns the alignment; retrieval-only or contrastive-only pretraining loses the language-model conditioning signal.

Both papers today are computational-only. Both would benefit from wet-lab validation — Y-T-H-D-C-1 to Z-alpha binding by co-I-P or S-P-R for the first paper, and downstream evaluation of Prot-B-L-I-P-2-S-S-T's generated captions on out-of-distribution proteins by human expert annotation for the second. Take the results as strong internal computational hypotheses. That is where the field is at this week.
