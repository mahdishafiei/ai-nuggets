## Script

Good morning. This is Affinity Matured for Friday, July seventeenth, twenty twenty-six. Three items today, all landing on the same broad question from very different angles — what do sequence models actually capture about the biology they claim to model, and where does that capture translate into a usable therapeutic. A benchmark of generative models for immune receptor repertoires from a Greiff, Peters, and Sandve collaboration. A protein language model driven affinity maturation of an adeno-associated virus purification ligand, going from a de novo designed starting point to a single-digit micromolar peptide over three rounds of docking. And a unified therapeutic peptide developability platform that stitches together an antibody-scale protein language model and a peptide-chemistry model into one property-prediction interface. Let's get into it.

The first item is a bioRxiv benchmark posted yesterday, titled "What Do Generative Models Learn About Adaptive Immune Receptor Repertoires? A Benchmark Study." First author Charlotte Würtzen, corresponding authors Victor Greiff at Oslo, Bjoern Peters at La Jolla Institute for Immunology, and Geir Kjetil Sandve at Oslo. Just a note up front — this preprint went up yesterday and bioRxiv has not rendered the full body HTML yet, so what follows is grounded in the abstract and the design of the study as the authors describe it, not the tables. If any of the specific model rankings change how you'd act on this, wait for the preprint body or the code release before treating it as ground truth.

The motivation is one that anyone who has trained an antibody or T-cell receptor language model has felt. Generative models of adaptive immune receptor repertoires — the antibody heavy chain, T-cell alpha and beta chain repertoires that come out of bulk sequencing — are increasingly used to model sequence distributions, to sample plausible new receptors, and downstream to design therapeutic antibodies or T-cell receptors. But the standard machine learning evaluations — held-out likelihood, held-out perplexity, sample quality by nearest-neighbor distance — don't cleanly translate to the immune repertoire regime. There is no biological ground truth for what an "in-distribution" receptor even is. The repertoire is shaped by a two-stage process — V-D-J recombination and thymic or peripheral selection — and both stages leave signatures the model could either capture or miss without the loss function telling you which. So authors of new AIRR generative models have been reporting benchmark numbers on quantities that may or may not correspond to what a clinical or discovery user actually cares about.

The paper puts a suite of AIRR-tailored evaluation metrics against five families of generative models. Variational autoencoders, long short-term memory recurrent networks, antibody language models trained on repertoire data, explicit selection models — the kind that model generation and thymic selection as separate stages, in the tradition of the earlier Elhanati and Mora work — and simple statistical baselines. The task they picked is a clinically pointed one — learning individual-specific immune receptor repertoires, not the pan-population distribution. That framing matters because personalized immunotherapy, individual disease monitoring, and vaccine response studies are all about whether the model captures the receptors this donor's B or T cells will generate, not the receptors the average donor's cells will generate.

The angles they say they probe, from the abstract, are memorization risk — how much of a generated repertoire is just training-set copies with cosmetic edits — innovation capability — how much of it is genuinely novel receptor space consistent with the underlying biology — and sensitivity to hyperparameter choice, which is where a lot of practical model selection breaks down. The specific rankings will have to wait, but the design is worth flagging on its own because until a paper like this exists, the AIRR modeling literature has been essentially each group reporting its own home-cooked evaluation and the field has been picking winners on vibes. A shared benchmark with tailored metrics — coming from Greiff's group in particular, which has been consistent for years now that generic ML evaluation misreads immune data — is a piece of scaffolding the field has been overdue for. If you are building antibody or TCR language models, this is worth checking in a week when the body renders and the code drops.

Second item, published yesterday in the Journal of Chromatography A. "Leveraging protein language model for maturation of high-affinity peptide ligand in the purification of an adeno-associated virus vector." This is a nice concrete example of what protein language model driven affinity maturation looks like on a target that most gene-therapy production groups care about. The problem is downstream affinity purification of adeno-associated virus, which is the workhorse gene therapy vector. The current gold standard resins use immobilized antibodies against the capsid, and those resins require harsh elution — low pH — which stresses the capsid and reduces recovery. There has been a push for peptide ligands that give you comparable selectivity at mild elution. This paper is one such effort, with a twist — the maturation stage runs through E-S-M-3, the multimodal protein language model from EvolutionaryScale, rather than through directed evolution or purely physics-based docking.

The workflow. Step one, they targeted the receptor-binding region on the A-A-V-2 capsid — the surface region where A-A-V-2 makes contact with its heparan sulfate proteoglycan receptor — and applied a de novo peptide design method to nominate three candidate ligands. Step two, they took those three and ran them through E-S-M-3 driven maturation, letting the model propose sequence variants for docking evaluation. Step three, they iterated for three rounds of docking simulations, at each round retaining variants that improved predicted binding. The output was a peptide called A4 with an isolated dissociation constant of three point eight micromolar for A-A-V-2. Molecular dynamics on the A4-capsid complex indicated the binding is dominated by electrostatic interactions, which is consistent with the polyanionic character of the receptor-binding footprint.

Then they made a resin. A4 was coupled onto Sepharose four Fast Flow — the classic base matrix — to give Sep-four-F-F-A4 gels. Those gels adsorbed A-A-V-2 effectively across pH six to eight, which is the desired mild-binding regime, and — critically — showed serotype selectivity for A-A-V-2 over other serotypes tested. Elution can be run under mild conditions rather than the harsh low-pH elution required for antibody-based resins.

Why this matters. The individual result — a three point eight micromolar peptide — is not, on its own, spectacular. Existing peptide ligands for A-A-V, including the earlier Menegatti group ligands from twenty twenty-three, sit in a similar affinity range with lower reported nanomolar-equivalent dynamic binding capacities. The methodological contribution is that a protein language model can drive maturation of a de novo peptide ligand, against a non-antibody protein target, to usable purification performance in three rounds without a directed evolution wet-lab campaign. That is the kind of loop antibody engineers should be paying attention to even when the target is not an antibody — because it is the same loop we use for CDR maturation. Read the multimodal model's likelihood or score as a proxy for affinity, propose variants that shift the score in the right direction, dock and rank, keep the winners. The main question the paper opens — and one the authors do not fully answer from the abstract-level details — is how sensitive the outcome is to the choice of scoring function on top of E-S-M-3 versus the initial three-candidate seed set. A companion paper that ran the same loop with A-b-M-P-N-N or with ProteinMPNN as the mutation-proposal step would tell us a lot about whether the win comes from E-S-M-3 specifically or from the closed-loop design in general.

Third item, published yesterday in Nature Communications. "PeptiVerse: A unified platform for therapeutic peptide property prediction," from Pranam Chatterjee's group at Duke. This has been a bioRxiv preprint since late December — the news yesterday is the Nature Communications version dropping. Therapeutic peptides are, in the authors' framing, a modality sitting between small molecules and antibodies — modestly better than small molecules on target flexibility and specificity, modestly better than antibodies on immunogenicity and manufacturing cost, but with a developability profile that is bespoke for each candidate. Hemolysis, solubility, non-fouling behavior, membrane permeability, toxicity, plasma half-life, target binding affinity — each has its own tooling and each of those tools speaks a different input language. Some accept canonical amino acid sequences, some accept S-M-I-L-E-S strings for chemically modified peptides, and once you're doing cyclic peptides or N-methylation you need both.

PeptiVerse is one place that speaks both languages. Two foundation models under the hood. For the amino acid input, they use E-S-M-2 at the six-hundred-fifty million parameter scale, the standard protein language model of record. For the S-M-I-L-E-S input covering chemically modified peptides, they use PeptideCLM, a twenty-three million parameter chemistry-aware model whose tokenizer is designed for noncanonical peptide chemistry — non-natural amino acids, backbone modifications, macrocycles. The architecture uses those frozen embeddings and layers task-specific lightweight heads on top — X-G-Boost, elastic net, S-V-M, M-L-P, C-N-N, or transformer, whichever wins on that property. For binding affinity, they use a cross-attention transformer to build a joint peptide-and-protein latent representation, which is the standard trick for binding tasks.

The property coverage is broader than any single existing tool. Hemolysis, non-fouling, and binding affinity are supported for both amino acid and S-M-I-L-E-S inputs. Solubility and permeability-penetrance are amino-acid-only, since the training datasets are only labeled at that level. Toxicity and the P-A-M-P-A and Caco-2 permeability assays are S-M-I-L-E-S only, again limited by data. Half-life supports both.

On benchmarks against the recent PepLand baseline, PeptiVerse comes out ahead on most tasks under similarity-aware splits. Binding affinity from amino acid input, Spearman correlation zero point five five seven versus PepLand's zero point five zero three. S-M-I-L-E-S permeability, zero point six seven one versus zero point six two eight. Solubility F1, zero point seven five four versus zero point six six two. There is one loss they call out honestly — S-M-I-L-E-S binding affinity, where PepLand reports zero point seven six eight to their zero point five eight two, but PepLand's number is under random splits, which lets train-test similarity leak into the evaluation, and PeptiVerse's number is under similarity-aware splits, which is the right protocol for developability. That's a case where the honest-splits number is the meaningful one even if the headline number looks worse.

Two caveats. First, there is no wet-lab validation in the paper — the figure gestures at prospective downstream validation as a capability, but no experimental verification of any of the platform's predictions is reported. Second, this is a property-prediction platform, not a generative design tool — you bring peptides and PeptiVerse tells you what it thinks their properties are. What it is useful for is closing the loop on a generative design workflow that produces candidates upstream. Weights are on Hugging Face at ChatterjeeLab, a hosted web interface is available at Hugging Face Spaces, and the plotting code is on GitHub. For anyone running a chemically modified peptide or macrocycle campaign — the same infrastructure needed for cyclic peptide antibody-alternative work in T-cell engagers and similar formats — this is a reasonable default developability filter to run new sequences through.

Stepping back — three items today, one benchmark, one applied maturation loop, one property-prediction platform. The common thread is that the field is moving past the standalone protein language model as an artifact you can just quote a perplexity for, into an era where the sensible questions are about what the model captures under a domain-appropriate metric — the AIRR benchmark — how much of a wet-lab workflow you can compress by putting the model in an iterative loop — the A-A-V ligand paper — and how you route between multiple foundation models to cover the full developability surface — PeptiVerse. All three are worth an hour of your time if the corresponding modality is on your bench.
