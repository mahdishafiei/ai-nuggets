## Script

Good evening. One more edition of Affinity Matured for July 30th — two items, both published yesterday in Nature and Nature Biotechnology. First, a method for guiding any pre-trained protein generative model with experimental property data at inference time, without retraining; second, a framework for miniaturizing and modifying proteins using length-independent protein language model encodings.

First item. Nature Biotechnology, published July 29th, from the Listgarten lab at U-C Berkeley — Junhao Xiong, Ishan Gaur, and colleagues including David Savage. The method is called ProteinGuide. The problem it solves is specific: there is currently no principled way to condition a pre-trained protein sequence generative model on auxiliary property information — wet-lab assay data, a stability oracle, a binding predictor — without retraining from scratch. ProteinGuide solves this with a Bayesian formulation: at each step of the generation process, it samples from the product of the property predictor and the base model, executing Bayes' Rule in sequence space. The unifying technical insight is that masked language models like E-S-M-3, any-order autoregressive models like Protein-M-P-N-N, and discrete diffusion or flow-matching models like Multi-Flow all share mathematically equivalent training objectives and sampling distributions when trained with masking noise — so the same guidance algorithm transfers across all of them without model-specific adaptation. Two implementations are provided: a fast first-order Taylor-approximation method and a slower exact formulation for cases where the approximation is numerically unstable.

The key wet-lab demonstration uses an adenine base editor. Starting from a single pooled library of 2,000 variants used as property input, one round of ProteinGuide guidance produces variants with higher editing efficiency than was previously achieved using seven sequential rounds of directed evolution. The variants reach less than 80 percent sequence identity to wild type, indicating real exploration of novel sequence space rather than incremental refinement of the starting point. In multi-property Pareto optimization — simultaneously improving binding to a desired target while reducing off-target zinc coordination — ProteinGuide achieved a 75 percent success rate. For this audience, the immediate application is any situation where you have prior-round experimental data from affinity maturation, CDR screening, or thermostability assays: ProteinGuide is a way to inject those signals into Protein-M-P-N-N or E-S-M-3 at inference time rather than paying the cost of full fine-tuning, and because the framework is model-agnostic, it does not lock you to a specific generative architecture.

Second item. Nature, also July 29th, from Rohit Singh and Scott Soderling at Duke University — Kapil Devkota, Daichi Shonai, and colleagues. The method is called Raygun, and it tackles a problem that has been difficult for protein design since the beginning: generating proteins of a different length from a template while preserving function. Standard protein generative models operate over sequences, but comparing or interpolating between proteins of different lengths in a shared representational space is geometrically awkward. Raygun's conceptual move is to encode each protein not as a sequence of residues in high-dimensional space but as a probability distribution in a fixed-dimensional space. Specifically, it runs the protein through a protein language model to get per-residue embeddings and then summarizes those as a single distribution. Because the encoding is length-independent, proteins of any size become directly commensurable, and length changes become moves in the same space as sequence changes. The generation process is controlled by just two parameters: one governing the number of substitutions and one governing length changes, making the design decisions explicit and independently adjustable.

In cell-based validation: Raygun miniaturized fluorescent proteins to produce two variants that are shorter than 96 percent of all fluorescent proteins cataloged in the F-P-base database; it miniaturized TurboID, the synthetic biotin ligase widely used for proximity proteomics; and when applied in the expansion direction, it generated Epidermal Growth Factor variants — E-G-F variants — with higher binding affinity to the E-G-F receptor than wild type. Miniaturization ranged from 10 to 25 percent across these targets, with some constructs reaching over 50 percent reduction. Sequence diversity was introduced while functional sites were preserved. The relevance for this audience is direct: VHH nanobodies and single-domain antibody fragments are already among the smaller protein binders in use, but there are gene therapy contexts and bispecific formats where further reduction of the variable domain payload matters. Beyond size reduction, the CDR loop diversity angle — using Raygun to generate loops of systematically varied lengths while respecting the surrounding scaffold — is a use case the abstract explicitly supports and that is underexplored in CDR engineering pipelines.

Two for tonight — ProteinGuide giving a principled, model-agnostic way to condition E-S-M-3 or Protein-M-P-N-N on experimental data at inference time without retraining; and Raygun providing length-independent protein modification for miniaturization, expansion, and sequence diversification. Both code repositories are open-source. Back tomorrow.
