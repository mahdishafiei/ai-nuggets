**First: predicting antibody self-association with a sequence-structure fusion model — a fine-tuned E-S-M-2 plus AlphaFold residue graph trained against the clone self-interaction B-L-I developability assay.**

The failure modes of therapeutic monoclonal antibodies are not primarily about missing the target. They are about the biophysics of concentrated formulations — high viscosity, aggregation, unfavorable serum half-life driven by non-specific clearance — and about the fact that these liabilities usually only become visible late in development when material is expensive and program timelines are tight. The developability-prediction problem is: given a candidate antibody sequence, can we anticipate those liabilities early enough that lead selection avoids them.

The specific assay this paper is built around is called Clone Self-Interaction Biolayer Interferometry — C-S-I-B-L-I. It measures weak, reversible antibody self-association at low material cost in plate format. The value of C-S-I-B-L-I as a developability screen is calibrated against two things: viscosity at high concentration, and clearance in humanized mouse F-c-R-n transgenic Tg32 mice. On a panel of two-hundred-and-forty-six monoclonal antibodies, C-S-I-B-L-I moderately correlates with high-concentration viscosity. On the forty-one antibodies with matched mouse P-K data, C-S-I-B-L-I strongly associates with clearance. So it is a defensible experimental proxy for two of the biophysical failure modes that matter most for therapeutic antibodies. The question the paper takes on is whether you can predict a binary C-S-I-B-L-I outcome — high versus low self-interaction — from sequence and predicted structure alone, before the clone gets made.

The model architecture is an end-to-end sequence-structure fusion. A fine-tuned protein language model — specifically, E-S-M-2 fine-tuned on antibody data — is combined with residue-aligned three-dimensional context from AlphaFold-predicted structures encoded as a residue graph. The novel piece is what they call disentangled multi-stream attention: three parallel attention tracks over sequence content, over chain-aware positional information — separating heavy from light chain and framework from C-D-R — and over the graph-derived structural signal. The point of separating the streams is to capture spatially proximate residue-residue interactions that are distant in the linear sequence, which is exactly the biophysical setting where self-association happens — a hydrophobic C-D-R patch and a C-D-R-vicinity charge patch on opposite sides of the F-v that come into contact through solution geometry, not sequence proximity.

Training and evaluation is done with edit-distance-controlled splits across fourteen-hundred-and-ninety-nine I-g-Gs and eight-hundred-and-forty-one V-H-Hs to test generalization. On held-out data, the structure-aware model achieves the best performance: F-1 of zero-point-seven-six on V-H-Hs and F-1 of zero-point-five-seven on I-g-Gs. A sequence-only variant of the disentangled multi-stream architecture beats a standard protein language model baseline that has no structural input at all, which is the paper's evidence that the disentangled attention is doing work independent of the graph. A complementary interpretable model built from AlphaFold-structure-derived and sequence-derived physicochemical descriptors with cluster-aware feature selection reaches F-1 of zero-point-seven-two on V-H-Hs and zero-point-five-seven on I-g-Gs — nearly matching the neural model on V-H-Hs and matching it on I-g-Gs. Shapley value analysis on the interpretable model highlights charge and dipole descriptors, hydrophobicity, and aggregation propensity across both the C-D-Rs and the framework regions as the dominant contributors.

Two things are worth flagging here for the audience. First, on the biology: the fact that a purely biophysical-descriptor model matches the neural model on I-g-Gs and gets close on V-H-Hs is a real result. It says that for early developability triage, you may not need a deep model — a well-chosen set of physicochemical descriptors computed on the AlphaFold structure captures most of the predictable signal. That is a useful calibration for teams deciding how much modeling infrastructure to stand up. Second, the interesting gap is on I-g-Gs. Both the sequence-structure fusion model and the interpretable model reach F-1 around zero-point-five-seven on I-g-Gs, well below their V-H-H performance. A likely explanation is that I-g-G self-association is driven by heavy-light chain interface effects and by paired-domain geometry that a single-chain V-H-H does not have to worry about, and that current models capture the single-chain V-H-H story better than the paired-chain I-g-G story. For antibody-language-model development, that gap is a specific place to look for gains — better representations of heavy-light pairing and of F-v-quaternary geometry are the plausible route to closing it.

The framework is presented as extensible to other developability endpoints. Given that C-S-I-B-L-I is one of a family of similar early-screen assays, the same architecture pattern should transfer.

Paper link: https://doi.org/10.1080/19420862.2026.2694124

**Second: characterizing AlphaFold 3's ability to predict T-cell antigen specificity — from Charlotte Deane and Hashem Koohy's groups at Oxford, with Jamie Rossjohn at Monash.**

The IMMREP25 benchmark earlier this year showed that AlphaFold-based methods are the current best answer to the unseen-epitope challenge in T-cell receptor antigen specificity prediction — the challenge of ranking whether a T-cell receptor will bind an epitope it was not exposed to during training. Sequence-based T-cell receptor specificity models have been shown repeatedly to overfit their training peptides. Almost every model in IMMREP25 that beat random used AlphaFold 3 or an AlphaFold-3-derivative. But no one had systematically characterized what AlphaFold 3 is actually doing when it succeeds at this task, and where it fails. This preprint answers that question in a comprehensive series of experiments on structures the authors predicted themselves at scale.

The first contribution is engineering. The default AlphaFold 3 pipeline takes about an hour per T-cell receptor plus peptide plus M-H-C complex, with about fifty-one minutes of that going to M-S-A generation from the standard sequence databases. This is intractable for repertoire-scale analysis. The authors built an accelerated pipeline on top of the T-C-R-model-two sequence databases that reduces M-S-A generation to twenty-nine seconds — roughly a hundred-fold speedup — with storage requirements dropping from three hundred gigabytes to fifty megabytes, and with no measurable loss of specificity-prediction or structure-prediction quality. This alone makes T-cell receptor-p-M-H-C benchmarking practically feasible in a way it wasn't before.

Using that pipeline, they benchmark four structure prediction models — AlphaFold 2, AlphaFold 3, Boltz-2, and Chai-1 — on a set of fifteen hundred T-cell receptor plus peptide plus M-H-C complexes, seven hundred and fifty binders and seven hundred and fifty non-binders generated by an epitope-shuffling strategy. AlphaFold 3 and Chai-1 achieve comparable specificity prediction — both meaningfully outperform AlphaFold 2. Boltz-2, despite being based on the AlphaFold 3 architecture, only matches AlphaFold 2, not AlphaFold 3. On the twenty-one-structure hold-out benchmark for structure quality, AlphaFold 3 has the highest fraction of predictions in the C-A-P-R-I "high" quality bracket by Dock-Q score. Ablation studies show that both the paired and unpaired multiple sequence alignments matter for T-cell receptor specificity prediction — templates alone are not enough — and that the unpaired M-S-A is particularly critical.

Then comes the diagnostic that gives the paper its most useful finding. The authors compute twelve geometric and biochemical structural features from crystallographic T-cell receptor plus peptide plus M-H-C complexes — pitch angle, tilt angle, roll angle, scanning angle, X, Y, and Z distances of the T-cell receptor over the p-M-H-C, buried surface area, shape complementarity, number of interactions, number of interaction types, and residue-residue contact maps — and correlate each of these features against the AlphaFold-derived C-D-R-to-p-M-H-C P-A-E confidence score. In a bootstrapped analysis, four features come out as robust predictors of AlphaFold 3 confidence: the contact map, the number of interactions, and the X and Y distances of the T-cell receptor over the p-M-H-C. Eight features do not, including buried surface area, shape complementarity, pitch angle, tilt angle, Z distance, and the count of individual interaction types — hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic contacts, salt bridges, pi-stacking.

The takeaway is important: AlphaFold 3's predictive power for T-cell receptor antigen specificity is dominated by the geometry of where the T-cell receptor sits over the p-M-H-C, not by the biochemical interactions between them. The model is not learning the chemical determinants of T-cell receptor specificity; it is learning the geometric envelope. That is the source of the ceiling behavior the authors report — AlphaFold 3's specificity R-O-C-A-U-C peaks around zero-point-six, useful but well below the roughly zero-point-nine that would let it substitute for experimental assays.

They also introduce a small learned model on top of the AlphaFold P-A-E outputs called the P-A-E Aggregator, which learns a residue-position-specific weighting of the C-D-R-to-p-M-H-C P-A-E entries. The P-A-E Aggregator does two things. It makes the score distributions more normally distributed, and it significantly reduces a confounder: the intrinsic higher confidence AlphaFold 3 has for T-cell receptors and p-M-H-Cs whose crystal structures were in its training set. The specificity performance itself doesn't move much — a two-point R-O-C-A-U-C shift — but the score becomes more comparable across complexes.

Two additional applications show what AlphaFold 3 does well. On a repertoire of ninety-two T-cell receptors that bind an Epstein-Barr-Virus B-M-L-F epitope, previously shown by Dash and colleagues to cluster into distinct sequence groups suspected to represent distinct binding modes, AlphaFold 3 clusters the predicted structures in a way that aligns with the sequence clusters. That is direct structural evidence for the alternative-binding-modes hypothesis. On a mutational scanning experiment of a T-cell receptor called 572G binding celiac disease antigens, the P-A-E Aggregator score detects the disruptive mutations at the central tryptophan-aspartate motif of the C-D-R-3-beta at R-O-C-A-U-C zero-point-nine-two for the alpha-one epitope and zero-point-eight-three for the omega-one epitope — comparable to task-specific models trained directly on mutational scanning data.

The limitations the authors call out are candid. AlphaFold 3 has learned that C-D-R-3-beta sequences that don't start with the canonical C-A-S-S motif produce lower-confidence predictions, and it applies this prior incorrectly to functional non-canonical mutants. Selecting the top-ranked seed from an ensemble does not correlate with the highest structural quality on the disruption experiment. Full in-silico affinity optimization campaigns against a target with AlphaFold 3 alone are still out of reach at the current specificity ceiling.

For the audience: this is a paper about what AlphaFold 3 is doing when it appears to succeed at T-cell receptor antigen specificity, and it is honest about the fact that the geometric-envelope story is not the same as the model understanding immune recognition. For anyone building T-cell receptor design or repertoire-decoding tools that rely on AlphaFold-family models, the finding that the P-A-E scores are geometrically dominated is a specific pointer at where to spend representation-learning effort next.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.08.737208

**Third: AbICL — in-context learning for antigen-specific antibody affinity ranking, on the AbRank benchmark.**

Antibody affinity ranking — given a candidate list, ordering them by predicted affinity to a specified antigen — is one of the practical questions that lead-optimization teams need to answer well. Existing methods treat each affinity comparison as an isolated regression or classification problem: predict a value for antibody A against target X, predict a value for antibody B against target X, subtract. That framing ignores the fact that in real lead-optimization campaigns, the model has access to many other labeled comparisons — sibling designs from the same library, prior campaigns against related targets, related antigens with overlapping epitope surfaces. If the model can look at that context alongside the new pair, it can potentially calibrate to the specific antigen at hand.

AbICL, posted this week to arXiv, does exactly that. The architecture couples a pretrained structural encoder for antibody plus antigen inputs with a ranking head, and the whole thing is trained using episodic meta-learning. The training regime samples episodes that resemble the deployment scenario: for each episode, the model sees a support set of labeled antibody-antigen comparisons for a target, then is asked to rank new antibody candidates against the same target using that support set as in-context evidence — no gradient updates at inference time. That is the "in-context learning" framing: the model adapts to the specific antigen through the demonstrations it is shown, not through further training.

Evaluation is on the AbRank benchmark, which is the community's standard for antibody affinity ranking. The paper reports consistent improvements over existing baselines across the standard AbRank splits, with the largest gains in two regimes that matter most for real deployment: distribution shift — antigens whose statistics don't match training — and fine-grained affinity discrimination, meaning ranking candidates whose affinities differ by less than an order of magnitude. Both of those are exactly where lead-optimization campaigns typically live: the target of interest looks unlike training data, and the affinity spread across the top candidates in a library is narrow. If in-context demonstrations really are useful when the model is asked to rank in a regime it wasn't specifically trained on, that would generalize the method to new antigens without retraining. The paper doesn't report wet-lab validation — it is purely in-silico on public benchmarks — but the AbRank framing is standardized enough that the direction is meaningful.

The interesting architectural claim here is not the specific model but the shape of the framing. Meta-learning plus in-context adaptation is a natural fit for the antibody-affinity-ranking use case where each new campaign presents a new antigen with limited labeled binders. Whether the specific structural encoder plus ranking head is the right instantiation is one question. Whether the more general framing — episodic meta-learning against ranking targets — will become the dominant approach for antibody affinity models is a separate question, and the answer is not settled yet. For teams working on antibody affinity ranking, AbICL is worth reading as an entry point into the meta-learning framing, not as a settled architectural recipe.

Paper link: https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.05846

**Fourth: NAZ-mAb structure-guided computational design against p95-HER2 — a candid case study on the limits of fixed-backbone interaction-energy ranking.**

This one is a short but instructive item. Victor Greiff's group at the University of Oslo, with Esmaeil Dorraji and colleagues from the Kyte lab, publishes a computational antibody optimization workflow applied to their own antibody, previously called Oslo-2 and now called NAZ-mAb, against the tumor-specific truncated HER2 isoform 611-C-T-F-p95-HER2. This is a valid therapeutic target — it lacks most of the H-E-R-2 extracellular domain that conventional H-E-R-2 antibodies engage, and it correlates with metastasis and treatment resistance. So the parent antibody has a real reason to exist.

The workflow: start from the antibody sequence alone; predict the antibody-antigen complex computationally; generate a combinatorial mutational landscape using FoldX 5.0; and prioritize candidates by predicted interaction energy and by developability criteria. Two variants representing two distinct design strategies were carried forward for wet-lab validation. Variant one, called NAZ-mAb v1, is an aromatic double mutant — S31W and H107W on the light chain — where the strategy is to maximize interface interactions by inserting large aromatic side chains. Variant two, NAZ-mAb v2, is a conservative single point mutation — S31M on the light chain — the strategy being to sample the immediate mutational neighborhood without moving far from the parent.

Both variants expressed cleanly as recombinant I-g-Gs, which is the first practical criterion. Notably, v2 gave a five-fold higher expression yield than the parent NAZ-mAb, which is a real developability win. Both variants also retained antigen binding — the indirect E-L-I-S-A signal against p95-HER2 was actually higher than the parent's on the tested plates, again a positive readout at the screening level.

But the Biacore two-state kinetic analysis, which is the more rigorous readout, tells a different story. The equilibrium dissociation constant for v1 came in at thirty-two-point-six nanomolar. For v2, at nine-point-four-five nanomolar. And for the parent NAZ-mAb, at five-point-three-three nanomolar. So both computationally optimized variants bind their target less tightly than the parent antibody they started from — the direction the workflow was intended to move.

The paper's own framing of this result is the reason it's worth flagging. The authors do not overclaim. They state directly that these findings show the computational workflow can generate experimentally tractable, antigen-engaging variants, while also highlighting the limitations of fixed-backbone interaction-energy ranking as a predictor of binding affinity and yield. That is the correct level of restraint. FoldX and related fixed-backbone scoring functions were built for point mutations where the backbone barely moves, and where the interface is a static surface. Real interface engineering involves side-chain repacking, small backbone shifts, and the kinds of induced-fit rearrangements that a fixed-backbone score doesn't see. It should not be a surprise that a workflow built entirely on fixed-backbone interaction-energy ranking fails to reliably improve binding affinity — but it is remarkably useful to have a clean, published case study that shows the failure mode with actual K-D numbers on a real target.

For the audience, two takeaways. One, the developability side of the workflow — the five-fold expression yield improvement on v2 with a single conservative mutation — is a real win, and it points to what the current generation of computational antibody-optimization workflows is actually good at: prioritizing mutations that keep the antibody expressible and that don't obviously destabilize it. Two, for binding affinity gains, the field needs methods that go beyond fixed-backbone rescoring. The generative approaches — R-F-diffusion for backbone redesign, ProteinM-P-N-N for sequence assignment on new backbones, and the ligand-conditioned inverse folding methods discussed this morning — are the direction of travel, and the honest case studies like this one from the Greiff group are what tell us why the older methods are being replaced.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.07.736817
