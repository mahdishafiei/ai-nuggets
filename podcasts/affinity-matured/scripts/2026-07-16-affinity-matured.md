## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. Today is Thursday, July sixteenth. Two items today — a lighter edition as the field settles after a busy week. First, an updated version of the Baker lab's generative design pipeline for transmembrane beta-barrel nanopores, which is less about the nanopores themselves than it is about a recipe for adapting R-F-diffusion and Protein-M-P-N-N to structural classes they were not originally built for. Second, a version-three update to a preprint from the Jana Shen lab at the University of Maryland, asking whether protein language models encode not just sequence fitness, but electrostatic properties.

Let's start with the Baker lab work, because it is the more technically substantial of the two and has the clearest transferable lessons.

The paper is titled "Generative design of programmable asymmetric beta-barrel nanopores," and the version-two update was posted to bioRxiv yesterday. Lead author is Afra Philomin; David Baker's name anchors the end of a long author list, with Brian Coventry and Asim Bera also contributing. The version-two update adds crystal structures and expanded functional characterization on top of the original June preprint.

The problem they are attacking is one that has resisted AI protein design until now. Transmembrane beta-barrels — the cylindrical pore-forming proteins used for nanopore sensing, sequencing, and separations — have a fundamentally different structural architecture from the globular alpha-helical and mixed-fold proteins where R-F-diffusion has been most successful. They are made of antiparallel beta-strands that hydrogen-bond to form a cylindrical sheet spanning a lipid bilayer. Designing them requires control not just over fold geometry, but over membrane-specific features like hydrophobic thickness, strand pairing register, and native structural quirks — in particular, glycine twists and beta-bulges, which are the local deformations beta-barrel proteins use to close flat beta-sheets into cylinders without disrupting hydrogen bonding. Prior to this work, even expert-guided energy-based methods could only access a narrow range of barrel geometries, and applying R-F-diffusion2 off the shelf produced barrels with poor closure and essentially no expression in E. coli.

The solution has two parts, and the logic of both is worth understanding because it is the same logic you would apply to any new structural class.

Part one is a distillation dataset. The authors started with a database of all known transmembrane beta-barrel sequences, predicted structures for four hundred and eighty-five thousand of them using AlphaFold2, and filtered strictly on hydrogen-bonding completeness, strand-pairing quality, and barrel closure — working down to one hundred and seventeen thousand high-quality structures. This is orders of magnitude more than the roughly seven hundred experimentally solved transmembrane beta-barrels in the Protein Data Bank, and it is the training data that makes everything else possible. They then fine-tuned R-F-diffusion2 on this distillation set, with explicit conditioning on glycine-twist placements and beta-bulge positions. This model is called T-M-B underscore R-F-D-2. With native structural quirks encoded as inputs, the model generates backbones that spontaneously incorporate the correct frequency of these features even when they are not explicitly specified — meaning the conditioning has been internalized, not just patched on.

Part two is a sequence design model. Protein-M-P-N-N, when applied to transmembrane beta-barrel backbones, generates sequences that overload aliphatic residues in buried positions and underrepresent the amino-acid composition fingerprint of real membrane proteins. None of the sequences it designed expressed in E. coli. The fix: retrain Protein-M-P-N-N on the same distillation set, and expand its nearest-neighbor receptive field from the default thirty-two to ninety-six residues, so that residues on one side of the barrel can sense residues on the opposite side during design. This matters because transmembrane beta-barrels, unlike globular proteins, have a broad hollow core — the local neighborhood alone is not enough context for sequence decisions. This model is called T-M-B underscore M-P-N-N. With T-M-B underscore M-P-N-N generating sequences on T-M-B underscore R-F-D-2 backbones, E. coli expression success rates rose to sixty to eighty percent across all four barrel geometries tested — ten, twelve, fourteen, and sixteen strands. That is the kind of expression rate that makes a design campaign practically executable.

The experimental validation covers forty-eight designs in total. Single-molecule electrophysiology shows step-wise current insertions into planar lipid bilayers with stable, low-noise open-state conductances consistent with pore diameters between zero-point-seven and one-point-five nanometers. Several designs show asymmetric current distributions at positive versus negative voltage, evidence of directional transport within the pore lumen. Crystal structures of two designs — a ten-stranded parallel barrel and a ten-stranded asymmetric barrel with specified glycine twists — match the designed models at Cα root-mean-square deviations of one-point-one-five and zero-point-seven-two-six angstroms respectively. At that level, the glycine-twist residues in the designed and solved structures overlap essentially perfectly. This is the kind of number that means the model is predicting atomic coordinates, not just generating sequences that fold into something vaguely barrel-shaped.

On function: copper-coordinating residues designed into the pore lumen produce selective ion sensing for copper over cobalt, nickel, and zinc — overriding the Irving-Williams series, which normally dictates selectivity for divalent metals, in favor of the designed binding geometry. Larger fourteen-stranded designs support D-N-A translocation. Longer barrels with increased hydrophobic thickness mediate ion transport across three-dimensional networks of synthetic polymer-lipid hybrid membranes. These are not trivial demonstrations. Each requires a distinct pore diameter and lumen chemistry, and each one validates that the generative pipeline can produce them on demand with the right input specifications.

Now, what should you take from this if your work is on antibodies and not nanopores? The lesson is in the recipe, not the application. The four steps are: identify the structural class you want the generative model to handle; build a large distillation set from structure predictions filtered to that class; extract and encode the class-specific structural features as conditioning inputs during fine-tuning; and retrain both the backbone generator and the sequence designer with the expanded receptive field or architecture your class needs. That recipe took transmembrane beta-barrels from a zero-success-rate application of R-F-diffusion to sixty-to-eighty-percent expression in E. coli with sub-angstrom crystal structure accuracy. The analogous experiment for antibody CDR loops would be a distillation set of CDR-grafted backbones from the Observed Antibody Space and its structural extensions, conditioned on loop length, germline, and paratope geometry, fine-tuning R-F-diffusion2 with a CDR-loop-aware conditioning scheme and retraining Protein-M-P-N-N with a receptive field that spans the full VH-VL interface. The nanopore paper is a proof-of-concept that this effort would be worth making.

The second item is a version-three update to a preprint from the Jana Shen lab, posted to bioRxiv yesterday in the biophysics category. The paper is titled "Predicting Protein Electrostatics with Protein Language Models," and the significant new content in version three is a full comparison with E-S-M Cambrian — the recently released E-S-M model trained on two-point-seven-eight billion protein sequences with a new architecture designed to learn emergent structural features.

The core question is whether protein language models encode ionization state information from sequence alone — whether the per-residue embeddings from E-S-M contain enough information to predict p-K-a values for individual amino acids without any structural input.

The short answer is yes, and the accuracy is closer to structural methods than most people would have predicted. The models the Shen lab builds — K-a-M-L-E-S-M-2 and K-a-M-L-E-S-M-C — use per-residue embeddings from E-S-M 2 and E-S-M Cambrian respectively, fed into a multilayer perceptron trained on experimental p-K-a values from the P-K-A-D-3 database. Pretraining on synthetic p-K-a predictions from a structure-based model helps generalize from the small experimental dataset. Separate acid and base models — acidic residues and basic residues are mechanistically different and are better handled by dedicated weights. The result: root-mean-square errors approaching the experimental precision limit of about zero-point-five p-H units for aspartate, glutamate, histidine, and lysine. That is essentially as accurate as you can measure with current experimental techniques, achieved from sequence alone.

The comparison with E-S-M Cambrian is where the paper connects to the conversation the field has been having about protein language model scaling. E-S-M Cambrian at six billion parameters consistently outperforms E-S-M 2 at every size, including E-S-M 2 at fifteen billion parameters. The layer analysis makes the point concrete. For E-S-M 2, the best layer for p-K-a prediction is layer thirty-one out of thirty-three — not the final layer, and the overall error is non-monotonically related to layer depth, with different amino acids peaking at different layers. For E-S-M Cambrian, error decreases uniformly toward the final layer and the per-amino-acid patterns are more consistent, suggesting the model's capacity to encode electrostatic information has not saturated even at eighty layers. The interpretation the authors offer is that E-S-M Cambrian's enhanced ability to learn emergent structural features — which comes from architectural design choices and training on a much larger and more diverse sequence corpus, not just parameter count — translates directly into better electrostatic representations. Making the model bigger without improving the architecture or the data does not do the same thing.

For antibody engineering, p-K-a values are directly relevant. Charged residues in the CDR loops and at the VH-VL interface govern solubility at high concentration, aggregation propensity, and viscosity — the developability properties that govern whether a candidate progresses from the bench to a formulation. Standard practice at the design stage is either to ignore ionization state entirely or to run computationally expensive structure-based p-K-a calculations on predicted or solved structures. A sequence-based predictor approaching structural accuracy is a useful screening tool at the stage where you have hundreds of candidate sequences and need to filter for charge-state-related liabilities before committing to expensive structural characterization. And the result that E-S-M Cambrian embeddings carry more electrostatic information than E-S-M 2 is directly actionable: for tasks where pH sensitivity or charge distribution matters, using the more recently trained model as a feature extractor is the right default.

Two items, then, for Thursday. The Baker lab nanopore paper is the clearest recent demonstration that the R-F-diffusion plus Protein-M-P-N-N toolkit can be systematically extended to structural classes it was not designed for, via a distillation-and-fine-tuning recipe that any lab with access to structure prediction compute can replicate. The K-a-M-L paper is a reminder that protein language models are encoding biophysical properties that go beyond fitness, and that architecture and training data diversity, not raw parameter scale, are what generates those representations. Both results are pointing at things you can do with tools already in your hands. See you tomorrow.
