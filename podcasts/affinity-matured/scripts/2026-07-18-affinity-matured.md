## Script

Good morning. This is Affinity Matured for Saturday, July eighteenth, twenty twenty-six. Today is lighter than the packed Friday, but three papers are worth your attention: a scalable in vitro platform for measuring absolute binding affinities across tens of thousands of protein variants — aimed directly at generating training data for antibody design models; a benchmark study using large language models to mine biomedical literature for vaccine antigen candidates; and a new enzyme-specific protein language model published in Nature Communications that uses a hybrid Mamba-Transformer architecture, relevant to protein language model design trends more broadly.

First item, and the strongest of the day: a preprint updated to version two on bioRxiv yesterday from the Fordyce group at Stanford. The title is "Amplicon/Protein Bead Display enables quantitative in vitro biochemistry at scale." This is a methods paper, but it solves one of the most concrete bottlenecks in training antibody design models: the shortage of large-scale, thermodynamically rigorous sequence-to-affinity datasets.

The core problem is that cell-based high-throughput binding assays — yeast surface display, phage display, bacterial display — return enrichment scores, not dissociation constants. Enrichment scores correlate loosely with binding affinity, but they also pick up protein folding efficiency, surface trafficking, growth effects, and whatever post-translational modifications the cell introduces. If you want to train a model to predict true binding affinity from sequence, you need K-d values, not enrichment ratios. Getting those at scale has historically meant running individual biophysical assays one variant at a time, capping practical throughput at hundreds of variants per experiment.

A-P-B-Display, as the platform is called, solves this with a fully in vitro, bead-based architecture. The starting material is a pooled D-N-A library encoding all variants of interest, together with hydrogel beads dual-functionalized with oligonucleotides for D-N-A capture and benzylguanine groups for covalent protein attachment. The beads go into an emulsion P-C-R step — particle-templated emulsification using a foam tube holder on a standard vortex mixer, no specialized microfluidics required at run time — where Poisson loading ensures that each droplet captures at most one D-N-A molecule, giving you clonal Amplicon Beads each bearing many copies of a single variant's sequence. A second emulsification with in vitro transcription and translation reagents then expresses the variant protein, which self-attaches to the bead through a SNAP tag fused to the N-terminus. The result is a library of Amplicon/Protein Beads where every bead displays a single protein variant at roughly eight hundred thousand copies, alongside the D-N-A amplicon that encoded it. The whole workflow from library D-N-A to protein-decorated beads takes under eight hours with only standard laboratory equipment.

The actual affinity measurement works by incubating the bead library with a fluorescently-labeled ligand across a series of concentrations. In the paper the system is the M2 anti-FLAG antibody binding the FLAG octapeptide — a well-characterized system with a published co-crystal structure, which makes it a useful model for CDR-epitope interactions. Multi-bin F-A-C-S sorting at each concentration, combined with deep sequencing of variant identities within each sorted bin, generates parallelized binding curves from which K-d values are extracted by fitting a Langmuir isotherm. The platform achieved measurements for the complete single-substitution FLAG variant landscape — all one hundred fifty-two single amino acid substitutions at every position — in three days at fifteen cents per variant, with a thermodynamic resolution of less than one-point-four-fold, corresponding to about zero-point-three kilocalories per mole. Validation by fluorescence polarization on seven selected variants gave an R-squared of zero-point-nine-six against the A-P-B values. That is the kind of ground truth you want when training or evaluating an affinity prediction model.

Scaling up to multi-site mutant libraries reveals the platform's real ceiling. A twenty-three-thousand-member library covering all pairwise substitutions and quadruple combinations at four selected FLAG positions completed affinity quantification in three days, returning K-d values for over eighteen thousand variants. Within that landscape the paper identifies chemistry-dependent epistasis between positions four and five of the FLAG sequence — the energetic contribution of a substitution at position four depends on what residue occupies position five — a coupling that is invisible in single-substitution scans and directly relevant to training higher-order affinity models. For even larger libraries, an extension called A-P-B-SortSeq replaces the full concentration series with a single-concentration sort and applies a neural network denoising step to recover quantitative affinities from the noisier single-point data, bringing throughput to over eighty-eight thousand variants.

For antibody engineers, the implications are immediate. The FLAG/M2 system represents one of the cleanest antibody-epitope model systems in the field, which makes the resulting dataset a natural benchmark for CDR sequence-to-affinity predictors. But more importantly the platform is sequence-agnostic: any small protein or peptide of interest, including CDR loops displayed on a fixed framework, can be substituted in. The in vitro format removes the cell-surface trafficking and expression competition artifacts that frustrate yeast display data, and returning absolute K-d values rather than enrichment scores means the datasets slot directly into thermodynamic regression models. If your group is generating or planning to generate a deep mutational scan of an antibody CDR to train or benchmark an affinity predictor, A-P-B-Display is now a strong option alongside yeast display.

Second item: a preprint posted to bioRxiv yesterday, from a team in China, titled "SemVac: A Semantic Vaccinology Paradigm Powered by L-L-Ms for Antigen Discovery." This one sits at the boundary between large language model methodology and vaccine development.

Classical reverse vaccinology — predicting protective antigens from a pathogen's genome sequence using trained sequence-based classifiers — has been the standard computational approach since the early two-thousands. The limitation it hits is a data problem: the classifiers are trained on a relatively small set of experimentally validated antigens and struggle with functionally ambiguous proteins where no clear sequence signature of protective potential exists. What SemVac proposes is a parallel approach: ask whether the biomedical literature itself, as encoded in the internal representations of large language models, can predict protective antigen potential more robustly than sequence-based tools.

The approach is zero-shot text reasoning. Given a protein name and a query about its protective antigen potential, the language model draws on its training corpus — published descriptions of immune responses, surface exposure studies, functional annotations, immune evasion literature — and produces a prediction without fine-tuning on labeled antigen data. The paper benchmarks fourteen current-generation language models on a curated dataset of validated and non-validated antigens.

The headline finding is that text-reasoning language models match or exceed specialized deep learning models trained explicitly for antigen prediction, such as VaxiJen and N-E-R-V-E, in precision. That is a non-obvious result: the language models were not trained on antigen prediction tasks, yet the knowledge compressed into their representations from scientific literature appears sufficient to recover a useful signal.

The subtler and practically more important finding is what the authors call the over-reasoning pitfall. Chain-of-thought prompting — asking the model to reason step by step before producing a prediction — consistently increases recall: the model identifies more true protective antigens. But it just as consistently reduces precision: the model generates plausible reasoning chains that lead it to flag proteins that turn out not to be validated antigens. If you deploy chain-of-thought for antigen discovery, you get broader coverage of the true antigen space at the cost of more false positives, and you need to account for that in your experimental follow-up budget.

Applied to the complete Mpox proteome, SemVac recapitulates all known protective antigens and identifies B20R as a previously unrecognized candidate. The semantic reasoning behind that prediction links B20R to immune evasion and surface structural exposure — exactly the biological signatures expected of a protective antigen — though experimental validation is required and the paper frames B20R as a hypothesis, not a confirmed target.

For antibody campaigns, SemVac is most relevant as an upstream target-selection tool. Identifying which antigens are worth pursuing, and which epitopes within an antigen are accessible, conserved, and immunogenically dominant, shapes every downstream antibody design decision. A language-model-based literature sweep that flags candidate antigens more broadly than a trained sequence classifier — at the cost of some precision that experimental screening can adjudicate — is a useful complement to existing computational tools, particularly when the target pathogen has limited experimental antigen data.

Third item, brief: a paper published yesterday in Nature Communications from the Shen group at Johns Hopkins and collaborators. The title is "An enzyme-specific protein language model for catalytic property prediction." The model is called EnzGFM, and it is built around a hybrid Mamba-Transformer architecture with what the authors call hierarchical pre-training.

Mamba is a state-space model that processes sequences with computational costs that scale linearly with sequence length, in contrast to the quadratic scaling of standard self-attention in Transformer models. For long proteins, that difference matters both for training cost and for inference speed. The claim in EnzGFM is that layering Mamba operations with Transformer layers captures both long-range sequence dependencies and local structural patterns more efficiently than either architecture alone, under enzyme-specific pre-training.

The performance numbers, all measured as relative improvements over Transformer-based baselines at comparable parameter counts, are: sixteen-point-seven percent on kinetic parameter prediction, fifteen-point-seven percent on enzyme-to-reaction mapping, thirteen-point-two percent on E-C number classification, and twenty percent on mutation effect assessment, at two to five times faster inference. An agentic extension wraps the model in a candidate-selection pipeline the paper calls EnzGFM-Agent, with claimed experimental enrichment of beneficial variants in small candidate pools.

This is an enzyme paper rather than an antibody paper, and the benchmarks are on enzyme tasks. But the Mamba-Transformer hybrid architecture is a live question in protein language model design more broadly. If the speed and task-performance gains survive pre-training on antibody-specific sequence data — where sequences are shorter than typical enzymes but paired heavy-and-light chain co-modeling introduces other long-context challenges — this architecture class is worth tracking. The antibody language model field is still largely Transformer-based; EnzGFM is a working proof-of-concept that the architecture space is not closed.

That covers today. Three papers, a moderate Saturday. A-P-B-Display gives you a concrete new tool for generating absolute-affinity training datasets at scale, directly applicable to CDR mutational scanning. SemVac demonstrates that language model text reasoning is a competitive complement to sequence-based antigen discovery, with a precision cost you need to manage experimentally. And EnzGFM shows a hybrid Mamba-Transformer protein language model outperforming Transformer baselines on enzyme tasks, a trend worth watching for the antibody modeling space.
