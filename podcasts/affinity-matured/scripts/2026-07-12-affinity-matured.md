## Script

Welcome to Affinity Matured for Sunday, July 12th. Two bioRxiv preprints anchor today's brief. The first is a benchmark that stress-tests the current generation of AI structure predictors on proteins with more than one functional conformation, and the second is a protein-and-nucleotide foundation model that swaps the transformer for a state-space architecture. It is a moderate weekend on the preprint side — arXiv did not post over the weekend and PubMed indexing is patchy today — so we are keeping it tight and skipping the padding.

The lead is a benchmark from James Gumbart's group at Georgia Tech, and it is a paper that anyone who runs antibody-antigen complex prediction should read this week. The title is "Benchmarking AI Protein Structure Predictors Reveals a Persistent Bias in Multi-State Proteins." They evaluate four current-generation predictors — AlphaFold3, Boltz-2, Chai-1, and BioEmu — on four proteins that are known to occupy multiple functional conformations. The four are P-F-MATE, a multidrug-and-toxin-extrusion transporter that alternates between inward-open and outward-open states; LAO, the lysine-arginine-ornithine periplasmic binding protein with a classic open-and-closed hinge; SecA, the ATPase motor that drives protein translocation across the bacterial inner membrane; and beta-two adrenergic receptor, the canonical class A G-protein-coupled receptor with well-characterized active and inactive conformations.

The core finding is direct, and while it is not new in spirit — the field has known that AlphaFold defaults to a mode of the training distribution — it is important in the specifics. All four predictors preferentially return the conformational state that is most heavily represented in the Protein Data Bank for that protein. Adding a small-molecule ligand to the input — even a ligand known to bias the protein toward a specific state — has weak and inconsistent effects on which state the predictor produces. But adding a large protein binding partner does drive clear conformational switching between the two states. So the models are not blind to context; they are just very selectively sensitive to it, and the sensitivity is dominated by protein-protein interfaces, not by small-molecule cues.

They also test the classic multiple-sequence-alignment tricks — AF-Cluster and random subsampling of the alignment — that were designed to coax AlphaFold into sampling alternative states. Those tricks recapitulate similar biases in this benchmark. So the state bias is not an artifact of a single architecture; it appears to be a structural property of what these models are learning from the Protein Data Bank, and it survives across MSA-manipulation strategies and across the newer diffusion-and-flow-based architectures like Boltz-2 and Chai-1. The other listed method, BioEmu, is a Microsoft Research model explicitly trained to emit conformational ensembles rather than single structures, and it also shows the state bias here — worth noting because BioEmu was the one predictor in this list that a naive reader might have expected to escape it.

Why does this matter for antibody work? Two reasons. First, if you are using AlphaFold3, Boltz, or Chai to model an antibody in complex with a conformationally flexible antigen — a class A G-protein-coupled receptor, an ion channel, a transporter, a receptor with active and inactive states — you should assume the predictor is going to hand you the dominant Protein Data Bank state of the antigen, and treat any inference about state-selective binding with real skepticism. Second, if you are running any structure-guided design workflow — the kind we covered on the show earlier this week — the fixed-backbone assumption underlying tools like FoldX is now compounded by a state-selection assumption in the predictor that produced the model in the first place. The upstream single-state error propagates through everything downstream. This is a healthy pairing with the frustration-driven S-F-Cluster benchmark we covered on July 6th, which found that MSA composition — not architecture — is what unlocks conformational diversity. Today's paper reinforces that finding by showing the bias persists across architectures when you do not do that alignment-side work.

There are two caveats. The benchmark is four proteins, chosen precisely because they are known multi-state canonical cases. It is not a survey of the general protein universe, so quantitative failure rates should not be over-generalized. And "MSA tricks recapitulate similar biases" is a slightly stronger claim on the abstract than the field is likely to accept without seeing the numbers in full — worth reading the full preprint for exactly how AF-Cluster performed against random subsampling. But the direction of the paper is clear, and the practical message for antibody structural workflows is unambiguous: know which state your predictor is giving you before you build a design campaign on it.

The second paper comes from Nima Azbijari and colleagues at Oregon State, and it is not antibody work — it is a protein foundation-model architecture paper with implications for antibody language models. The title is "Metagenomic contextualization of proteins with state space models," and the model is called Nammu. Nammu is a 167-million-parameter mixed-modality foundation model built on the Mamba architecture — a state-space model, not a transformer. The motivation is directly quantitative: transformers have quadratic scaling in sequence length because of self-attention, and metagenomic contigs — and any long protein-context input — get truncated when you try to run them through a standard protein transformer. Mamba scales linearly, so long context is genuinely feasible.

Nammu is trained on the OpenMetaGenomic corpus with a curriculum. The first phase is protein-only, over 64 million protein sequences. The second phase is mixed-modality contigs — 32 million assembled sequences that mix coding and non-coding regions and thus interleave protein and nucleotide tokens. Context length during pretraining goes up to twenty thousand tokens, which is well beyond what a standard protein transformer sees. On downstream evaluation, they compare against gLM2, a mixed-modality transformer trained on the same corpus but with thirty-seven percent more tokens. On the Critical Assessment of Metagenome Interpretation marine dataset, Nammu outperforms gLM2 at every taxonomic level for taxonomy inference. And for functional prediction — KEGG Orthology annotation on deep-sea metagenome-assembled genomes — Nammu also outperforms the transformer baseline.

The relevance for antibody work is architectural rather than immediate. Antibody language modeling has been mostly transformer-based, and paired-chain or repertoire-context modeling collides with the same quadratic-attention wall that motivated Nammu. Paired heavy and light with a linker is short enough that it does not matter today. But repertoire-conditioned generative modeling — modeling a whole B-cell repertoire as one long sequence, or conditioning generation on many thousands of paired sequences from a single donor — is exactly the place where linear-scaling architectures might change what is trainable at all. Whether Mamba-style models learn the specific inductive biases that make antibody language models work well — complementarity-determining-region positional priors, germline structure, somatic-hypermutation patterns — is an open question that Nammu does not answer. But it is a concrete demonstration that state-space models are competitive with transformers as protein-and-nucleotide foundation models, and the antibody-specific version of this experiment is now clearly worth doing. Watch for a paired-chain state-space model to appear over the next few months.

To wrap up: two picks today. A structure-predictor benchmark from Georgia Tech that says AlphaFold3, Boltz-2, Chai-1, and BioEmu all default to the dominant Protein Data Bank state and can only be pushed off it reliably by protein partners, not by small-molecule ligands. And a Mamba-based protein-and-nucleotide foundation model from Oregon State that beats a same-corpus transformer on both taxonomy and functional annotation while enabling twenty-thousand-token context. Different problems, same underlying question — how much do we trust the predictions our pipelines are built on, and where does architecture actually change what is possible.
