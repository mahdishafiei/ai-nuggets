**First: a biophysical model for what I-g-D is for.**

Immunoglobulin D — I-g-D — is one of the most conserved antibody isotypes across jawed vertebrates. It's co-expressed with I-g-M on the surface of transitional and mature naive B cells, and that co-expression pattern has been preserved for more than four hundred million years of evolution. Despite that conservation, since its discovery in 1965, the physiological purpose of I-g-D has been genuinely unclear. What's been clear is that it's structurally distinctive: I-g-D has an unusually long hinge region — up to a hundred and sixty amino acids — between the antigen-binding arms and the constant region. That hinge is much longer and more flexible than the equivalent segment in I-g-M or I-g-G, and the field has not had a satisfying account of what that flexibility does functionally.

A preprint from the Nussenzweig lab at Rockefeller, with theory contributions from the Chakraborty group at M-I-T and single-molecule measurements from Scott Blanchard's group, offers a physical answer. The core idea is that the long, dynamic I-g-D hinge imposes an entropic penalty on bivalent antigen binding. If the two antigen-binding arms of a B-cell receptor are floppy — sampling a wide conformational ensemble — then locking them into a productive bivalent engagement with antigen costs more free energy than it would for a rigid receptor. The predicted consequence is that surface I-g-D binds antigen more poorly than surface I-g-M of matched specificity, and that the effect shows up specifically in the bivalent regime that dominates B-cell receptor signaling.

They test this in three layers. First, single-molecule measurements directly quantify the conformational dynamics of the two antigen-binding arms in I-g-D versus I-g-M. The I-g-D arms are substantially more dynamic. Second, on live B cells, I-g-D B-cell receptors capture antigen less efficiently than matched-specificity I-g-M B-cell receptors, consistent with the entropic-penalty prediction. Third, they generate mice with tailored isotype configurations — mice expressing wild-type I-g-M plus I-g-D, mice expressing I-g-M only, mice expressing I-g-D only, and — the load-bearing control — mice expressing I-g-D with a truncated hinge that removes the extra flexibility.

The mouse data lock the story in on two well-defined selection thresholds. The entropic penalty on I-g-D binding attenuates negative selection by self-antigen — B cells whose only surface immunoglobulin is I-g-D are less likely to be culled for weak self-reactivity than cells with I-g-M. And the same penalty raises the affinity threshold that a naive B cell must clear to be positively selected into the germinal center after immunization. When you shorten the hinge to remove the flexibility, both effects erode. The biophysics is doing the work.

The take-home is that I-g-D is not a redundant copy of I-g-M. Its physiological role is to desensitize B cells to antigen — expanding the initial naive repertoire by tolerating weakly self-reactive clones, while simultaneously tightening affinity-based selection into the germinal center. For anyone thinking about repertoire modeling, B-cell receptor signaling, or the mechanics of affinity maturation, this is a load-bearing paper: it recasts the geometry and dynamics of the receptor itself — hinge flexibility, arm dynamics — as tunable parameters of the selection landscape rather than as fixed background.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.02.735902

**Second: a broad benchmark of zero-shot protein language models on a real thermostability landscape.**

A recurring frustration in protein language model evaluation is that most benchmarks are either narrow — a single deep mutational scan or a handful of proteins — or synthetic. Antibody engineers want to know: if I train no model of my own and I need to zero-shot rank mutations against a new target for stability or activity, which off-the-shelf protein language model actually works? A preprint posted today from Michael Jewett's group at Northwestern, with Debora Marks at Harvard, Pascal Notin — who works on the ProteinGym benchmark — and Greg Beckham at the National Renewable Energy Lab, takes a serious swing at that question on a real industrial engineering target: thermostable carbonic anhydrase for carbon capture.

The methodology is the interesting part. They built a high-throughput platform that couples cell-free gene expression with a gas-phase colorimetric assay for carbon-dioxide hydration activity. Cell-free expression lets them screen thousands of enzyme variants without running an in-vivo expression pipeline for each one. From ninety-six natural carbonic-anhydrase homologs they identified a robust variant from the Aquificota bacterial phylum, and then ran an essentially complete single-amino-acid saturation scan on that enzyme — over ninety-nine percent of all possible single substitutions, four thousand three hundred and sixty-five mutations, assayed in thirty-nine thousand two hundred and eighty-five reactions, at two temperatures: seventy degrees Celsius and ninety degrees Celsius. That gives them a paired thermostability fitness landscape, not a single-condition one — and that pairing is what makes it a useful benchmark, because the two temperatures separate mutations that improve activity from mutations that improve stability under stress.

With that landscape in hand, they evaluated twenty-two zero-shot protein fitness models — the standard menu of protein language models plus MSA-based methods, alignment-free single-sequence models, and structure-informed variants. Two things about that comparison are worth flagging. First, it's on a real forward-engineering target under conditions that most natural enzymes fail — ninety degrees is well above the stability of the majority of homologs — and it uses a nearly complete saturation scan rather than a sparse sample, so the ranking of the twenty-two models is not driven by benchmark artifacts. Second, from the landscape they identified critical mutations that improve enzyme stability at ninety Celsius by more than three-fold.

They then close the loop. Using Protein-M-P-N-N, they generate an in-silico library of a hundred thousand candidate variants. They apply both zero-shot protein language models and supervised regression heads trained on their saturation-scan data to filter that library down to four hundred and nineteen model-picked variants, characterize them experimentally, and identify a best-in-class enzyme that retains activity even after incubation at ninety-five Celsius. The workflow is: high-throughput cell-free assay to generate a real saturation benchmark, benchmark the zero-shot models on that data, then use the best models — combined with modest supervised data — to filter a much larger Protein-M-P-N-N-generated library.

The relevance to antibody engineering is direct on three fronts. First, this is currently one of the more rigorous head-to-head evaluations of zero-shot protein language models on a real stability landscape — the specific model rankings will be worth reading closely when the paper's body fully renders on bioRxiv. Second, the workflow — high-throughput cell-free saturation assay, then Protein-M-P-N-N-plus-language-model filter of a large in-silico library — translates cleanly to antibody developability engineering, where thermostability, expression yield, and aggregation propensity are all well-suited to cell-free readouts. Third, and consistent with a thread running through several of this week's episodes — the E-S-M plus M-M-G-B-S-A benchmark from Monday morning, and the ThermoFusion structure-plus-sequence combination we covered last night — hybrid pipelines that combine a language model with a modest amount of task-specific supervised data or a physics-based scoring layer beat single-model pipelines on real fitness data. Add another data point to that pile.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.07.736810
