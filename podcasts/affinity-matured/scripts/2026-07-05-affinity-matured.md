## Script

Welcome to Affinity Matured, your daily brief on machine learning for antibody and protein design. Today, four items from the last two days: a new geometric descriptor for immune-receptor–antigen docking, an uncertainty-aware model for T-cell receptor specificity with structure-guided data augmentation, a cryo-electron microscopy structure that rewrites how we think about brain-shuttle antibodies, and a distilled protein language model that acts as a foundational implicit solvent for molecular dynamics.

First item. A group at M.D. Anderson led by Ken Chen has released a preprint called T-C-R FramePose. It is a local-frame descriptor set for T-cell-receptor–peptide–M-H-C complexes, and while it is written for T-cell receptors, the ideas are directly relevant to how we should be thinking about antibody–antigen docking, especially C-D-R-H-3 pose. The motivation is simple: docking geometry in receptor–antigen complexes is usually summarized at the whole-receptor level, most famously by a single crossing angle. That collapses a rich six-degree-of-freedom rigid-body pose into a scalar, and it makes the loop-local geometry of C-D-R-3 hard to compare across structures. FramePose instead decomposes each complex into three bodies — the whole receptor, C-D-R-3 alpha, and C-D-R-3 beta — each measured relative to a groove frame anchored on the peptide–M-H-C. For each body, they extract three quantities: a reach, an offset direction on a sphere, and an orientation on the rotation group. Those get mapped into eighteen tangent-space coordinates per complex, which are analyzable with standard multivariate statistics.

They applied this to a curated set of three hundred and seventy-eight alpha–beta T-cell-receptor–peptide–M-H-C crystal structures. Two findings stand out. First, in cross-validated association analyses FramePose adds nonredundant information about buried surface area and binding affinity beyond conventional descriptors, and the modest affinity gain concentrates in the C-D-R-3 orientation blocks — the very features that are least recoverable from crossing angle and contact statistics. Second, and more interesting for engineering, when they group receptors by target they find that shared pose over a given peptide–M-H-C is organized primarily by germline V-region framework, not by C-D-R-3 sequence. Put another way: the framework fixes the pose, and C-D-R-3 sequence variation does not detectably reposition the rigid-body dock after antigen context and germline are fixed. That is a strong claim, and it has an obvious analogue in antibodies, where the choice of V-heavy and V-light genes is known to bias approach angle. Practically, the eighteen-coordinate descriptor is designed as a geometric filter for in-silico models — you can ask whether a predicted complex sits inside the distribution of native poses seen for that germline family — and as a feature layer for structure-guided receptor engineering. It is a good template for the antibody community to borrow.

Second item. Another preprint out this week, from the Jonsson group at U-C Santa Cruz, introduces PRISM, an uncertainty-aware metric-learning framework for T-cell-receptor-beta sequences. Here again the machinery is T-cell-focused but the ideas transfer. The problem PRISM tackles is that models predicting which peptide a T-cell receptor recognizes are limited by three things: sparse labels, a heavy viral bias in public training data, and no principled way to know when a query receptor sits outside the training distribution. PRISM addresses all three at once. The architecture is a metric-learning embedder that maps receptors into a peptide-organized latent space, returns candidate peptides by nearest-neighbor retrieval, and — this is the key move — abstains on out-of-distribution receptors by modeling an intrinsic uncertainty that tracks annotation correctness. To offset viral bias, they augment training with structure-guided synthetic receptors: sequences that are diversified away from the observed data while preserving the interface energetics of the T-C-R–peptide–M-H-C complex. That is a nice trick, because the alternative — augmenting with sequence perturbations alone — would just teach the model that neighbors in edit space share labels, which is exactly the failure mode you want to avoid on rare epitopes.

Across a held-out set of nine hundred and twenty-three peptides and the independent I-M-M-R-E-P twenty-three benchmark, PRISM matches or exceeds sequence-only baselines, with the largest gains on rare epitopes — the regime where existing models effectively memorize. When they inspect the learned attention weights, the model concentrates on the C-D-R-3-beta salt-bridge and hydrophobic contacts that structural biology has already flagged as central to peptide recognition. Two takeaways for the antibody-design audience. First, structure-guided synthetic augmentation is a general strategy for any low-label receptor-specificity task, and it is precisely the kind of augmentation that antibody paratope prediction and epitope binding models tend not to use. Second, an abstention mechanism trained on annotation-correctness is a much more useful uncertainty signal than the softmax-entropy calibration that most antibody classifiers ship with today, especially when you are screening a repertoire that will contain many receptors outside your training coverage.

Third item. A structural biology preprint that is directly on antibody engineering. A collaboration between Cambridge, Oxford, and AstraZeneca — Paul Miller and colleagues — has published a molecular mechanism of action for the model brain-shuttle antibody 8-D-3. This is the reference tool antibody for transferrin-receptor-mediated transcytosis, which is emerging as the dominant strategy for getting therapeutic antibodies across the blood–brain barrier. They determined the cryo-electron microscopy structure of mouse transferrin receptor one bound to 8-D-3, and combined it with cell binding, localization, and in-vivo brain-penetration studies of engineered variants.

Three points that matter for anyone designing brain-shuttle constructs. One: the 8-D-3 architecture can structurally support small-scale inter-receptor cross-linking — think self-contained pairs — that produces avidity effects but does not drive receptor redistribution or lysosomal degradation. That is the sweet spot: you want an avidity boost without triggering the pathway that ends with your antibody destroyed inside a lysosome. Two: the structure explains p-H-dependent binding modes, so you can rationalize why some 8-D-3 variants disengage in the acidic endosome and recycle back to the luminal surface while others hang on and get degraded. Three, and most usefully, they design and characterize a graded panel of variants with different p-H sensitivities, and they show these variants achieve different levels of brain penetration in vivo. For the audience: this is not an M-L paper, but it is the kind of structure–function map that antibody language models and structure-based design tools should be conditioning on. If you are training a shuttle-antibody generator, you want your reward function to include not just transferrin-receptor affinity, but the p-H response curve and the avidity mode that this preprint just quantified.

Fourth and final item. This one landed in PubMed on July third — a paper by Justin Airas and Bin Zhang at M-I-T, in the Journal of Chemical Theory and Computation, titled "Knowledge distillation of a protein language model yields a foundational implicit solvent model." The setup: E-S-M-3, the current large protein language model from EvolutionaryScale, is used as a teacher, and a graph neural network is trained as the student to reproduce E-S-M-3's effective energies over protein conformations. The graph neural network is then used as an implicit solvent term, combined with a standard electrostatics term, to drive stable long-timescale molecular dynamics simulations.

Why this matters. Traditional implicit solvent models — generalized Born, Poisson–Boltzmann — are fast but they were parameterized on small-molecule and folded-protein data, and they struggle on intrinsically disordered regions, where the conformational ensemble is exactly what you want to characterize. Language-model-derived potentials, by contrast, are trained on the evolutionary distribution of protein sequences and inherit implicit knowledge of both folded and disordered regimes. The distilled network reproduces folding free-energy landscapes for benchmark proteins and, more interestingly, predicts the structural ensembles of intrinsically disordered proteins that classical implicit solvent gets wrong. For antibody people this is directly relevant in two ways. First, developability workflows increasingly need to sample disordered linker and complementarity-determining region conformations, and current force-field-plus-implicit-solvent pipelines are demonstrably biased there. Second, a language-model-distilled potential is a portable substitute for expensive full-atom simulations — the kind of thing you might drop into an antibody design loop that today calls out to Rosetta or a molecular-dynamics oracle for scoring. Note the caveat: the underlying preprint has been out since January, so this is not brand-new science. But the journal version landing this week means it has now been through peer review, and the paper's benchmarks are worth the read if you have not already.
