**First: GELYTACs — a nanobody-and-IGF2 bifunctional protein encoded in AAV for persistent IgG depletion in vivo.**

This paper is from Carolyn Bertozzi's lab at Stanford — a lab primarily known for bioorthogonal chemistry but increasingly active in protein degradation and targeted trafficking — with a collaborating contribution from Karl Deisseroth's group. The work extends a class of molecules the Bertozzi group developed previously: lysosome-targeting chimeras, or LYTACs, which are bifunctional molecules that bind a target on one end and engage a lysosomal trafficking receptor on the other, routing the target for degradation. The earlier generation of LYTACs were polypeptide conjugates administered as recombinant proteins with short half-lives and the need for repeat dosing. This paper asks whether you can move the whole construct into a gene therapy vector and have the animal produce it continuously.

The construct design is elegant. A genetically-encoded LYTAC — a GELYAC — is a two-module secreted protein. The first module is murine IGF2, which is a 7.5 kilodalton protein that binds with low nanomolar affinity to the mannose-6-phosphate receptor, also called the IGF2 receptor. The mannose-6-phosphate receptor is a well-established lysosomal trafficking receptor: when it captures a ligand at the cell surface, it routes the captured cargo intracellularly to lysosomes. The second module is a nanobody that binds IgG. The two modules are connected through a 20-amino acid glycine-serine linker, arranged N-terminus to C-terminus as IGF2 then linker then nanobody. The construct is secreted — it exits the producing cell and circulates — where it captures IgGs in the extracellular space and presents those IgGs to the mannose-6-phosphate receptor on nearby cells, routing them for lysosomal degradation.

The in vitro results establish that the bifunctional architecture works across a broad panel of murine cell types: lymphocytes, macrophages, epithelial cells, microglia, and hepatocytes all internalized labeled IgG specifically and via a lysosomal route when the mGELYAC was present, confirmed by LAMP1 colocalization in confocal microscopy. Media depletion assays showed the mGELYAC clears labeled IgG from the coculture supernatant significantly better than a nanobody-only control, which is the key negative control — the nanobody alone can bind IgG but cannot route it to lysosomes without the IGF2 module.

The in vivo experiments encode the mGELYAC in an AAV8 vector under an EF1-alpha promoter and deliver it via tail vein injection in C57BL/6 mice. The first question is persistence: how long does the construct continue to be produced and secreted? The answer from bioluminescence imaging — using both a nanoLuciferase fusion and a split nanoLuciferase HiBit system that selectively reports on secreted extracellular mGELYAC — is that expression is sustained in vivo for at least 77 days from a single injection, with no evidence of decline in the imaging window reported. The organs showing the strongest mGELYAC-nanoLuciferase signal are consistent with the known tropism of AAV8 for liver.

For IgG clearance in vivo: mice were co-injected with AAV8 encoding the mGELYAC and a bolus of labeled IgG. At day 15, animals receiving the mGELYAC had significantly lower circulating labeled IgG compared to animals receiving the nanobody-only control or a non-targeting mGELYAC control. A direct comparison at a fixed dose places the recombinant form of the mGELYAC as equivalent or superior to efgartigimod alfa — the FDA-approved FcRn inhibitor that works by blocking neonatal Fc receptor recycling and is the current standard-of-care for reducing IgG burden in IgG-driven autoimmune diseases. The critical difference is pharmacokinetics: efgartigimod alfa and similar FcRn-directed agents require weekly or biweekly infusions because they clear rapidly. The AAV-encoded mGELYAC, once the transduced liver cells are established, continues producing the bifunctional protein for months.

The two conditional variants are worth understanding separately. The protease-gated version works by fusing an IGF2-binding protein — one of the six known insulin-like growth factor binding proteins — to the N-terminus of mGELYAC via a protease-cleavable linker. The IGF2-binding protein masks the IGF2 module, preventing mannose-6-phosphate receptor engagement, so the construct is inactive in the caged form. When a disease-specific protease cleaves the linker, the mask is released and the mGELYAC becomes active. The authors demonstrate this principle using granzyme B as the activating protease. Granzyme B is released at the immune synapse — the contact zone between cytotoxic T cells or natural killer cells and their targets — meaning that in an autoimmune context where such immune synapses are forming inappropriately, granzyme B would locally uncage the IgG-depleting function at the relevant cellular interface rather than throughout the circulation. That is a potentially meaningful level of spatial selectivity. The small-molecule-gated variant uses an X-on alternative splicing element: the construct includes an intronic sequence that, in the absence of drug, is spliced out in a way that disrupts the reading frame; addition of the splicing-correcting small molecule restores proper splicing and enables mGELYAC expression.

For this show's audience, the technical component worth dwelling on is the nanobody. The IgG-binding nanobody is the targeting module that gives GELYTACs their IgG specificity, and in principle, swapping in a different nanobody would redirect the degradation machinery toward a different target. The group has demonstrated in prior work that LYTACs can be built against other extracellular targets; the IgG-targeting version is particularly compelling because eliminating pathogenic IgGs addresses a well-defined mechanism in autoimmune encephalitis, myasthenia gravis, systemic lupus, rheumatoid arthritis, and transplant rejection. The novelty relative to B-cell depletion with rituximab or B-cell-directed CAR-T approaches is that GELYTACs act on the antibody molecules themselves, not on the B cells that produce them — which means they have no effect on IgG produced by CD20-negative or CAR-T-resistant plasma cells, but they also don't require eliminating the cellular compartment that makes memory. And relative to FcRn inhibition, GELYTACs are not dependent on the FcRn recycling pathway and are not blocked by FcRn-independent IgG pharmacokinetics.

The study is a proof-of-concept in mouse models with an anti-rabbit IgG nanobody as the targeting module. The translational next steps — switching to an anti-human IgG nanobody with appropriate FcRn binding properties, moving to humanized or non-human primate models, evaluating whether the transduced cell types also express IGF2R at high enough levels for efficient autocrine or paracrine depletion — are not addressed here, and the discussion names them as open questions. The fundamental principle — a genetically encoded, nanobody-directed lysosomal routing construct that provides sustained activity from a single AAV injection — is demonstrated clearly enough to call this a proof-of-concept that advances the field.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.05.736665

**Second: monobody 12D4 KRAS recognition — a structural and computational dissection, and redesign toward previously untargetable mutants.**

Monobodies are a class of engineered binding proteins built on the fibronectin type III domain — a small, disulfide-free scaffold with loops that tolerate CDR-like diversification. They share conceptual territory with VHHs and nanobodies as compact single-domain binding proteins capable of targeting extended protein surfaces. A monobody called 12D4 was previously reported to have high affinity and selectivity for KRAS G12D — one of the most prevalent oncogenic K-RAS mutations — but the structural basis of its mutant selectivity was not known. This preprint from Yu-ming Huang's group at Wayne State University combines molecular dynamics simulations with free energy calculations to characterize 12D4 binding across five K-RAS states: wild-type, G12C, G12D, G12V, and G12R in both GTP-bound and GDP-bound conformations. The simulations identify a conserved hydrophobic interaction network in the monobody's F-G loop — centered on residues L77, F78, and W79 — that anchors the monobody to the Switch II and alpha-3-helix surface of K-RAS regardless of mutation status. The mutation-specific selectivity for G12D, by contrast, comes from a single electrostatic interaction: lysine 75 of 12D4 forms a specific ion pair with the aspartate at position 12 of the G12D variant. That ion pair is absent in WT, G12C, G12V, and G12R. No existing monobody covers KRAS G12R or the GDP-bound states of G12C and G12V. The authors computationally redesigned position 75, identifying K75Q, K75Y, and K75M as mutations predicted to improve binding to those currently untargetable variants by forming new contacts. The work is abstract-only at time of recording, so wet-lab validation of these redesigned variants is not reported here. For the audience, the significance is the principle: a single residue position in a loop of an engineered single-domain binding protein governs the mutation specificity for a clinically critical target, and computational redesign of that position may access variants that neither small molecules nor existing monobodies can currently address.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.09.737552

**Third: anti-alpha-synuclein single-domain antibody 2H1 — in vivo efficacy in a Drosophila synucleinopathy model.**

This is a brief note on a new single-domain antibody from the Einar Sigurdsson group at New York University Grossman School of Medicine, reported at abstract-only depth since the preprint body is still rendering. The Sigurdsson lab has a long track record developing anti-alpha-synuclein single-domain antibodies as candidates for Parkinson's disease and related synucleinopathies. This preprint evaluates five anti-alpha-syn sdAbs in parallel — first for their ability to clear pathological alpha-syn in mouse neuronal culture, then for in vivo efficacy in a Drosophila model of synucleinopathy that recapitulates dopaminergic neuron loss, mitochondrial dysfunction, and motor impairment. The most effective sdAb is called 2H1, which is described as a new sdAb not previously reported. It targets the aggregation-prone region of alpha-synuclein specifically. In synucleinopathy flies, 2H1 reduced phospho-serine 129 alpha-syn levels, prevented dopaminergic neuron loss, alleviated mitochondrial dysfunction, improved motor function, and prolonged survival — all robustly compared to the other four sdAbs tested. The turboID proximity labeling experiments localize the sdAb-alpha-syn interaction to synaptic and vesicle trafficking compartments in fly neurons. For the show's audience: this is a wet-lab validation of a new intracellular-targeting VHH scaffold, and the specific finding — that sdAbs targeting the aggregation-prone region of an intrinsically disordered disease protein outperform those binding other epitopes in an in vivo disease model — is the kind of structure-activity data point that should eventually feed into computational tools for intracellular sdAb design.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.07.735601
