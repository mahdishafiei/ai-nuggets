**First item**, published today in Scientific Reports from a Cambridge and Imperial College London collaboration with Chimeris U-K. The paper is "Developability engineering of scFvs enables robust CAR function under transient mRNA expression," with Mangolini and Ramon as co-first authors, corresponding authors Sormanni at Cambridge and Imperial and Onuoha at Chimeris. A note: the full text is behind a journal paywall from this machine; what follows is grounded in the abstract and in the Sormanni group's published methodology, which is documented in detail across prior work on C-A-M-Sol and related tools.

The core question is: does the biophysical quality of a single-chain variable fragment — the scFv — matter differently under mRNA-LNP delivery than under stable viral transduction? The intuition behind the question is straightforward. With lentiviral or retroviral delivery, the CAR construct integrates stably and the cell keeps producing protein over days and weeks. Whatever fraction of each protein copy misfolds, aggregates, or fails to traffic to the membrane is simply replaced by the next round of transcription. The quality floor for functional CAR surface expression is relatively permissive because you have time to accumulate the stable fraction. With mRNA-LNP delivery, the expression window is transient — hours to a couple of days for the burst, then it's gone. You get one allotment of protein, and if a substantial fraction misfolds or aggregates before reaching the cell surface, that loss is permanent. The effective CAR surface density available for antigen engagement is determined by the fraction that clears the biophysical quality filters on the first pass, not by an equilibrium between production and clearance.

The paper tests this using three clinically relevant scFv families chosen specifically to span the biophysical quality spectrum. FMC63 is the anti-CD19 binder that underpins axicabtagene ciloleucel and lisocabtagene maraleucel. It is predominantly monomeric, well-humanized, and already a biophysically permissive starting point. 14G2a is an anti-GD2 binder used in neuroblastoma CAR-T programs; it is known to be aggregation-prone, with a murine-derived sequence that scores poorly on aggregation propensity metrics. MGA271, also called chBRCA84D, is an anti-B7-H3 binder that sits between the other two in baseline biophysical quality.

The computational workflow applies the Sormanni group's established developability toolkit — humanness scoring by comparison to human germline databases, aggregation propensity from the C-A-M-Sol solubility predictor, thermal stability estimates from sequence-based predictors, and non-specific interaction propensity. That battery generates a multi-axis developability profile for each starting sequence. From there, in silico humanization variants are generated for each family, scored against the profile, and the variants with improved aggregate scores go forward to cell-based functional testing.

The outcome for each family is a study in how the starting point determines the headroom. For FMC63, optimization improved humanness and in some cases thermal stability, but CAR surface expression and cytotoxic activity in primary human T cells after mRNA-LNP delivery were essentially unchanged. FMC63 was already biophysically adequate, and incremental improvements within a quality space that was already permissive produced no functional gain. That is the positive control: it confirms that the workflow doesn't degrade a working antibody, and it tells you that the ceiling for this binder under mRNA delivery is determined by something other than biophysics.

For 14G2a, the result is clear and clinically relevant. The parent scFv, aggregation-prone as it is, showed poor CAR surface expression after mRNA delivery in primary T cells, and cytotoxic activity against GD2-positive target cells was proportionally impaired. The computationally optimized variant — substantially reduced aggregation propensity — markedly increased both CAR surface expression and cytotoxic function. The aggregation propensity of the parent was the rate-limiting failure mode, and correcting it in silico before touching a cell was sufficient to rescue function. For anyone running an mRNA CAR-T program where the binder was selected for affinity and specificity without a parallel developability check — which describes most traditional antibody discovery workflows — this is the risk signal the paper quantifies.

For MGA271, the picture is mechanistically interesting. The optimized variants had reduced non-specific interaction scores and showed reduced off-target killing in functional assays. Monovalent affinity to B7-H3 was lower in the optimized variants — a standard cost of reducing hydrophobic surface character — but on-target killing of B7-H3-positive cells was preserved while the off-target signal dropped. Developability engineering and selectivity engineering were aligned here: the same moves that improved biophysical quality also cleaned up specificity.

The broader message is that computational developability scores — numbers the field has been generating primarily as a quality gate for manufacturing and clinical candidate selection — turn out to have direct functional relevance for a new delivery context. The scores predict real performance variation in primary human T cells under mRNA-LNP delivery, not just physical chemistry on a plate reader. For anyone building or optimizing a scFv for an mRNA-encoded CAR program, this paper establishes that the Sormanni-group metrics are worth running before the cell work, not after.

**Second item**, published July sixteenth in Oncogene from Zhejiang University. The paper is "Multiparatopic antibodies overcome tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance by inducing lysosomal degradation of EGFR mutants," from Lin and Liu as co-first authors, Huang Deli at Zhejiang as corresponding author. Full text also behind a paywall from this machine; the abstract is the basis here.

The target is EGFR in non-small cell lung cancer cells, specifically cells that have acquired resistance to osimertinib, the third-generation EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor. Osimertinib resistance commonly emerges through secondary mutations in the kinase domain, of which C797S is the canonical example. Those mutations sit in the cytosolic portion of the receptor, which tyrosine kinase inhibitors access directly. An antibody that binds the extracellular domain avoids the cytosolic resistance mutations entirely — but the challenge is that simple EGFR-blocking antibodies like cetuximab do not eliminate the receptor; they compete with E-G-F for binding or block ligand-induced dimerization, and the receptor can work around those mechanisms through bypass signaling, receptor amplification, or mutations elsewhere. What the paper proposes is to eliminate the receptor itself: engineer a multiparatopic antibody that physically removes EGFR from the cell surface by routing it to lysosomal degradation.

The design logic relies on an established but underexploited observation: a monomeric antibody binding one epitope on a cell-surface receptor slows recycling modestly, but the receptor predominantly returns to the surface through normal endosomal pathways. Receptor clustering — forced when a molecule with two or three binding arms crosslinks multiple receptor copies simultaneously — promotes endocytosis through dynamin-dependent pathways and shifts the endosomal fate toward lysosomal degradation rather than recycling. More receptor copies engaged per molecule, and more simultaneous clustering, shifts the balance further toward degradation.

The paper implements this by generating nanobodies against four distinct epitopes in the EGFR extracellular domain. Those nanobodies are then assembled into biparatopic formats — two nanobody binding units targeting two different epitopes per molecule — and triparatopic formats — three binding units, three different epitopes. The key variable is which combination of epitopes is engaged. The paper reports that the degradation outcome is epitope-dependent: not all biparatopic combinations produce equivalent receptor clearance. The triparatopic format, engaging three epitopes simultaneously, consistently outperforms biparatopic formats on EGFR degradation in N-S-C-L-C cell lines carrying diverse EGFR mutations including those resistant to osimertinib, and suppresses downstream signaling — E-R-K and A-K-T phosphorylation — through receptor elimination rather than kinase inhibition.

In xenograft models, triparatopic antibodies showed superior antitumor efficacy compared to both biparatopic formats and osimertinib alone, in both TKI-sensitive and TKI-resistant lines. Combination with osimertinib produced additive effects, consistent with a dual-mechanism interpretation: antibody removes receptor, inhibitor suppresses residual kinase activity in the fraction that escapes degradation.

The generalizability finding is the most conceptually transferable result. When multiparatopic antibodies targeting multiple P-D-L1 epitopes were applied, antibody-mediated crosslinking triggered lysosomal degradation of PD-L1 by the same mechanism. That means receptor degradation through multiparatopic clustering is not a quirk of EGFR biology — it is a consequence of sufficient epitope engagement creating a clustering geometry that overrides normal receptor recycling. The engineering implication is that any membrane protein where eliminating the receptor rather than blocking its function is the desired pharmacological outcome is a candidate for this approach, provided nanobodies targeting multiple non-overlapping epitopes can be obtained.

For the antibody engineering audience, the central design variable is epitope selection — not which single epitope maximizes affinity, but which combination of epitopes creates a clustering geometry that routes the receptor to degradation. Those are different optimization criteria that could select different antibodies. The nanobody format makes the triparatopic construction compact: three nanobodies fused in series with short linkers fit in a single polypeptide chain that is still smaller than a full IgG. Whether the format translates to Fc-containing formats for half-life extension without losing the clustering geometry is the natural follow-on engineering question.

**Third item**, shorter. Published July sixteenth in mAbs, from the Sanofi N-A-N-O-B-O-D-Y Research Platform in Ghent. The paper is "A N-A-N-O-B-O-D-Y molecule that blocks MerTK ectodomain cleavage in vitro and in vivo," corresponding author Duprez.

MerTK — Mer tyrosine kinase — is a member of the TAM receptor family, alongside Tyro3 and AXL. Its primary physiological role is in efferocytosis: the clearance of apoptotic cells by macrophages and dendritic cells. Efficient efferocytosis is anti-inflammatory — it removes dying cells before they become necrotic, prevents the inflammatory signal from intracellular damage-associated molecular patterns spilling out, and drives suppressive cytokine signaling in the phagocyte. MerTK function is required for efficient efferocytosis in several tissue contexts, and impaired efferocytosis contributes to pathology in inflammatory diseases.

One mechanism that limits MerTK function is ectodomain shedding: ADAM17, a metalloprotease, cleaves MerTK's extracellular domain from the cell surface. That generates two consequences. The shed ectodomain circulates as a soluble decoy receptor — called soluble Mer, or sMer — that binds Gas6 and Protein S, the natural MerTK ligands, and sequesters them away from membrane-bound MerTK. And the cell-surface stub left behind after shedding lacks the ligand-binding domain and cannot signal. Net effect: ADAM17-mediated shedding suppresses efferocytosis by reducing functional cell-surface MerTK and by decoy-trapping the ligands that would activate whatever receptor remains.

The therapeutic hypothesis is whether a nanobody that physically occludes ADAM17's access to the MerTK cleavage site can preserve MerTK surface expression without disrupting anything else the receptor normally does. The selectivity requirement is strict: do not compete with Gas6 binding (that would mimic shedding's decoy effect), do not activate MerTK kinase directly (that would be constitutive signaling), and do not inhibit ADAM17's enzymatic activity broadly (that causes on-target toxicity against the many other ADAM17 substrates, the same problem that derailed clinical development of pan-ADAM17 inhibitors).

The nanobody A0445046C08 achieves all three selectivity requirements. Surface plasmon resonance placed its dissociation constant for human MerTK below twenty-three picomolar — the number fell beneath the measurement floor of the instrument. In primary human macrophages stimulated with lipopolysaccharide, which activates endogenous ADAM17, the nanobody blocked MerTK cleavage measured as sMer in conditioned medium, with an I-C-50 of one-point-two to one-point-six nanomolar across donor preparations. Competitive binding assays confirmed it does not displace Gas6 from MerTK. Downstream kinase readouts confirmed it does not activate Erk1/2. And direct ADAM17 activity assays confirmed it does not inhibit the protease at the active site — the mechanism appears to be steric occlusion near the cleavage site in the MerTK juxtamembrane region.

A half-life-extended version, A044500050, was constructed by fusing one or two MerTK-binding nanobody units to a C-terminal albumin-binding nanobody, ALB23002, which borrows circulating albumin's long half-life to extend the molecule's pharmacokinetics. That version showed slightly higher I-C-50 values in macrophages — three-point-seven to five-point-five nanomolar — presumably a consequence of steric effects from the albumin-binding domain. In a humanized MerTK transgenic mouse model of Zymosan-induced sterile peritonitis, A044500050 reduced sMer levels in peritoneal lavage fluid in a dose-dependent manner at three and ten milligrams per kilogram, providing the first in vivo evidence that nanobody-mediated blockade of ectodomain shedding from the substrate side is pharmacologically feasible.

There is no machine learning or computational design in this paper — the nanobody came from conventional camelid immunization and phage display. What makes it worth flagging is the pharmacological strategy. Substrate-selective shedding inhibition — blocking ADAM17's access to one specific substrate from the substrate side rather than inhibiting the enzyme's active site — is a targeting specificity that small molecules cannot easily achieve and that conventional antibodies are geometrically poor at, because their CDR loops are flat while the cleavage-site-adjacent regions of membrane proteins are often narrow, recessed, or occluded. Nanobodies, with their convex paratopes and the long CDR3 loops that can penetrate tight geometric spaces, are structurally suited for exactly this kind of steric blockade. The design concept generalizes to any membrane protein where ectodomain shedding is a pathological loss-of-function mechanism: shed TNFR as a cytokine decoy in autoimmune disease, shed B-C-M-A limiting the available antigen for BCMA-targeted CAR-T in multiple myeloma, ErbB4 ectodomain shedding in various cancers. This paper is the first clean in vivo proof that the concept works with a single-domain antibody.
