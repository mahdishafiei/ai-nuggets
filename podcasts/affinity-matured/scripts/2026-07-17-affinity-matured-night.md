First item: PNAS, published July fourteenth, from the Wilson, Ward, and Schief groups at The Scripps Research Institute and IAVI. The title is "Structural basis of membrane engagement and polyreactivity control in H-I-V-1 M-P-E-R broadly neutralizing antibodies."

The problem the paper attacks has been sitting in the H-I-V vaccine field for years. The membrane-proximal external region — the M-P-E-R — of H-I-V's envelope glycoprotein E-N-V is one of the best-conserved and most accessible neutralization targets on the virus, and a handful of broadly neutralizing antibodies hit it with formidable breadth. DH511 neutralizes over ninety-nine percent of tested pseudoviruses. VRC42 and 4E10 have comparable breadth. The complication is that all of these antibodies physically contact viral membrane lipids as part of their recognition mechanism, and that lipid contact creates a tolerance problem — B cells that make antibodies touching self-membranes get culled or silenced early in development. The practical result is that decades of M-P-E-R immunogen design have produced antibodies that are polyreactive enough to trigger autoreactivity screening, and it has been hard to know which structural features are driving the problem and which features could be traded away to get a cleaner antibody while preserving the breadth.

The paper resolves this by determining crystal structures of three antibodies — DH511.1, its plasma-cell-derived variant DH511.12P, and VRC42.01 — in complex with an M-P-E-R peptide and phosphatidic acid, plus a cryo-E-M reconstruction of DH511.2 bound to membrane-embedded E-N-V. Combined with structures of earlier members of this class, that gives them a diverse enough panel to sort the antibodies into two mechanistic bins.

The first bin is groove-mediated binders. DH511 and 10E8 fall here. These antibodies do not make direct hydrogen bonds to lipid headgroups. Instead, they present a concave surface with positively charged residues positioned near the membrane interface, and the lipid tucks into a groove that is shaped by aromatic residues from CDR H3 and the light chain. DH511 specifically builds an aromatic cage, and it approaches the membrane at a different tilt angle than 10E8 — the two antibodies solve the same docking problem with different geometries. Within the DH511 lineage itself, the paper finds that the plasma-cell-derived variant DH511.12P carries tyrosine substitutions in CDR H3 that specifically tune the lipid coordination geometry toward the viral envelope lipid composition and away from host membrane lipids. That's differential cardiolipin polyreactivity tied to somatic mutation — the same affinity maturation process that sharpens antigen recognition is simultaneously trimming down the off-target lipid reactivity.

The second bin is heavy-chain-mediated binders. 4E10, PGZL1, and VRC42 fall here. These antibodies have positively charged patches at CDR H1 that make direct hydrogen bonds to lipid headgroups. 4E10 has the largest and strongest of those positive patches, and it has the highest polyreactivity. VRC42.01 solves the same binding mode with a weaker positive patch, which translates to lower polyreactivity. PGZL1 goes a step further — it introduces a negatively charged patch on CDR H3 that creates electrostatic repulsion against negatively charged lipid headgroups, providing a kind of built-in specificity filter that limits nonspecific membrane contacts even while the antibody is still using direct lipid recognition for the viral epitope.

The structural framework gives you a design grammar. If you want to reduce polyreactivity in a heavy-chain-mediated binder, the options the paper lays out are: reduce the size and charge density of the positive patch at CDR H1, or introduce an anionic counterbalance at CDR H3. If you are working with groove-mediated binders, the DH511 somatic mutation data suggests that CDR H3 tyrosine composition is a dial you can turn to tune lipid selectivity. For immunogen design, the authors sketch a sequential vaccination logic: prime with germline-targeting immunogens that clear the early autoreactivity checkpoint, then boost with membrane-anchored E-N-V that drives somatic hypermutation toward groove-mediated engagement. The idea is that groove-mediated contact is geometrically constrained by the epitope topology in a way that limits the antibody's access to the flat, unstructured membranes it would encounter on host cells, while still allowing engagement with the viral membrane in the narrow window where the M-P-E-R is exposed during fusion.

This paper has no machine learning component — it is crystallography and cryo-E-M and structure-based reasoning. But if you are designing M-P-E-R immunogens computationally, building in silico models of antibody-membrane interactions, or trying to understand the tolerance checkpoints that structurally constrain H-I-V vaccine design, the classification of groove-mediated versus heavy-chain-mediated lipid recognition is the kind of structural rule you can encode in a scoring function or use to annotate training data for polyreactivity prediction.

Second item: SweetFold, a preprint posted to bioRxiv yesterday from Keshav Sundar and Hyunjun Yang. A note before the summary — this was posted in the last twenty-four hours and bioRxiv has not yet rendered the full body text, so what follows is grounded in the abstract; if the benchmarks in the paper body change the picture, check back after the preprint renders.

The paper is a glycan-aware adaptation of Boltz-1x, the open-source all-atom biomolecular diffusion model. The motivation is structural: existing biomolecular diffusion models — AlphaFold3, Boltz, Chai — can handle proteins, nucleic acids, and small-molecule ligands, but glycans fall between the chairs. Glycan structure prediction is hard for a specific set of chemical reasons. Glycans branch rather than forming linear chains, so the topology is more complex than a protein backbone. The glycosidic linkage connecting each sugar unit has an anomeric configuration — alpha or beta — that controls the three-dimensional geometry of the junction, and if your model doesn't track that stereo state explicitly it will generate structures that are chemically implausible. And glycans are highly flexible — even a short oligosaccharide samples a broad conformational ensemble that linear-chain models aren't designed to capture.

SweetFold addresses this by representing glycans as pseudo-polymers rather than as generic ligands. In this representation each monosaccharide is a node, the glycosidic connectivity and anomeric state are preserved as edge attributes, and atom-level stereochemistry is encoded explicitly. The model pairs that representation with glycan-specific architecture changes, stereochemical supervision during training, and a sugar-centric training curriculum. The result, across monosaccharide, oligosaccharide, lectin-glycan complex, and glycoprotein benchmarks, is improved structural metrics relative to the Boltz-1x baseline, without regression on protein-only benchmarks.

The relevance to antibody engineering is real but indirect. Many of the most important antibody targets — influenza hemagglutinin, H-I-V envelope, coronavirus spike — are glycoproteins whose neutralization epitopes are either adjacent to glycan shields or occluded by them. Understanding the precise geometry of those glycan shields matters for epitope accessibility, for modeling how a broadly neutralizing antibody approaches a glycoprotein target, and for designing immunogens that present the correct glycoform. For groups using structural prediction in antibody-antigen docking pipelines, a model that handles the glycan layer of a target glycoprotein more accurately is directly useful. And for anyone studying antibody effector function — Fc glycosylation controls complement activation and F-C-gamma receptor engagement — better glycoprotein structure prediction is a background capability that propagates into multiple downstream applications.

Third and shortest item: a bioRxiv preprint posted yesterday, "Biological Continued Pretraining Reshapes the Capability Profile of a Foundation Model Without Catastrophic Forgetting." Single author, Wang, at an unspecified affiliation. The abstract is self-contained enough for a brief summary, but note it's abstract-only and the paper appears to report a reanalysis rather than primary training experiments.

The paper tests a common assumption in the protein and antibody language model field: if you take a large general-purpose language model and do continued pretraining on biological sequence data — D-N-A, protein sequences, biomedical text — you will inevitably trade away some of the model's general competence in exchange for biological capability. The standard worry is catastrophic forgetting. The paper argues this assumption is empirically wrong, at least for the model lineage it examines.

The system is Gemma-4-26B, a twenty-six billion parameter mixture-of-experts model, and the analysis compares three checkpoints from the same training lineage: the instruction-tuned base, the same base after biological continued pretraining on 8.7 billion tokens of DNA, protein sequence, and biomedical text, and a version that then received supervised fine-tuning on top of the biological continued-pretraining checkpoint. Across three independent capability axes — general knowledge and reasoning benchmarks, code generation, and biomedical knowledge assessment — biological continued pretraining does not degrade the model; it lifts it. M-M-L-U goes up thirteen points. Code generation on M-B-P-P nearly doubles, from a pass rate of zero-point-three-three to zero-point-six-three. BixBench, a biomedical knowledge assessment, improves sharply in discrimination, with the M-C-C going from zero-point-two-three to zero-point-nine-two. The one measured regression is TruthfulQA, dropping eight-point-eight points — the authors characterize this as interpretable domain drift rather than general degradation. And critically, the vocabulary expansion to handle biological tokens accounts for less than half a point on every general metric, so the capability gains are from continued pretraining, not from the tokenizer change.

The more interesting finding is what happens with supervised fine-tuning on top of that checkpoint: S-F-T narrows everything back down. All three capability axes fall to near-base levels. The interpretation the author offers is a clean division of labor — biological continued pretraining re-organizes and raises the shared capability substrate; supervised fine-tuning cashes it out onto specific target tasks at the cost of general breadth. The practical implication is that if you are building an antibody-specialized model by fine-tuning on antibody sequences or antibody engineering tasks, the order matters. Doing biological continued pretraining first and S-F-T second preserves the general-capability gains that continued pretraining buys you. Collapsing both into a single fine-tuning stage, or doing S-F-T before biological continued pretraining, may leave capability on the table. This is the kind of training-order result that rarely makes it into the main text of protein language model papers and is useful to know explicitly.

Stepping back: the three items tonight share a common theme of boundary conditions. The M-P-E-R paper is about the boundary between viral lipid recognition and host membrane autoreactivity, and how the geometry of CDR-lipid contact determines which side of that line an antibody lands on. SweetFold is about the boundary between what current biomolecular diffusion models handle well and where they fall apart, specifically at the protein-glycan interface. And the biological continued pretraining paper is about the boundary between continued pretraining and fine-tuning and how each reshapes a model's capability profile in opposite directions. All three are small papers, but all three are the kind of result that changes how you think about a design problem.
