Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. This is the evening edition for July fourteenth. The morning already carried five items — a domain-centric protein-language-model result, the Baker-lab kinase-modulator platform, the Beltrao AlphaFold-three audit, the ESM-3 knotted-protein topology study, and the asymmetric-transfer paper. Four fresh items tonight, and the lead one is a real milestone. In order: the first-in-human clinical trial of a monoclonal antibody optimised by artificial intelligence, from Generate Biomedicines, published today in the Journal of Infectious Diseases. Second, a Nature Computational Science paper from Columbia that finally makes rigorous something the fitness-prediction community has been muttering about for two years — that bigger protein language models are not always better at fitness prediction, and it explains exactly why. Third, PeptideCLM-two, a SMILES-based chemical language model for therapeutic peptides from Novo Nordisk and U-T Austin. And fourth, a structure-guided peptide-design pipeline called M-D-M-I that gets away with tiny training sets by front-loading structure into the featurisation.

Let's start with the AI-designed antibody trial, because it is the item that actually makes news outside of this show. The title is "Randomized, Double-Blind, Placebo-Controlled First-in-Human Trial of a First-in-Class AI-Designed Monoclonal Antibody G-B-0-6-6-9 Against the Conserved SARS-CoV-two Spike S-two Stem Helix," published July fourteenth in the Journal of Infectious Diseases by Borriello, Koh, and colleagues at Generate Biomedicines, with Snyder and De Alwis as the senior authors. Generate Biomedicines is one of the AI-first biotechs that has been running a generative-antibody-design platform in-house — this is the first time one of their molecules has completed a Phase-one study and been reported in a peer-reviewed venue.

The target choice is worth explaining, because it is not accidental. The original wave of clinical antibodies against SARS-CoV-two — the antibodies from Regeneron, Eli Lilly, AstraZeneca, and Vir — were all targeted at the spike receptor-binding domain, or R-B-D. That is the surface that engages A-C-E-two on host cells, so binding it blocks entry directly. The problem, which everyone learned in real time between 2020 and 2023, is that the R-B-D is also the surface subject to the strongest selective pressure — mutations there are selected by the humoral immune system in every recovered patient and every vaccinee. Omicron and its descendants accumulated enough R-B-D mutations that all four of those antibody products lost neutralisation activity and were withdrawn from clinical use.

The S-two stem helix is a different region entirely. S-two is the membrane-fusion subunit of spike — the part that drives the conformational rearrangement that fuses viral and host membranes after A-C-E-two engagement. The stem helix within S-two is highly conserved across sarbecoviruses, because its sequence is constrained by the folding and refolding it has to undergo during fusion. It is under limited selective pressure from natural antibody responses precisely because most people's antibodies don't target it — the R-B-D is more immunodominant. If you can design a high-affinity antibody against the stem helix, you get a molecule that is much more likely to remain active across variants, and that is exactly the pitch for G-B-0-6-6-9.

The A-I part. The paper does not, in the version I read, spell out the internal design pipeline in detail — Generate's platform is proprietary and the clinical paper focuses on P-K, P-D, and safety. But the framing is that G-B-0-6-6-9 was "optimised using artificial intelligence" — the substrate on which the optimisation ran is a stem-helix-targeting antibody scaffold, and the optimisation targets include affinity, developability, and a half-life-extending F-c modification. What the paper does deliver, and what makes it citable, is the clinical readout.

The clinical study. Fifty-one healthy adults aged eighteen to fifty-five, randomised across five ascending dose cohorts — one hundred, three hundred, six hundred, twelve hundred, and twenty-four hundred milligrams — with thirty-six subjects receiving G-B-0-6-6-9 and fifteen receiving placebo. Subjects were monitored for forty-three weeks. No dose-limiting toxicities. All adverse reactions were grade one or grade two — mild by clinical-trial classification. Pharmacokinetics were dose-proportional up to twenty-four hundred milligrams, and the half-life came in at fifty-four days. Fifty-four days is characteristic of an F-c with an L-S mutation or equivalent half-life-extension engineering, which is standard practice now but is on the F-c side and not really the A-I design contribution.

Pharmacodynamics were assessed by serum live-virus neutralisation — that is, drawing serum from dosed subjects at various time points and measuring in a live-virus neutralisation assay how much SARS-CoV-two the serum neutralises. Dose-dependent increases in serum neutralisation appeared at six hundred and twelve hundred milligrams, with clear separation from placebo. The authors also compute a "neutralising index" that combines the observed serum concentration with the in-vitro neutralising potency of the antibody, and they report that this index supports therapeutic efficacy for approximately two weeks post-administration at the higher doses. Combined with the fifty-four-day half-life, that supports moving forward with a twelve-hundred-milligram dose in a Phase-two study aimed at immunocompromised patients — the population that most benefits from monoclonal-antibody prophylaxis or treatment when their own humoral response is inadequate.

Two things to say about how to read this result, because they are worth being clear about. First, the paper does not, in the reported clinical data, definitively distinguish "A-I-designed" from "conventionally-engineered" — a Phase-one safety-and-P-K study is not designed to do that. What it does establish is that a molecule that came off an A-I design pipeline can go from bench to human safety trial without hitting a wall on the way, and that its behaviour in humans matches the expectations set by the pre-clinical work. That is not nothing. For years, the counter-argument against A-I-designed antibodies was "they will fail on developability or in-vivo P-K." That prediction is now falsified on at least one molecule.

Second, the important number will come in the Phase-two efficacy data, which is where "therapeutically useful against currently circulating variants" is actually demonstrable. The Phase-one data supports moving forward; it does not, on its own, establish clinical efficacy. But the setup — targeting a conserved region of spike, with a half-life-extended F-c and a moderately potent-looking neutralising activity — is the correct architecture for a molecule that is supposed to be variant-resilient, and the neutralisation-index-supported two-week window at the higher dose is roughly in line with what you would want for treatment of an acute infection.

The takeaway for the affinity-matured audience. This is the first published Phase-one trial of a monoclonal antibody that carries the "A-I-designed" label from a company that actually built its pipeline around that framing. Whatever the specific role of A-I in the design — Generate has said publicly it operates across sequence, structure, and developability in an integrated generative framework — the molecule went through a full I-N-D-enabling and Phase-one program without stumbling. It is one data point, not a phase change, but it is the data point the field has been waiting for since the mid-two-thousand-twenties talk-track about generative antibody design started.

Second item — the fitness-prediction scaling paper. Titled "Understanding language model scaling for protein fitness prediction," published in Nature Computational Science on July thirteenth by Chi Hou, Yufeng Shen, and colleagues at Columbia University Irving Medical Center, systems-biology department. This one is short in the abstract but the result is technically important and it should reshape how anyone using a protein language model for fitness scoring thinks about model choice.

The setup. Standard practice for zero-shot mutation-effect prediction is to take a protein language model — E-S-M-two, ProGen-two, Progen, ProtT-five, or a masked language model of your choice — and use the model's assigned sequence likelihood, p of sequence, as a proxy for fitness. If you mutate residue i from wildtype to mutant, the model's likelihood ratio between the mutant and wildtype gives you a fitness score. This works surprisingly well on benchmarks like ProteinGym, and it is the workhorse for a lot of deep-mutational-scanning-style predictions today.

The scaling puzzle. Deep-learning received wisdom is that bigger is better — the loss follows a power-law and larger models generalise more. For protein fitness prediction, empirically, this has not held. Performance on fitness benchmarks tends to peak at intermediate model size and then decline. The E-S-M-two 15-billion-parameter model, in many published comparisons, does not beat E-S-M-two 650-million or 3-billion for fitness prediction. This has been anomalous, and various post-hoc explanations have been floated. The Columbia paper gives a mechanistic account.

The mechanism, as they describe it. Model size, training-data size, and stochastic training elements can bias the predicted p of sequence away from real fitness. Model performance on fitness prediction depends on how well p of sequence matches evolutionary patterns in homologs. There is a sweet spot — a moderate range of predicted wildtype sequence likelihood — where p of sequence tracks evolutionary constraints closely enough to be useful. Outside that range, the mapping breaks down. At the low end of predicted likelihood, the model has essentially placed the wildtype in a low-probability region of its space and predicts uniformly low probabilities for all its mutations, so the ranking degenerates. At the high end, the model has placed the wildtype in a high-probability region and predicts uniformly high probabilities for all its mutations — again, the ranking degenerates because the differences among mutations are too compressed to be informative.

The scaling connection. Larger models tend to predict higher p of sequence for the same input, because they have modelled the natural-sequence distribution more sharply and confidently. That pushes many proteins out of the moderate-likelihood sweet spot and into the compressed high-end. That is the mechanism by which scale actively hurts fitness prediction — not that big models are worse in general, but that their sharper assignment of probability to observed sequences washes out the mutation-effect signal for the majority of proteins.

Practical implications for anyone using protein language models on antibodies. First, if you are doing zero-shot affinity-maturation ranking or humanisation scoring, do not assume that swapping in a bigger E-S-M-two variant will help. Check whether your wildtype's p of sequence under the model is in a reasonable middle range. Second, ensembling across model sizes may actually be useful because it hedges against the sweet-spot problem for individual proteins. Third, for antibody-specific fitness prediction, this argues for antibody-specific language models with training distributions that are calibrated to antibody sequence space — the same problem exists if a general-purpose model has never seen enough C-D-R-H-three diversity to place antibody wildtypes in the moderate-likelihood band. The paper is genuinely useful diagnostic guidance, not just an academic curiosity.

Third item — Peptide-C-L-M-two. Titled "Scaling SMILES-Based Chemical Language Models for Therapeutic Peptide Engineering," published July thirteenth in the Journal of Chemical Information and Modeling by Feller, Secor, Swanson, Wilke, and Deibler. First and last authors are affiliated with Novo Nordisk's Molecular A-I group; Wilke is at U-T Austin. This is a chemistry-language-model paper — smiles-based — aimed specifically at therapeutic peptides, which as a modality sit in an awkward gap between what protein language models handle well and what small-molecule chemical language models handle well.

The gap. Therapeutic peptides are increasingly interesting as a drug modality — glucagon-like peptide-one analogues, oxytocin analogues, cyclic peptides, macrocycles, peptidomimetics. They typically have high target specificity like proteins do, but they also often incorporate non-canonical amino acids, N-methylation, cyclisation, D-amino acids, or non-peptide moieties that break the natural-amino-acid alphabet that protein language models are trained on. Protein L-Ms — E-S-M, ProGen, and their kin — are restricted to the twenty canonical amino acids as tokens, or at best a modest augmentation. Chemical L-Ms trained on SMILES strings for small molecules can in principle handle any non-canonical modification, but they have historically been trained on small-molecule distributions and choke on the length and polymer-like structure of a fifteen-to-fifty-residue peptide. Neither model class has been the right tool.

The approach. The authors take the chemical-L-M direction and push it far enough that it can absorb peptide-scale sequences. They train PeptideCLM-two — a suite of chemical language models — on over one hundred million molecules, and specifically curate the training set to include peptide-like sequences. The natural representation is SMILES, so a peptide is encoded as a long SMILES string with all its modifications explicit — no restriction to canonical amino acids, no separation between backbone and side chain. The model learns a token distribution that can natively represent complex peptide chemistry, including modifications that would be invisible to a canonical-amino-acid protein L-M.

The benchmarks. The paper reports strong performance against prior methods on three concrete downstream tasks — membrane diffusion, biological function, and half-life prediction — all of which are actual downstream endpoints in peptide-drug development. Membrane diffusion matters for permeability and oral bioavailability; biological function is the direct assay readout; half-life determines dosing regimen. Beating both custom multi-embedding pipelines and static chemical descriptors on these three endpoints is a meaningful demonstration that the SMILES-native approach is now competitive.

Why this is worth watching for antibody engineers. Two reasons. First, the technology-transfer question — the same architectural approach of a SMILES-based chemistry L-M might be pushed further to encode full-length modified antibodies, or antibody-drug conjugates. A-D-Cs are exactly the modality that has been hardest to characterise computationally, because the small-molecule payload lives in chemistry-space while the antibody scaffold lives in protein-space, and no single model has handled both natively. A chemistry L-M with peptide-scale capacity is a step in the direction of a model that could handle A-D-C payload-linker-antibody triples end-to-end. Second, the developability-prediction task on peptides is a smaller-scale, faster-cycle proxy for the same problems that afflict antibody developability — solubility, self-aggregation, viscosity, thermal stability. Techniques that work well for peptide developability prediction, especially in low-data regimes, often transfer conceptually to antibody developability.

Fourth and final item, and the one that came out today on bioRxiv. Titled "Minimal Data, Maximal Insight — M-D-M-I — a Structure-guided Pipeline for Discovering Functional Alternatives in Peptide-Protein Interfaces," posted to bioRxiv on July fourteenth by Bayat, Perkins, and colleagues, with Keith Pardee at the University of Toronto as senior author. Body HTML isn't rendered yet — going off the abstract for specifics — but the pipeline architecture and the split-G-F-P results are clearly delineated.

The premise. Designing functional peptides for a protein target is a data-hungry problem. Machine-learning pipelines trained on sequence alone need thousands or tens of thousands of labelled examples to learn the sequence-to-function map for a given interface. Real projects — a synthesis chemist trying to find a functional analog of a natural peptide, a group with a small set of screened variants — typically have data at the tens-to-hundreds scale, not the tens-of-thousands scale. That regime has been out of reach for pure sequence learning.

The M-D-M-I approach. Two stages. Stage one integrates three-dimensional structural features derived from predicted peptide-protein complexes into a machine-learning model that captures interface geometry and binding energetics — that is, instead of learning from sequence alone, the model gets to see the actual predicted interface for each variant, complete with the geometric arrangement of side chains at the interface and per-residue energetic contributions. That featurisation is what allows small annotated datasets to be enough — most of the sequence-to-function signal is already extracted by AlphaFold-style structure prediction plus energy estimation, and the machine-learning model just has to learn a lightweight function on top. This is a defensible approach architecturally, and it is very similar in philosophy to what the Fleishman lab and others have been doing for antibody-repertoire design.

The pipeline pairs this structure-aware predictor with a genetic algorithm for exploring sequence space. Stage two takes successful stage-one variants and recombines them motif-by-motif to generate higher-divergence functional variants.

The benchmarks. On an all-negative benchmark panel — variants known to be non-functional — the structure-aware predictor reduced false positives from seventy percent, which is what a sequence-only baseline gave, to close to zero. That is a striking specificity gain. And in a general in-silico comparison, M-D-M-I produced approximately four times more high-confidence in-silico binders than state-of-the-art peptide-and-protein-design baselines the authors compared against.

The experimental testbed is worth explaining because it is elegant. They use the split-G-F-P system — the G-F-P protein is engineered to require two peptide fragments to complement into a functional fluorescent unit, so peptide-protein complementation directly yields a fluorescence signal you can read out. That gives them a very fast functional readout for hundreds or thousands of variants. In stage one, M-D-M-I produced peptides with up to thirty-eight percent sequence divergence from wildtype that still complemented and fluoresced. In stage two, motif-guided recombination pushed that to over fifty percent sequence divergence with retained function — and interestingly, the variants clustered into two distinct functional groups in sequence space, meaning the pipeline had found two different sequence solutions to the same functional constraint.

They also do a nice validation experiment. A top-performing peptide variant was expressed as a full-length G-F-P fusion — that is, the peptide is embedded into a full G-F-P sequence context — and the resulting fusion retained a G-F-P-like emission profile, supporting that the peptide is genuinely reconstituting a fluorescent-scaffold-like structure. That is a strong functional validation.

Transferable pattern for the audience. Antibody C-D-R redesign is exactly the same kind of task — small labelled dataset, need to generate diverse alternatives that retain binding to the same epitope. If you replace "predicted peptide-protein complex" with "predicted antibody-antigen complex" in the M-D-M-I framing, and swap the split-G-F-P readout for an antibody-affinity assay, you have a pipeline that would fit directly into antibody-engineering workflows where a hundred labelled variants is a big screen. The two-stage architecture — structure-informed predictor plus genetic algorithm, then motif-guided recombination — is worth borrowing.
