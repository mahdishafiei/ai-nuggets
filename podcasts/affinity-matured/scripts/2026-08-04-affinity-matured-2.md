All three sit on the design side rather than the structure-prediction side, which is where this morning's episode lived. First, Kukulu, from a UNC Charlotte and UNC Chapel Hill group under Colby Ford — a structure-aware Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture paired with conditional diffusion for CDR loop reconstruction inside antibody-antigen complexes, benchmarked across ten well-known therapeutic antibodies including MEDI-eighty-eight-fifty-two against influenza hemagglutinin. First item. Kukulu, spelled K-u-k-u-l-u, from Seth Rabinowitz, Prbhuv Nigam, Nicholas Santolla, and Colby Ford at UNC Charlotte and UNC Chapel Hill, with a company affiliation to Silico Biosciences. The framing is that antibody CDR design — especially heavy-chain CDR three — has to jointly respect the amino-acid identity and the three-dimensional loop geometry, and existing generative approaches split the field roughly two ways. Classical energy-based tools like Rosetta Antibody Design come from a hand-crafted energy landscape and expensive search, and they choke on the local flexibility of the loops. Sequence-only antibody language models — the p-Iggen or IgLM family — can propose new CDR sequences but without any explicit structural conditioning. Full-structure diffusion models trained on the PDB — the RF-diffusion family, and here they specifically compare against Boltz-Gen — do produce topologically valid backbones, but they generate everything at once and are not designed for local loop reconstruction against a fixed framework.

Kukulu's proposal is to insert something between those regimes — a JEPA-plus-diffusion setup restricted to the CDR loops. JEPA stands for Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture, which is the Yann-LeCun-associated self-supervised paradigm that predicts latent representations of masked regions from context rather than predicting the raw pixels or coordinates. The idea, transplanted here, is that you clean up an antibody-antigen PDB, trim to the Fv variable domain, identify the CDR loops by Chothia numbering, mask the CDR residues, and train a context encoder over the masked structure — represented in the atom thirty-seven format that AlphaFold-two popularized — and a transformer predictor that outputs latent representations of what the masked CDRs should look like. Then a diffusion head reconstructs the actual loop coordinates, atom presence, and residue identities under geometry-aware losses. The generation-time behavior is what makes this useful in practice — the model denoises only the masked CDR residues while preserving the framework and antigen context, then optionally rebuilds sidechains with local frame templates and runs an OpenMM AMBER relaxation.

Training numbers. Seven thousand nine hundred forty-three paired prepared-and-masked structures, roughly ninety percent train, ten percent validation. Fifty epochs, about eighty minutes per epoch on the hardware they used. The composite training loss is decomposed into seven components, and this is where the paper is honest about a real problem — the JEPA latent loss is thirty-six of the roughly thirty-nine total, and the geometry-oriented terms are one-tenth of that. In the discussion the authors flag this explicitly as loss-imbalance and say weight recalibration is future work. That is not incidental; it is telling you that the reconstructed backbones sometimes come out geometrically unnatural and the AMBER relaxation is doing more than polishing — it is fixing chain discontinuities and unphysical torsion angles at the framework-loop boundary. The paper acknowledges this.

Evaluation. Ten antibody-antigen complexes, chosen for coverage across therapeutic areas — pembrolizumab against PD-one, cetuximab against EGFR, trastuzumab against HER-two, bevacizumab against VEGF-A, adalimumab against TNF-alpha, omalizumab against IgE, CR-thirty-twenty-two against SARS-CoV-two spike, VRC-oh-one against HIV gp-one-twenty, MEDI-eighty-eight-fifty-two against influenza hemagglutinin, and palivizumab against RSV F protein. For each of the ten, Kukulu generated five hundred Fv sequences with full AMBER relaxation. All five thousand were scored with HADDOCK. The result — and this is where the paper's honest reporting is useful — is bimodal across antibodies. For MEDI-eighty-eight-fifty-two, the anti-influenza-HA-stem broadly neutralizing antibody, the mean HADDOCK score of the five hundred generated designs is minus three-seventy-four with a standard deviation of two-twenty-two, and the reference complex's HADDOCK score is minus five-seventy-four. So Kukulu-generated designs on average score worse than the reference, but the distribution has a substantial tail that beats the reference — the best design scored minus five-oh-four. Similar picture for VRC-oh-one against HIV gp-one-twenty. But for the oncology antibodies — cetuximab against EGFR, trastuzumab against HER-two — the mean generated score is positive, meaning the average design does not bind at all, and even the best designs rarely match the reference. The buried surface area of the successful designs also lands well below the reference for these cases.

Two interpretations, from an antibody engineering standpoint. First, Kukulu can produce novel CDR-H-three sequences that beat well-optimized therapeutic antibodies on HADDOCK score for some targets, particularly the infectious-disease broadly neutralizing antibodies. Second, the model has clearly not learned enough to consistently improve on antibodies against structured cell-surface receptors like HER-two and EGFR, where the interface geometry may impose harder constraints than the current JEPA-plus-diffusion representation captures. The paper is upfront that the loss imbalance and the resulting geometric artifacts are open work, and the ablation matrix that would separate the JEPA latent objective from the geometry regularizers has not yet been run. Code is at github.com/silicobio/kukulu, weights on Hugging Face, and there is a generate function you can call with a PDB path, an H-chain identifier, an L-chain identifier, and a set of antigen chains. Worth trying, worth watching for the ablation follow-up.

Second item. Humanized Anti-PD-one Antibodies Generated Using The Conditional Kernel-Elastic Autoencoder, from Yuanjun Shi, Haote Li, Pulan Liu, Christopher Bunick, Shaogeng Tang, Jimin Wang, and Victor Batista at Yale — Chemistry, Molecular Biophysics and Biochemistry, Dermatology, and the Wu Tsai Institute. The framing is a very old problem — nivolumab and pembrolizumab, both marketed anti-PD-one antibodies, both binding the same PD-one FG-loop hotspot, but from orientations that differ by one hundred seventy-four degrees — essentially antiparallel binding modes on the same epitope patch. Nivolumab has a binding affinity of about four nanomolar; pembrolizumab, about twenty-seven picomolar. Both are clinical successes. The design question is whether you can generate new antibodies that live in the interpolation between these two — sharing their epitope specificity, keeping their favorable therapeutic properties — the "antibody medicine-likeness" of the paper's language — but with tunable potency.

The model is a Conditional Kernel-Elastic Autoencoder, or C-K-E-A. Autoencoder in the usual sense — an encoder maps antibody sequences into a latent space, a decoder maps latent points back to sequences. The kernel-elastic part means the latent-space geometry is regularized under a kernel prior that keeps chemically-similar sequences close in latent space, and the elastic part uses a regularizer that limits how far latent codes can drift under decoding. Conditional means the generation process is conditioned on a target medicine-likeness score, which is a scalar the paper defines and calibrates on the two seed antibodies — nivolumab at negative five-point-five-two, pembrolizumab at negative one-point-five-two, chosen so that better therapeutic properties correspond to higher score. The generative process is set to target a medicine-likeness score of ten, well outside the interpolation between the two parent scores, and then six antibodies are sampled by linear interpolation in latent space between the two parents. Call them P-two-N-one through P-two-N-six, where P-two-N-one is closest in latent space to pembrolizumab, and P-two-N-six is closest to nivolumab.

The evaluation stack is where this paper does more than a typical latent-space-generation paper. First, each of the six generated antibodies is put into a docked PD-one antibody complex, solvated in explicit water, and simulated in NAMD for four hundred nanoseconds at three-hundred-ten Kelvin using hydrogen mass repartitioning and a four-femtosecond time step. That is a substantial simulation. From each trajectory, they compute an electron density map and an electrostatic potential map by summing over frames — a technique borrowed from crystallography-in-silico workflows — and fit the equilibrium structures against the MD-derived density. Complex stability is judged by the quality of the electron density map at the antibody–PD-one interface. Then all six are actually expressed in cell culture, purified, and tested for PD-one binding by biolayer interferometry.

Results. Five of the six designed antibodies had desirable medicine-likeness properties. One — P-two-N-one, the pembrolizumab-adjacent design — showed a favorable complex in the MD but failed to purify at all. Three of the remaining five bound PD-one experimentally, with saturation curves reaching asymptotic values in about two minutes, which is on the same time scale as the natural PD-one-PD-L-one interaction. The signals were relatively weak, so no tight kinetic or thermodynamic constants, but the binding is real. And the MD-derived stability ranking is worth noting — P-two-N-six, the nivolumab-adjacent design, produced a higher-quality electron density map at the interface than nivolumab itself, and comfortably above the third binder P-two-N-five.

The take-home for the field is not that Yale designed a drop-in nivolumab replacement — three-out-of-six with weak binding is a proof of concept, not a lead. The take-home is that when two well-characterized therapeutic antibodies bind the same epitope from opposite orientations, the latent-space between them is not empty — it contains functional antibodies that adopt one of the two orientations and bind, and the MD-guided ranking correctly identifies which. That is a template for antibody humanization workflows where you have a parent antibody with a known epitope and want to explore the local neighborhood in a chemically-informed way. The scale of MD used here — four hundred nanoseconds per candidate, six candidates — is not free, but it is entirely tractable for lead optimization. The paper does not release code, and the CKEA architecture details are in the supplementary, so it is not immediately reproducible, but the workflow — CKEA sample plus MD refinement plus expression — is generalizable.

Third item. Directed Evolution in Codon Space, from James Heuschkel, Laura Kingsley, Jon Reed, Di Li, Matthew Warner, Noah Pefaur, and Steven Cramer, split between Boehringer Ingelheim's Biotherapeutics Discovery group and the Cramer lab at Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute. The tool is SynCodonLM, and the framing is a nice conceptual reframe of an old industrial problem. Recombinant expression of therapeutic antibodies in CHO cells is a manufacturing bottleneck — small gains in titer translate directly to lower cost per gram at commercial scale. Codon optimization has been used for decades to boost expression, and there are established methods — host codon-usage tables, mRNA folding avoidance, translation-kinetics heuristics — but every industry pipeline that ships a clinical antibody has already run its coding sequence through those tools. The received wisdom has been that these production-optimized sequences are at a local codon-space optimum, and further tinkering will do more harm than good.

SynCodonLM asks whether that is actually true. The reframe is to treat codon optimization not as a one-shot design problem but as directed evolution — the same iterative-local-search paradigm that protein engineering uses in amino-acid space, transplanted to synonymous codon substitutions in D-N-A space. At each step, SynCodonLM — a codon-level language model trained on natural coding sequences — proposes the synonymous substitution that most increases the model's likelihood of the resulting sequence. That substitution is applied, the sequence updated, and the process repeated. The fitness proxy is model likelihood — the paper calls it the Evo score — which is a proxy justified by prior work showing that language-model likelihoods correlate with sequence fitness in protein engineering.

Before the antibody expression experiments, the authors validate that the Evo score encodes evolutionarily-meaningful signal. They test on temporal series of influenza A H-one-N-one nucleoprotein, hemagglutinin, and neuraminidase — sampling year against SynCodonLM Evo score. For all three proteins, Evo score increases significantly with sampling year, correlation r around zero-point-four to zero-point-five with statistical significance. Nucleoprotein shows the most pronounced shift, which the authors note aligns with historically documented influenza evolution. Critically, when they compute protein-level pseudo-log-likelihoods on the matched translated sequences using a protein language model as a control, the trend flips sign — protein-level likelihood decreases over time, r equals minus zero-point-three-two. That inversion is the paper's most important control. It rules out the simple explanation that both the codon-level model and any protein-level model would trend the same direction with time. Codon space carries evolutionary signal that amino-acid space does not, at least for this class of rapidly-evolving virus.

The main event is the antibody expression benchmark. Twenty-four clinical-stage antibody-based therapeutics from Boehringer Ingelheim's pipeline, including full-length antibodies and antibody-based fusion proteins — melredableukin alfa, an IgG-one kappa scaffold fused to an IL cargo, and vislarafusp alfa, an anti-HER-two IgG-one kappa with a SIRP-alpha D-one domain appended. The isotype breakdown is IgG-one heavy chains dominating, fifteen molecules, and IgG-four for eight of them. Each therapeutic's disclosed patented coding sequence is treated as a high-fitness parental baseline — that is the key setup — and iteratively refined by SynCodonLM with all substitutions constrained to synonymous ones so the encoded protein is preserved exactly.

Results. Across twenty-four molecules, eighteen showed a statistically significant increase in CHO cell secreted titer under the refined coding sequence versus the parental — a seventy-five percent responder rate. Sixteen of those eighteen were individually significant at paired t-test p less than zero-point-oh-five. The panel-wide mean expression increase was about one-point-two-six-fold, at paired t-test p less than zero-point-oh-oh-one and Wilcoxon confirmed. That is not a moon-shot number, but at commercial scale a twenty-six-percent titer increase on a monoclonal antibody at fed-batch fifty-thousand-liter is material.

Two important controls. First, all twenty-four constructs — including the six negative responders — showed positive delta-Evo values. In other words, the iterative refinement did move all sequences toward higher codon-language-model likelihood in every case, so the six failures are not iteration failures — they are cases where the higher-likelihood sequence happened not to translate to higher CHO titer. That distinction matters because it separates model behavior from biological outcome. Second, the correlation between the model's delta-Evo score and the observed log-two-fold change in titer is r equals zero-point-four-four, p equals zero-point-oh-three-three, across all twenty-four constructs. So model likelihood is a real, if noisy, predictor of expression gain — better than any of the six mRNA-structure or codon-usage heuristics they benchmarked against, including well-known Kudla five-prime folding descriptors, which they show do not transfer from bacterial expression to CHO. And crucially, product quality was preserved — the paper reports paired violin plots of biophysical attributes for parental versus refined, and the distributions overlap essentially completely, so the titer gain is not paid for in aggregation, glycosylation shift, or other measurable quality metrics.

The scientific claim, and it is a strong one, is that even production-optimized coding sequences for clinical-stage antibodies retain accessible fitness in synonymous codon space that the industry standard has been leaving on the table. The tool, SynCodonLM, treats codon optimization as an iterative process rather than a one-shot design step, and does so without an explicit expression objective — the fitness signal is entirely evolutionary. For antibody engineering specifically, this is a workflow that plugs in downstream of amino-acid design and improves manufacturability without touching the molecule you spent a decade optimizing.
