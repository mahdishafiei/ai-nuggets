## Script

Welcome back to Affinity Matured, your daily briefing on machine learning for antibody and protein engineering. This is the afternoon edition for July fifteenth. Four items to walk through, and the thread today is antibody escape and de novo binder generalization — how well the antibodies and designed proteins we have hold up against the specific tests we care about. Two of the items come from the Bloom lab at Fred Hutchinson, both revised on bioRxiv yesterday with substantive additions. Then a physics-based generative pipeline for binding very small ligands, from the Longxing Cao group at Westlake University. And finally a topology-guided study using a protein language model to design knotted proteins, which is a nice probe of what these representations actually encode.

Let's start with the influenza work, because it is the most directly on-brief piece today for anyone doing anti-influenza antibody engineering.

The paper is titled "Near real-time data on the human neutralizing antibody landscape to influenza virus as of early 2026 to inform vaccine-strain selection," and it is the current bioRxiv version of the Bloom-lab pipeline for turning sequencing-based neutralization assays into an operational feed for World Health Organization vaccine-composition meetings. Lead author Caroline Kikawa; corresponding author Jesse Bloom. The context is the February vaccine-strain selection decision for the twenty-twenty-six to twenty-twenty-seven Northern Hemisphere season. Between the September twenty-twenty-five meeting that picked the Southern Hemisphere strain and the February meeting, two new antigenic daughter subclades became dominant: subclade K within H-three-N-two, which is derived from J-point-two-point-four with hemagglutinin mutations at positions two, one-forty-four, one-fifty-eight, one-sixty, one-seventy-three, and three-twenty-eight; and subclade D-point-three-point-one-point-one within H-one-N-one, which adds four mutations relative to its parent D-point-three-point-one. The vaccine-strain decision that is about to be made is downstream of the antigenic question of how much these new subclades escape existing human immunity. That is the question this paper measures.

The methodology is a barcoded, pseudovirus, deep sequencing-based neutralization assay that lets you score many viral variants against many sera in one experiment. The library contains fifty-three recently circulating H-three-N-two strains and thirty recently circulating H-one-N-one strains, plus eight past vaccine strains for anchoring — that is ninety-one distinct hemagglutinins, most represented with two or three barcodes for internal replicate structure, giving one hundred and eighty-six unique barcoded variants. The sera panel is three hundred and two individuals aged zero to a hundred and three, from five geographic sites, most collected in October and November of twenty-twenty-five. Fifty-nine of the Pennsylvania donors have paired pre- and post-vaccination sera for the current trivalent egg-based Flulaval. The full data matrix is twenty-seven thousand four hundred and nine titers.

The headline finding is that median titers across all sera differ by more than three-fold across current H-three-N-two strains, and the lowest titers are concentrated in subclade K. Aggregate titers to subclade K are significantly lower than to every other current H-three-N-two subclade except J-point-two-point-four, which subclade K is derived from. But the interesting part is the subvariant structure inside subclade K. The canonical subclade K carries many mutations in antigenic regions A and B, but the subvariants where median titers drop further are the ones that add mutations in antigenic regions D and E — specifically at sites ninety-six, two-hundred-seven, and two-twenty-three in region D, and site two-sixty-one in region E. These are not the sites that defined the K subclade in the first place; they are new. And they are the ones that could plausibly rise in frequency over the coming year, because they are what erodes titers further from an already-low baseline.

For H-one-N-one, the story is smaller in magnitude but similar in structure: median titers to subclade D-point-three-point-one-point-one strains are lower than to the current Northern Hemisphere vaccine strain, and lower than to the parent D-point-three-point-one subclade that was chosen for the twenty-twenty-six Southern Hemisphere. And there is a striking age-stratified pattern that does not show up in the median-across-sera aggregate: adolescents and young adults have much lower titers to H-one-N-one strains carrying mutations at sites one-fifty-five or one-fifty-seven than other age groups. This kind of per-serum, per-subvariant heterogeneity is exactly what the sequencing-based assay was built to expose — hemagglutination-inhibition on ferret sera would flatten it.

The vaccination arm is worth reporting because it is where the antibody-engineering audience should feel the tension. Post-vaccination titer increases across recent H-three-N-two strains are modest, roughly one-point-five to two-point-five-fold, and the increase is smaller for the newest subclades — K, J-point-two-point-four, and J-point-two-point-three. For subclade K strains with additional mutations in antigenic region D, the post-vaccination titer increase drops to about one-point-five-fold. In other words, the current vaccine is generating antibodies that are less effective at boosting neutralization of exactly the strains that are becoming dominant. That is the concrete case for updating the strain, and it is the kind of measurement that a broadly neutralizing anti-flu antibody pipeline should be reading directly rather than getting through the summary tables of a strain-selection meeting.

For antibody-engineering practice, there are two useful takeaways beyond the vaccine decision itself. First, the data are public and Nextstrain-linked with real-time updates — if you are engineering an anti-hemagglutinin antibody, especially one aiming for breadth across current human H-three-N-two, subclade K and its region-D-and-E subvariants are the concrete escape landscape you should be testing against, not last year's panel. Second, the assay architecture — a barcoded pseudovirus library scanned against many sera — is a reference implementation for the kind of experiment that produces a large, machine-learning-ready titer matrix. If you are training an antibody-antigen affinity model against influenza, this is closer to the ground-truth input distribution than anything from ferret hemagglutination-inhibition assays.

Item two is the same lab, same technique family, applied to a different virus and a different problem — antibody escape mapping for the licensed R-S-V monoclonals. Paper title: "Complete definition of how antibodies used to prevent R-S-V are affected by F mutations." Lead author Cassandra Simonich; corresponding author Jesse Bloom again. Version two, posted yesterday, adds a substantial amount of experimental content over version one, including deep mutational scanning for two additional candidate antibodies — one-A-two and one-B-six — and new deep mutational scanning against five human polyclonal sera, plus expanded validation neutralization assays.

The setup is that two anti-fusion-protein monoclonals — nirsevimab, which targets the apex antigenic region called region zero, and clesrovimab, which targets the lateral-face antigenic region four — are now the standard of care for infant R-S-V prophylaxis. Between them they have substantially reduced infant hospitalizations. But sporadic nirsevimab-resistance strains have already been reported in breakthrough infections, and past experience with the failed Regeneron antibody suptavumab shows how quickly a single naturally arising escape mutation can end a program.

There are two conceptual pieces in this paper worth calling out for the antibody-engineering audience. The first is a biophysical model of why nirsevimab escape mutations are more common and more impactful in R-S-V subtype B than in subtype A, even though nirsevimab's binding footprint on the fusion protein is nearly identical between the two subtypes. The answer is Fab affinity. When the monovalent Fab has very high affinity for the antigen, bivalent I-g-G binding buffers point mutations that reduce Fab affinity — because with two arms binding, even a mutant virus stays mostly antibody-bound. When Fab affinity is only moderate, that buffering vanishes, and the same mutation now shows up as reduced I-g-G neutralization. Nirsevimab has higher Fab affinity for the R-S-V-A fusion protein than for R-S-V-B, so mutations that would look silent in the A background produce measurable escape in the B background. The authors validate this quantitatively — they show that two known subtype-B nirsevimab escape mutations, K-sixty-eight-Q and asparagine-two-oh-one-S, reduce Fab neutralization in both A and B, but only reduce I-g-G neutralization in B. That is a mechanistically clean result and it has practical consequences: if you want an escape landscape that generalizes across subtypes and across genetic backgrounds, you should measure with the Fab and not just the I-g-G, because the I-g-G measurement is distorted by exactly the bivalency buffering that varies with baseline affinity.

The second piece is the deep mutational scanning itself. The authors use a pseudovirus system on the R-S-V Long strain fusion protein — pseudoviruses that carry no viral genes other than F and undergo only a single round of cell entry, so this is done at biosafety level two rather than requiring authentic R-S-V handling. They construct two independent libraries covering nearly every amino acid substitution across the fusion-protein ectodomain, measure cell-entry fitness for every mutant, and then measure neutralization by seven different antibodies — nirsevimab, clesrovimab, suptavumab, R-S-M-oh-one, one-A-two, and one-B-six — in both I-g-G and Fab forms, plus five human polyclonal sera. For clesrovimab specifically, the scan identifies escape mutations at sites four-hundred-twenty-six, four-hundred-twenty-nine, four-hundred-thirty-three, and four-hundred-seventy that were not previously reported as clesrovimab-resistance mutations. For nirsevimab, the scan confirms the known region-zero escape sites but adds several new resistance-conferring substitutions that are functionally tolerated by the fusion protein. And they show that natural R-S-V sequences with high summed escape scores from the scan really do exhibit reduced neutralization by the corresponding antibody — the escape score is predictive.

The polyclonal-sera arm is important for calibration. When they run the same deep mutational scan against five human sera, no single mutation causes more than a few-fold reduction in serum neutralization — nothing like the sharp escape you see against individual monoclonals. The sites that do modestly affect serum neutralization cluster at the fusion-protein apex, near the nirsevimab epitope. That is consistent with prior evidence that a substantial fraction of human memory-B-cell-encoded anti-fusion-protein antibodies target the apex.

For antibody engineers, the takeaway is that the paper effectively becomes a reference dataset for R-S-V-antibody escape prediction. Every point in this matrix is a labeled data point of the form "F protein carrying mutation X reduces neutralization by antibody Y by amount Z, in Fab and I-g-G form." That is exactly the training signal that antibody-antigen affinity models want. And the Nextstrain integration — where phylogenetic trees are colored by summed escape scores in real time — is a template for how a fielded escape-prediction model should surface its outputs.

Item three moves from natural antibodies to de novo binders, and specifically to the difficult case of very small polar ligands. The paper is titled "De novo design of ligand binding and sensing with a physics based generative approach," from the Longxing Cao group at Westlake University. Cao is well known for physics-based binder design work carried out of the Baker lab, and this paper is a Westlake-Cao continuation of that program.

The problem statement is that de novo protein binder design has moved past the easy targets. Large hydrophobic ligands are relatively easy — they present many atoms and permit many high-quality contacts. Very small polar molecules are the opposite — few atoms, so success requires nearly exceptional shape and chemical complementarity. Neurotransmitters like serotonin and dopamine are canonical hard cases: dopamine has a molecular weight of one hundred and fifty-three daltons, four heavy-atom polar contacts, and no significant hydrophobic surface for a binder to grip.

The pipeline is a physics-based generative approach called ProBuilder that simultaneously builds a protein backbone and optimizes the ligand-binding pocket, rather than the older workflow of building a huge scaffold library first and docking ligands into pockets afterwards. The core primitive is a rotamer interaction field, or R-I-F — an enumeration of energetically favorable side-chain-to-ligand contacts that yields both interaction-energy estimates and a map of critical contact residues. Backbone construction is anchored at those R-I-F contact residues, with the rest of the fold built outward via Monte Carlo fragment assembly. At each step the score function is a joint objective: a folding-quality term computed from a residue-pair transform score, and a ligand-binding term computed from the R-I-F. Metal-ion binding is handled with a coordination-residue R-I-F that captures the geometric preferences — tetrahedral for zinc with histidines and aspartates or glutamates, octahedral for nickel. The final sequences are optimized with Rosetta and ProteinMPNN, and design filtering includes AlphaFold2 self-consistency.

The experimental scorecard is worth reading carefully. For dolutegravir, ninety-six designs were tested and fourteen bound; affinity maturation via a helix-extension strategy pushed the best design from four-point-nine micromolar down to nine hundred and seventy-one nanomolar. For cianidanol, twenty of ninety-four designs bound. For the fluorogenic dye D-F-H-B-I, two of sixteen designs produced clear fluorescence increases on binding. For serotonin — one hundred and seventy-six daltons — six of thirty-eight designs bound; sequence optimization on one design got the affinity from an initial seventy-three micromolar down to thirty micromolar. For dopamine, one of thirty-three designs bound at seventy-five micromolar. The dopamine result is the outlier — a single binder — which is worth being honest about: the smaller and more polar the ligand, the sharper the success-rate cliff. For zinc and nickel, the design ZK-two binds zinc with a dissociation constant of one hundred and fifty nanomolar and no detectable binding to copper, cobalt, or nickel — which is the interesting selectivity claim, because the Irving-Williams series would predict copper wins over zinc.

Then they turn the binders into sensors. Serotonin- and dopamine-binders are split between two helix bundles and each half fused to the termini of a circularly permuted super-folder G-F-P, so that ligand-induced re-association drives a fluorescence change. The final serotonin sensor construct — wc-SRO-thirty-point-one — has a delta-F over F of three hundred and forty percent, with a half-maximal effective concentration of five hundred and forty micromolar. That is slightly weaker in dynamic range than the established iSeroSnFR sensor (nine hundred and thirty percent) but with a lower effective concentration and a sensing module less than half the size — one hundred and twenty-nine amino acids versus two hundred and seventy-two. The dopamine sensor is the first reported soluble de novo dopamine biosensor, at delta-F over F of thirty-eight percent — early-stage performance but a proof of concept in a target that has historically resisted de novo design. The zinc sensor uses a completely different transduction mechanism: split-nanoluciferase reconstitution triggered by zinc-induced protein folding, giving half-maximal effective concentrations around seventy-three nanomolar with strong selectivity against copper, calcium, magnesium, and cobalt.

Crystal structures matter here because de novo work needs structural validation. The serotonin-bound SRO-thirty design was solved at two-point-five-eight angstroms with a C-alpha R-M-S-D to design of zero-point-nine-seven angstroms. Dopamine-bound DPA-nine solved at two-point-four-seven angstroms with C-alpha R-M-S-D of zero-point-seven-nine. Zinc-bound ZK-two solved at one-point-two-three angstroms with C-alpha R-M-S-D of zero-point-four-five. So the atomic-level design accuracy is being validated end-to-end.

For antibody engineers, this is a peripheral but instructive item — it is not antibody design. The lesson to carry is that a physics-based generative pipeline that anchors backbone construction to a pre-enumerated interaction map is producing usable binders for exactly the small-ligand regime where deep-learning-only diffusion methods struggle. For CDR-loop design against very small epitopic features — small linear peptides, glycans, or metal-ion coordination sites — the pattern of anchor-then-fold matters as a design primitive, whether the actual implementation is R-I-F-based or its neural equivalent.

Item four is a short and technically focused paper on knotted proteins as a probe of what protein language models actually learn. Title: "Advancing Knotted Protein Design with E-S-M-three: Guided Generation and Topological Insights." Version two, posted yesterday. Authors are at C-E-I-T-E-C, Masaryk University. This is not an antibody paper, but it addresses a question that comes up whenever anyone asks how much global structural information sits inside a protein language model's embeddings: can you steer generation toward a rare, non-local topological feature that the model has never been explicitly trained to produce? And when you steer, is the topology encoded robustly, or is it a lucky sample?

Knotted proteins are the test case because they are rare in the training distribution — natural knotted proteins are unusual — and because their identifying feature is unambiguously non-local: a knot is defined by whether the backbone chain, when its ends are closed by stochastic projection, forms a nontrivial topological invariant. The tools they use are E-S-M-three at the small-model size, one-point-four billion parameters, in half-precision on A-ten-G G-P-Us, and Topoly, which computes Alexander polynomials over stochastic-closure projections and returns a probability of knottedness.

The main methodological move is derivative-free guided decoding. E-S-M-three is a masked language model that iteratively unmasks partially specified sequences. At each unmasking step, several candidate completions are sampled, and each is scored against a user-defined objective — in this case, the knot score computed by running the completion through structure prediction and Topoly. The highest-scoring candidate is retained and decoding continues. The result: guided E-S-M-three generation produces outputs classified as knotted at an eighty-nine percent success rate — the ninety-five-percent confidence interval is eighty-one to ninety-four percent — compared to the roughly zero-point-five percent success rate previously reported for unguided diffusion-based approaches. That is a two-order-of-magnitude improvement, driven by the ability to close the guidance loop with a structural evaluator.

The confidence analysis is worth the caveat. Generated knots have mean predicted-L-D-D-T of zero-point-three-two and mean predicted-T-M of zero-point-two-one, versus zero-point-nine-o and zero-point-eight-four respectively for real knotted proteins evaluated under the same pipeline. In other words, the model can be steered to produce structures that Topoly calls knotted, but the model itself is not very confident these structures actually fold that way. So these should be treated as topology-enriched samples pending independent validation, not validated designs. That honest framing is exactly the right one for the field.

The robustness result is where the paper gets interesting for representation-learning questions. If you take real knotted proteins and mask a fraction of their residues, then let E-S-M-three regenerate the masked positions, how much sequence can you destroy before the knot topology breaks? On average, eighty-four percent of the sequence has to be altered before the knot is lost, and the loss follows a sharp threshold rather than a gradual degradation. Even more striking: structural drift — measured as C-alpha R-M-S-D between the pre-masking and post-regeneration predicted structures — accumulates well before topology breaks. At fifty percent masking, R-M-S-D is already three-point-two angstroms and sequence identity is down to seventy-three percent, but knot probability is still eighty-five percent. This decoupling means the topological feature is encoded in a distributed manner across the sequence, not tied to specific three-dimensional coordinates.

For antibody-language-model practitioners, the transferable lesson is about representation depth. If a general protein language model can encode a global topological feature — that is, a property whose definition requires reasoning about the whole chain — well enough to be extracted by a linear probe, then features that are conceptually similar but antibody-specific are plausibly there too: repertoire structure, canonical C-D-R-L-three canonical class, framework-versus-loop segmentation, and so on. And the guided-decoding trick — where a task-specific evaluator scores candidate unmaskings at each step — is a lightweight way to steer generation toward rare properties without any fine-tuning. That is a design pattern worth borrowing for antibody-specific goals like enforcing paratope geometry or targeting a specific epitope-facing residue.
