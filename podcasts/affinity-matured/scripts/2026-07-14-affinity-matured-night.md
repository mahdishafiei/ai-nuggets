First item. The paper is titled "A geometric atlas of how E-S-M-three organizes modalities across depth," posted to bioRxiv on July twelfth by Jacob Steenwyk, supported by the Life Sciences Research Foundation.

To understand what the paper is asking, some context. E-S-M-three — the smallest open-source variant is one-point-four billion parameters across forty-eight transformer layers — is a multimodal protein language model. Unlike E-S-M-two, which takes only amino-acid sequence as input, E-S-M-three simultaneously ingests five input channels: amino-acid sequence, three-dimensional structure, secondary structure labels (the eight-state S-S-eight encoding), solvent accessibility — S-A-S-A — and discrete functional annotations. All five get converted to token embeddings that are summed together into a single shared residual stream from the very first layer. The fundamental architectural question that nobody had addressed experimentally is: what happens to those five representations as they pass through forty-eight layers? Do they stay separate? When do they converge? Do they all converge equally?

The analysis uses representational-similarity analysis — sometimes called R-S-A — applied once per modality in isolation at every one of the forty-eight layers. Representational-similarity analysis compares how similar the internal representations are across conditions: if two modalities produce similar internal structures at a given layer, they're said to be fused; if they remain dissimilar, they're occupying separate subspaces. Steenwyk runs this across five-thousand-five-hundred-fifty-five proteins from twelve organisms spanning bacteria, archaea, and eukaryotes.

The results fall into two clean groups, which is the core finding. The four physical modalities — sequence, structure, S-S-eight, and S-A-S-A — begin in distinct subspaces. They stay maximally separated through roughly the first twenty-five layers. Then between layers twenty-five and thirty-five, they fuse into a shared low-dimensional subspace. But the fusion is not simultaneous. Structure, S-S-eight, and S-A-S-A — the three modalities that are all derived from three-dimensional coordinates — are mutually aligned with each other from the very first layer. They were always similar to each other; what happens in the twenty-five-to-thirty-five window is that they become similar to the rest. Sequence is the outlier. Sequence doesn't join the fused space until after layer twenty-eight, meaning structure-derived representations fuse among themselves first, and sequence arrives late.

The fifth modality — the functional annotation channel — never fuses at all. It remains representationally orthogonal to the physical modalities at every one of the forty-eight layers, and this orthogonality holds whether the annotation is supplied as a whole-protein label or as per-residue labels. The authors interpret this as content-driven rather than tokenization-driven: it's not an artifact of how annotation tokens are formatted, it's that the model has learned to keep functional annotation informationally separate from the physical-property stream. A randomly-initialized model of the same architecture shows no such fusion, confirming this is a learned property of training, not a structural artifact.

Two additional results. First, the fusion reorganizes variance in a specific way: between-condition variance converts to within-condition variance as the modalities merge, but the residual stream never approaches isotropy — it retains organized structure throughout. Second, fusion depth is independent of protein length but is delayed by structural disorder. In intrinsically disordered regions, modality fusion happens deeper in the network, not at layer twenty-five but later.

Why this matters for the antibody-engineering audience. Several practical implications. For fine-tuning E-S-M-three on antibody-specific data, the fusion window between layers twenty-five and thirty-five is the transition point where the model is in the process of integrating structural and sequence signals. Inserting trainable adapters in the layers just before or just after this window preserves versus modifies the integration depending on what you need. If your goal is to inject antibody-specific sequence preferences without overriding the structural reasoning, targeting adapters before layer twenty-eight — before sequence joins the fused subspace — is a principled choice.

For probing E-S-M-three, the structural signals are most accessible in layers twenty-five to thirty-five, after structural modalities have fused and before the stream equilibrates. If you're trying to extract C-D-R contact maps or predict antibody-antigen interface geometry from internal representations, intermediate layers in this band are likely to carry more signal than the final layer.

The functional-annotation orthogonality is worth a specific note. If you're using E-S-M-three on antibody sequences annotated with known binding specificities or isotypes, those annotation tokens are not bleeding into the physical-property representations. The two streams stay separate throughout the model. That means the model can in principle use both channels independently — functional annotation for one class of tasks, structural or sequence representations for another — without one corrupting the other.

The result on structural disorder deserves the most attention for antibody applications. C-D-R loops — especially C-D-R H-three — are intrinsically disordered in the unbound state. If structural disorder delays modality fusion, then for the CDR-loop tokens specifically, the sequence and structure modalities are staying separate deeper into the network than they would for a structured framework region. That is a mechanistic account of why CDR-loop representation is harder: the model has to work through more layers before integrating the signals for the region that matters most for binding. If you're probing or fine-tuning specifically for C-D-R-H-three, use later layers than you would for framework residues.

The paper doesn't make strong claims about downstream task performance — it's a characterization study, not a benchmark. But the atlas it provides is exactly the kind of mechanistic grounding that makes informed fine-tuning and probing choices possible rather than empirical guesswork. That's its contribution.

Second and final item tonight. It's a review: "Progress in structure prediction and design of adaptive immune receptors," published July tenth in Current Opinion in Structural Biology by Tomer Cohen, Tanya Hochner, and Dina Schneidman-Duhovny at the Hebrew University of Jerusalem. The full text is behind a paywall, so this is a pointer based on the abstract and the journal — worth knowing the review exists and reading it if you have access.

The Schneidman-Duhovny group has been active at the intersection of docking, structure prediction, and adaptive immune receptors for over a decade — C-lu-sPro, S-caN-Net, and work on antibody-specific structural scoring. A review from them now is a current-state-of-the-field document from a group that has worked through many of the problems they're surveying.

The scope covers all three active design paradigms: A-I-R-specific language models, including the antibody and T-cell-receptor language models that have proliferated over the last three years; deep-learning-based structure prediction for antibody-antigen and T-C-R-peptide-M-H-C complexes; and epitope-conditioned generative design using inverse folding, diffusion-based backbone generation, sequence-space diffusion, and sequence-structure co-design. They also cover datasets and benchmarks — explicitly noting sources of bias that complicate cross-study comparisons — and outline the challenges the field hasn't solved: accurate modeling of flexible C-D-R loops, reliable ranking of antibody-antigen complexes, and scalable epitope-specific design.

The challenges list is familiar ground if you've been following this show, but having them enumerated in a peer-reviewed review paper from a senior group is the kind of document that anchors grant proposals and research-planning discussions. The benchmark-bias section is likely the most immediately useful part — understanding where evaluation artifacts inflate reported performance is necessary for deciding which published results to trust when picking a starting model or pipeline for your own engineering work. If you have institutional journal access, it's worth an hour.
