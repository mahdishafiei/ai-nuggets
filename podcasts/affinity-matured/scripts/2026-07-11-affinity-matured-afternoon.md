**First: obligate trispecific IL-2 receptor agonistic antibodies from argenx and KU Leuven — engineering VHH geometry for regulatory T-cell selectivity.**

Interleukin-2 is a small cytokine with an outsized therapeutic footprint. Its receptor exists in two forms. The intermediate-affinity dimeric receptor — the beta subunit, C-D-122, plus the common gamma chain, C-D-132 — is broadly expressed on effector T cells, memory C-D-8 T cells, and natural killer cells, and binds I-L-2 at roughly one nanomolar. The high-affinity trimeric receptor adds a third subunit, C-D-25, the alpha chain of the I-L-2 receptor, and binds I-L-2 at roughly ten picomolar. C-D-25 does not itself signal, but it acts as a cell-specific concentrator that selectively boosts I-L-2 sensitivity on cells that express it. Regulatory T cells — the Treg compartment that enforces immune tolerance — constitutively express high levels of C-D-25. That is why low-dose I-L-2 therapy has been an active area of clinical research for autoimmune disease, transplant rejection, and graft-versus-host disease: at low doses, the high-affinity trimeric receptor on Tregs is engaged and the intermediate-affinity dimeric receptor on effector cells is not. But native I-L-2 has poor pharmacokinetics, a narrow therapeutic window, and never gives you a truly Treg-clean expansion. The engineered I-L-2 analogue landscape — muteins with altered receptor affinities, I-L-2-antibody complexes, pegylated I-L-2, and half-life-extended fusions — is the community's answer to that problem, and none of the answers have been fully satisfying.

This paper from Valentina Lykhopiy and colleagues at argenx, Dualyx, and Susan Schlenner's Adaptive Immunology Lab at KU Leuven — published this week in Nature Communications, with the earlier Research Square preprint from mid-2025 already in circulation — takes a fundamentally different route. Instead of engineering I-L-2 itself, they build an obligate trispecific antibody that simultaneously engages all three I-L-2 receptor subunits. The antibody mimics I-L-2's signaling function by physically approximating C-D-122 and C-D-132, which triggers J-A-K-1-J-A-K-3-S-T-A-T-5 signaling, and the third arm — engaging C-D-25 — provides the same cell-specific concentrating effect that C-D-25 does for native I-L-2. Cells that lack C-D-25 don't get the concentrating advantage, and see the trispecific antibody with much lower potency than Tregs do. It is a molecular reformulation of the same selectivity logic that low-dose I-L-2 relies on, but built into an antibody with antibody-like half-life and antibody-like tissue distribution.

The building blocks are single-domain antibodies — V-H-Hs, or camelid nanobodies — raised by D-N-A immunization of llamas with plasmids encoding all three human I-L-2 receptor subunits. From those campaigns, phage display and E-L-I-S-A plus surface plasmon resonance screening yielded 85 unique C-D-25 binders across 15 C-D-R-3 families, 153 unique C-D-122 binders across 38 families, and 92 unique C-D-132 binders across 7 families. Epitope binning identified 3, 4, and 5 distinct epitope bins on the three subunits respectively. This is deliberately a diverse starting library — the point of raising this many binders is to enable subsequent architectural exploration in a regime where individual affinity, individual epitope location, and their combinatorial arrangement can all be varied. The V-H-Hs were reformatted as V-H-H-Fc fusions with the LALA-P-G Fc-silencing mutations to abrogate effector function. The one-arm-plus-two-arm trispecific architecture — anti-C-D-25 V-H-H on one heavy chain, anti-C-D-122 and anti-C-D-132 V-H-Hs tandem-fused on the other, joined through the Fc — is assembled by controlled Fab-arm exchange with the K-409-R plus F-405-L mutation pair.

The first surprise in the results is that binding affinity does not drive signaling. Bispecific anti-C-D-122-C-D-132 antibodies — the two-arm building blocks without the C-D-25 arm — did induce STAT-5 phosphorylation with potency comparable to recombinant I-L-2 on H-E-K cells expressing all three receptor subunits, but the effect was driven by geometry, not affinity. Several strong-binding bispecifics failed to signal, and several weak-binding bispecifics signaled well. The interpretation is what a cytokine biologist would predict: what matters is not how tightly each domain binds, but whether the arrangement of the two arms brings C-D-122 and C-D-132 into the correct spatial approximation to trigger receptor dimerization and the downstream J-A-K-S-T-A-T cascade. The affinity of the individual V-H-H is a distant secondary variable.

The second finding is the C-D-25 role. Adding the C-D-25-targeting arm to the bispecific — creating a trispecific — imposed Treg selectivity in the way you would predict from I-L-2 biology, but with an important nuance. Trispecific constructs incorporating a high-affinity anti-C-D-25 V-H-H that binds within the I-L-2 binding epitope on C-D-25 were the winners on Tregs. Trispecific constructs incorporating a lower-affinity anti-C-D-25 V-H-H, or a high-affinity binder that engaged non-I-L-2 epitopes, were worse. The C-D-25 arm's job is not just to concentrate the antibody on C-D-25-positive cells; it is to concentrate the antibody in a specific spatial and epitope-matched manner that is compatible with productive trimeric-receptor engagement. Their lead candidate, called alpha-beta-gamma V-H-H forty-eight, matches native human I-L-2 in E-C-50 on the H-E-K cell line — the antibody's E-C-50 is fifteen picomolar versus ten picomolar for human I-L-2 — while it selectively activates Tregs over conventional C-D-4 T cells, C-D-8 T cells, natural killer cells, and N-K-T cells in human peripheral blood mononuclear cell cultures.

In vivo, this lead trispecific antibody was tested in a humanized N-S-G mouse expansion model — N-O-D-S-C-I-D-I-L-2-R-gamma-knockout mice engrafted with human P-B-M-Cs, then given two intraperitoneal doses of thirty nanograms of antibody per mouse on days one and eight after engraftment. The result: at day fourteen, both the ratio of Tregs to conventional C-D-4 T cells and the ratio of Tregs to C-D-8 T cells were significantly elevated in the trispecific-treated mice compared to the bispecific control. K-i-67 staining showed proliferation-driven expansion of Tregs specifically. This is a modest engraftment model — not a disease model — but it is a clean readout of what the trispecific does that the bispecific does not.

The final experiment is where the paper's title lands. Starting from the lead trispecific — which is monovalent for C-D-25 with one anti-C-D-25 V-H-H — the authors built five new variants that are all bivalent for C-D-25 with two anti-C-D-25 V-H-H copies, keeping the exact same specificity and valency for C-D-122 and C-D-132, but varying only the spatial arrangement of the V-H-H domains within the antibody scaffold. Different combinations of domain positioning, linker placement, and arm topology were tested. The five variants — labelled D-C-41, D-C-43, D-C-44, D-C-45, and D-C-60 — all showed higher potency on Tregs than the original monovalent lead. D-C-41 and D-C-44 achieved single-digit picomolar to femtomolar E-C-50 on Tregs while producing S-T-A-T-5 phosphorylation on non-Treg populations only at concentrations one hundred to one thousand fold higher. D-C-43 and D-C-60 acted as partial agonists but with the widest Treg-to-non-Treg potency window. This is the geometry-versus-selectivity trade-off in explicit form: at the same underlying binder set, different scaffolds map to different points on the potency-selectivity Pareto front, and the mapping is not predictable from the individual binder properties. The authors are careful to note that they did not identify a single geometric feature that predicts the outcome, which is one of the interesting parts of the paper: even with a highly controlled panel varying only spatial arrangement, the selectivity-potency behaviour requires empirical screening.

Two things stand out for this show's audience. First, this is a live case of the geometry-first design paradigm — the way spatial arrangement of binding domains within an antibody scaffold determines therapeutic function beyond what individual binder affinities predict. Cytokine receptor agonism is one of the clearest instances where this matters, because the receptor is only active in a specific dimerization geometry, but the same principle applies to any target where multivalent engagement or receptor clustering is the goal — T-cell engagers, N-K-cell engagers, chimeric antigen receptor formats, agonistic tumor necrosis factor receptor family antibodies. That is a wide-open opportunity space for computational or structure-guided methods that can predict antibody format from receptor geometry. Second, the paper does not use any of the current-generation machine-learning tools — the design is entirely empirical, driven by phage display, controlled Fab-arm exchange, and a modest panel of hand-designed variants. It is worth flagging exactly because it defines the target for computational methods: if a design tool can predict, from receptor architecture and V-H-H sequence, which of these spatial variants will occupy which point on the potency-selectivity Pareto front, that would collapse this kind of program from a two-year screening effort to a week of in-silico evaluation. It also suggests that antibody-format-prediction benchmarks — of which there are essentially none in the current literature — would be a high-value contribution.

Paper link: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-026-75024-6

**Second: hybrid quantum-classical generative design of M-H-C class I-binding peptides — a photonic quantum processor as a prior for a generative adversarial network.**

I am going off the abstract for this one — the preprint was posted this week to bioRxiv and the body H-T-M-L is still rendering, so the details below are what the authors present in the abstract and what the framing implies. I flag this because the field would benefit from a careful read of the full text once it becomes available.

The setting is de novo peptide design for M-H-C class I binding — designing peptides that, when displayed by a specific human leukocyte antigen, are stable and immunogenic and can serve as personalized vaccine payloads or targets for T-cell receptor engineering. The generative model is a standard generative adversarial network trained on peptide sequences, and the innovation is in what feeds the model. Deep generative models sample from a prior distribution — typically a factorised Gaussian over the latent space. That prior determines which regions of sequence space the generator will explore. Recent work in the machine-learning-plus-quantum literature has suggested that sampling from a physical quantum system can produce a structured non-classical distribution that may serve as a more useful inductive bias for certain tasks. The question the paper asks is whether that helps for the biological design of peptides.

The pipeline is an end-to-end coupling of a G-A-N to latent vectors sampled from a real photonic quantum processor — a hardware-realisable non-classical prior. The authors evaluate this in silico across 131 H-L-A alleles, comparing quantum-prior generation to classical baselines. The reported finding is that quantum-derived priors increased the yield of predicted strong binders, with the largest relative gains for understudied alleles where classical baselines perform worst. The mechanism they claim to observe is broader sequence exploration at non-anchor positions — the residues that do not directly contact the M-H-C anchor pockets and are therefore where a wider search can be productive — while anchor specificity is preserved. Three understudied alleles were then taken forward to in vitro validation using peptide-M-H-C class I stability E-L-I-S-A assays, confirming that quantum-designed peptides act as potent stabilisers of the peptide-M-H-C class I complex.

The reason this is worth flagging even at abstract-only depth is that it addresses a specific pain point in personalized immunotherapy design: the H-L-A alleles for which we have plentiful training data are the same ones that classical predictors do best on, and the underrepresented alleles — often in non-European populations — are exactly where the field's blind spots are. If quantum-derived priors help most on low-data alleles, that is a useful direction to explore, and the wet-lab validation on three of those alleles is what turns this from a theoretical curiosity into a data point worth tracking. The obvious caveats are the ones the community will want to see addressed: an ablation between the quantum prior and any well-tuned structured classical prior, a proper T-cell response readout beyond peptide-M-H-C stability, and clarity on which parts of the pipeline are inherited from prior peptide-design generative networks. The full text will settle those questions. The takeaway for now is that hardware-realisable non-classical priors are being seriously tested as an inductive bias for immunotherapy-relevant peptide design, and the first end-to-end validation with wet-lab confirmation has appeared.

Paper link: https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.64898/2026.07.09.736951
